<Callout type="note" title="What you need from Chapters 4 and 5">
Those chapters are not written yet, so here is the short version of the three tools this chapter uses.

- **An AR(1) series** is a series that remembers its last value: $x_{t+1} = \mu_x + \phi x_t + v_{t+1}$.
  The number $\phi$ is its **persistence**. Near 1, a high value today means a high value for many years.
- **Overlapping observations.** A five-year return that starts in 1950 and one that starts in 1951 share four
  years. They are not two independent facts, so their errors move together.
- **Newey–West standard errors** are standard errors that allow for this kind of shared error. They are usually
  larger than the classical ones, and that is the honest direction.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Are stocks expensive, and does it matter?

At the end of 1931, the dividends of the US stock market were 10.1% of its price. At the end of 1999, they were
1.1%. That ratio, dividends over price, is the **dividend–price ratio**. It is low when prices are high compared
with the cash that companies pay out. At the end of 2025 it was 1.15%, almost back to the 1999 low.

Now look at what came next. Take the ten years after the end of 1949, when the ratio was 6.8%. Over that decade,
money in stocks grew 4.8 times as much as money in Treasury bills. Take the ten years after 1999. Over that
decade, money in stocks ended 29% *behind* money in Treasury bills.

So here is the question you probably have. When prices are high compared with dividends, should you expect lower
returns? If yes, then the reward for holding stocks is not a fixed number. It goes up and down over time. This
chapter tests that idea on 100 years of data, and it is honest about how much those 100 years can show.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> A scatter of today against the future

The test is a picture. Each dot is one year. Across, you see the log dividend–price ratio at the end of that year.
Up, you see the log excess return over the next $k$ years: the stock return minus the Treasury bill return, added
up year by year. If a high ratio comes before high returns, the dots slope upward.

At one year, the pattern is weak. The fitted line rises, but the dots scatter far above and below it. The slope

year, while their average is 6.5%.

At ten years, the picture changes. The slope is 0.39, with a Newey–West $t$-statistic of 1.63, and the $R^2$
is 11.8%. Years with a high ratio were mostly
followed by good decades. Years with a low ratio, like the late 1990s, were mostly followed by poor ones.

Why should the slope grow? Think of the ratio as a slow-moving weather front. A front does not tell you much
about one hour of rain. But it tells you a lot about the total rain of the next week, because it stays in place
for days. In the same way, a high ratio adds a little to the expected return of *each* coming year. It also
moves slowly, so the little extras add up over a long horizon.

<Callout type="key">The dividend–price ratio barely forecasts next year's return. It forecasts the next ten years
much better, because it is slow and its small effect on each year adds up.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> The predictive regression and its horizon

Write $\Signal{dp_t} = d_t - p_t$ for the log dividend–price ratio at the end of year $t$. Write
$\Ret{r^e_{t+1}}$ for the log excess return during year $t+1$. The return over the next $k$ years is the sum of
the yearly log excess returns, because log returns add up over time:

$$
\Ret{r^e_{t \to t+k}} = \Ret{r^e_{t+1}} + \Ret{r^e_{t+2}} + \dots + \Ret{r^e_{t+k}}.
$$

The **predictive regression** puts the future on the left and today on the right:

$$
\Ret{r^e_{t \to t+k}} = a_k + b_k\,\Signal{dp_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+k}}.
$$

We use $a$ and $b$, not $\alpha$ and $\beta$, so that the slope never looks like a pricing error or a factor
exposure. The error $\Err{\varepsilon_{t+k}}$ is the part of the return that $\Signal{dp_t}$ cannot see. Ordinary
least squares (OLS) picks $a_k$ and $b_k$ to make those errors as small as possible.

### Why the slope grows with the horizon

Here is a simple model that explains the picture. It has two equations. The one-year return depends on last
year's ratio, and the ratio is an AR(1):

$$
\Ret{r^e_{t+1}} = a + b_1\,\Signal{dp_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}, \qquad
\Signal{dp_{t+1}} = \mu_x + \phi\,\Signal{dp_t} + v_{t+1}.
$$

The shocks $\Err{\varepsilon}$ and $v$ cannot be forecast with anything known at the time. Take the expectation
of the AR(1) given what you know at $t$. One year ahead, $\mathbb{E}_t[\Signal{dp_{t+1}}] = \mu_x +
\phi\,\Signal{dp_t}$. Two years ahead, apply the same rule to that forecast:

$$
\mathbb{E}_t[\Signal{dp_{t+2}}] = \mu_x + \phi\,\mathbb{E}_t[\Signal{dp_{t+1}}] = \mu_x(1 + \phi) +
\phi^2\,\Signal{dp_t}.
$$

Each step multiplies the effect of today's ratio by $\phi$ once more. So $j$ years ahead the coefficient on
$\Signal{dp_t}$ is $\phi^j$. Now forecast the return in year $t+j$. It depends on the ratio one year earlier:

$$
\mathbb{E}_t[\Ret{r^e_{t+j}}] = a + b_1\,\mathbb{E}_t[\Signal{dp_{t+j-1}}] = \text{constant} +
b_1\,\phi^{\,j-1}\,\Signal{dp_t}.
$$

Add these forecasts for $j = 1, \dots, k$. The coefficient on $\Signal{dp_t}$ is a sum of powers of $\phi$:

$$
b_1(1 + \phi + \phi^2 + \dots + \phi^{k-1}) = b_1\,\frac{1 - \phi^k}{1 - \phi}.
$$

The last step is the sum of a geometric series. The forecast error over $k$ years is uncorrelated with
$\Signal{dp_t}$, so the regression slope is this coefficient:

$$
b_k = b_1\,\frac{1 - \phi^k}{1 - \phi}.
$$

Two cases show what it means. If $\phi = 0$, the ratio forgets itself after one year, and $b_k = b_1$ at every
horizon. If $\phi$ is close to 1, then $b_k$ is close to $k\,b_1$: the slope grows almost in proportion to the
horizon. On our data the estimated persistence is $\phi = 0.92$. Then $\phi^{10} = 0.45$, and the ten-year slope
should be about 7.2 times the one-year slope. With $b_1 = 0.042$, that gives 0.30. The direct estimate is 0.39.
The two numbers are close but not equal, because the model is a simplification and 100 years are few.

### Why $R^2$ grows too

$R^2$ is the share of the return variance that the fitted line explains:

$$
R^2_k = \frac{b_k^2\,\operatorname{Var}(\Signal{dp_t})}{\operatorname{Var}(\Ret{r^e_{t \to t+k}})}.
$$

When $\phi$ is close to 1, the top grows like $k^2$, because $b_k$ grows like $k$. If yearly returns are close to
independent, the bottom grows only like $k$. So $R^2$ rises roughly in step with the horizon at first. It levels
off once $\phi^k$ gets small and $b_k$ stops growing.

### What the $t$-statistic can and cannot tell you

There is a catch. With 100 years of data and a ten-year horizon, the regression has 90 observations. But
neighbouring observations share nine of their ten years of returns. Return data from 1927 to 2025 contain only 9
separate, non-overlapping decades. That is the real amount of information about ten-year returns.

The classical OLS standard error treats the 90 observations as 90 independent facts. It is far too small. The
Newey–West and Hansen–Hodrick estimators allow for errors that are shared across $k$ neighbouring observations.
At ten years, the classical $t$-statistic is 3.43, the Newey–West one is 1.63 and the Hansen–Hodrick one is 1.44.
Only the first one looks impressive, and it is the wrong one.

Even the corrected $t$-statistics are only approximations when there are so few independent decades. Later
chapters return to this, and to other small-sample problems of these regressions.

<Callout type="warning">A large $t$-statistic at a long horizon is not, by itself, strong evidence. Overlapping
returns make the effective sample small. Always check which standard errors the number uses.</Callout>

The simulation shows why the warning above matters. In seeds 1 and 5, the model and its parameters are the same.
Only the luck of the draw changes. With 100 years, a ten-year slope can come out twice the truth or the wrong
sign, and both can look "significant". The real data are one draw like these.

The evidence also depends on the sample. In the scatter widget above, the full sample gives a one-year
$t$-statistic of 0.94. Cochrane (2011) studies returns from 1947 to 2009 (Table I, p. 1). On our data, the
one-year regression with returns over those years gives a slope of 0.12, a Newey–West $t$-statistic of 2.46 and
an $R^2$ of 10.1%. The same model looks much stronger in the shorter, post-war sample.

### How to read the evidence

So what does the evidence say? It says that the **expected return** on stocks, the average return you should
plan for, is not a constant. A low dividend–price ratio means investors pay a high price for each dollar of
dividends. If those dividends do not grow faster later, the high price must come with lower returns. Cochrane
(2011, p. 2) reads the evidence this way. High prices compared with dividends have been followed by years of
poor returns, and low prices by years of good ones.

How big is the change? The standard deviation of $\Signal{dp_t}$ is 0.50. With a one-year slope of 0.042, a
one-standard-deviation move in the ratio moves the expected one-year return by about 2.1 percentage points. The
average excess return is 6.5% a year, so that change is large. A forecast with a small $R^2$ can still matter,
because one-year returns contain so much noise that no forecast could explain most of it.

The second limit is just as important. Everything here is **in-sample**. The slope uses all 100 years, including
years that an investor in 1950 could not have seen. A good in-sample fit is not a trading result. Welch and Goyal
(2008, p. 1455) find that the dividend–price ratio and many other predictors forecast poorly out of sample, and
would not have helped an investor to time the market. Later chapters test forecasts the way an investor would
have used them.

<Callout type="key">Read the dividend–price ratio as a measure of expected returns that change over time. Do not
read it as a trading signal. The in-sample fit and the overlapping horizons both make the evidence look stronger
than it is.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- A predictive regression puts the return over the next $k$ years on the left and today's dividend–price ratio on
  the right.
- On goyal-welch-annual data from 1926 to 2025, the slope grows from 0.042 at one year to 0.39 at ten years, and
  $R^2$ grows from 1.2% to 11.8%.
- With a persistent AR(1) predictor, $b_k = b_1(1 - \phi^k)/(1 - \phi)$: a small, slow effect adds up over the
  horizon.
- Overlapping returns make the effective sample small. Use Newey–West or Hansen–Hodrick errors, and still read
  long-horizon $t$-statistics with care. In-sample fit is not a trading result.
- The best reading of the evidence: expected returns change over time.
