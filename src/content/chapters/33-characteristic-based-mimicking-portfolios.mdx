## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> How do you buy a number?

Book-to-market is a number in a firm's accounts. Consumption growth is a number in the national accounts. Neither
is a price, and you cannot buy either one. Yet asset pricing keeps asking what each one "earns". So how do you hold
a portfolio that pays off when a characteristic, or the economy, does?

[Chapter 19](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/19-size-and-value/) gave one answer for book-to-market: sort the stocks
and hold value minus growth. [Chapter 17](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/17-cross-sectional-regressions-and-fama-macbeth/)
gave a second answer without saying so. Each month, it regressed the 25 size and book-to-market portfolios on their
betas, and each month's slope was the return of a zero-cost portfolio. Run that regression on the portfolios' B/M
scores instead of their betas. Over the 758 months from 1963-07 to 2026-08, the monthly slope has a correlation of
0.967 with HML. So a regression slope is a trade, and nearly the same trade as the sort.

Consumption growth is harder. It is not a characteristic of any asset, and it is the factor of the consumption CAPM
that [Chapter 26](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/26-gmm-for-asset-pricing-models/) tested on quarterly data. This
chapter builds a traded stand-in for it, then asks how much of the fit is real.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Two ways to weight the same assets

The grid below holds the 25 portfolios of Chapter 19, with small stocks in the top row and value stocks in the right
column. Give each portfolio two **scores**: a B/M score, its B/M quintile minus 3, and a size score, 3 minus its size
quintile. Each score runs from −2 to +2. Small stocks score +2 on size, and value stocks score +2 on B/M.

Now each cell shows a **weight**: the share of capital put in that portfolio, in percent. Positive cells are long
and negative cells are short.

Both portfolios are long value and short growth. Both hold equal weights in every size row, so neither bets on size.
The readout shows the two numbers that make them comparable. Each has an exposure of 1.00 to the B/M score and 0.00
to the size score. An **exposure** to a score is the weighted average score, $\sum_i w_i \times$ (score of $i$). An
exposure of 1 means: move one quintile step in B/M, and nothing else.

The two portfolios differ in how they spread that exposure. The sort puts all of it into the two extreme columns.
The regression lets the middle columns help, with weights that rise in a straight line with the score. Over the
sample, the sort earned 1.23% a year with $t = 3.17$, and the regression 1.11% with $t = 2.94$. Their correlations with
HML are 0.963 and 0.967. Switch to "Size", and both portfolios earn 0.49% a year, with $t$-statistics of 1.05 and 1.07.

<Callout type="key">A sort and a cross-sectional regression are two sets of weights on the same assets. Each one
has unit exposure to its own characteristic and none to the other. They differ only in how they spread that
exposure across the assets.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Slopes, weights and projections

### Every slope is a portfolio return

Let $Z_t$ be the $N \times \dim z$ matrix of characteristics known at $t$. Row $i$ is $z_{it}'$, with a constant 1
first. Each period, run the cross-sectional regression of the excess returns $\Ret{R^e_{t+1}}$ on $Z_t$. Its OLS
slopes are

$$
\hat\xi_{t+1} = (Z_t'Z_t)^{-1}Z_t'\,\Ret{R^e_{t+1}}.
$$

The curriculum writes this slope as $\hat\gamma_t$. Here $\gamma$ is always risk aversion, so the slope is
$\hat\xi_{t+1}$, dated $t+1$ like the return it is made of.

The matrix $(Z_t'Z_t)^{-1}Z_t'$ has $\dim z$ rows and $N$ columns. Entry $j$ of $\hat\xi_{t+1}$ is row $j$ times
the $N$ returns. That is a sum of returns with fixed weights, so it is the return of a portfolio whose weights are
row $j$. The weights are known at $t$, because $Z_t$ is.

What does that portfolio hold? Multiply the weights by the characteristics:

$$
(Z_t'Z_t)^{-1}Z_t'\,Z_t = I.
$$

Row $j$ of the left side lists the exposures of portfolio $j$ to each characteristic: entry $l$ is the sum over
assets of the weight times characteristic $l$. The identity says this is 1 for $l = j$ and 0 for every other $l$. One characteristic is the constant, whose "exposure" is the sum of the weights. So every slope portfolio
except the constant's has weights that sum to zero: it is long–short and costs nothing. The constant's portfolio
sums to one.

The time-series mean of $\hat\xi_{t+1}$ is the Fama–MacBeth estimate of
[Chapter 17](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/17-cross-sectional-regressions-and-fama-macbeth/), and its
$t$-statistic is the mean over its standard error. So the Fama–MacBeth test on a characteristic asks one plain
question: does this portfolio earn a mean return different from zero? Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) read the slopes the
same way. They call the portfolios characteristic-managed, and they note that their own estimator nests the
period-by-period Fama–MacBeth slopes as its least restricted case (NBER WP, p. 15).

### The 25 portfolios, worked out

For the grid, $z_i = (1, z^{\text{size}}_i, z^{\text{BM}}_i)'$, with the two scores from the Intuition. A portfolio
keeps its quintiles, so the scores never change and we drop the date. Now compute $Z'Z$ entry by entry.

1. The constant: $\sum_i 1 \cdot 1 = 25$.
2. Each score takes the values −2, −1, 0, 1 and 2 five times each. So
   $\sum_i (z^{\text{BM}}_i)^2 = 5(4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4) = 50$, and the same for size.
3. Each score sums to zero, so its cross-product with the constant is zero.
4. Every B/M score meets every size score exactly once. So the sum of $z^{\text{size}}_i z^{\text{BM}}_i$ over the 25
   cells is the sum of the five size scores times the sum of the five B/M scores. Each of those sums is zero.

So $Z'Z$ is diagonal, $\operatorname{diag}(25, 50, 50)$, and its inverse is $\operatorname{diag}(1/25, 1/50, 1/50)$.
The B/M row of $(Z'Z)^{-1}Z'$ is then

$$
w^{\text{reg}}_i = \frac{z^{\text{BM}}_i}{50},
$$

which is −0.04, −0.02, 0, 0.02 and 0.04 from growth to value: the grid's "Regression" view. The sort holds 1/5 in
each value portfolio and −1/5 in each growth portfolio, divided by the spread of 4 between the scores so its
exposure is 1:

$$
w^{\text{sort}}_i = \begin{cases} \phantom{-}1/20 & \text{if } z^{\text{BM}}_i = 2, \\ -1/20 & \text{if } z^{\text{BM}}_i = -2, \\ \phantom{-}0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Check the exposure: $5 \cdot \tfrac{1}{20} \cdot 2 + 5 \cdot (-\tfrac{1}{20}) \cdot (-2) = 1$. Each B/M column holds
every size score once, and the size scores sum to zero, so the size exposure is 0.

### Why the regression spreads its weights

Both weight vectors meet the same three conditions, $Z'w = (0, 0, 1)'$: a zero sum, no size exposure, unit B/M
exposure. Among all such $w$, the regression's has the smallest sum of squares. Here is why, in four steps.

1. Write $w^{\text{reg}} = Z(Z'Z)^{-1}(0, 0, 1)'$. This is the B/M row of $(Z'Z)^{-1}Z'$, turned into a column.
2. Take any $w$ with $Z'w = (0, 0, 1)'$. The gap $w - w^{\text{reg}}$ then has $Z'(w - w^{\text{reg}}) = 0$.
3. Expand: $w'w$ equals $w^{\text{reg}\prime}w^{\text{reg}}$, plus $(w - w^{\text{reg}})'(w - w^{\text{reg}})$, plus
   $2\,w^{\text{reg}\prime}(w - w^{\text{reg}})$.
4. The last term is $2\,(0, 0, 1)(Z'Z)^{-1}Z'(w - w^{\text{reg}}) = 0$ by step 2. The middle term is never negative.
   So $w'w \ge w^{\text{reg}\prime}w^{\text{reg}}$.

On the grid, $w'w$ is 0.020 for the regression and 0.025 for the sort. If the 25 returns had equal variances and
independent noise, a smaller $w'w$ would mean a less volatile portfolio for the same exposure. That is the
Gauss–Markov reason that OLS is efficient. The 25 portfolios are not independent: they move together every month,
as Chapter 17 showed. So $w'w$ is not the variance here, and the $t$-statistics of 3.17 for the sort and 2.94 for the regression show no
gain from the smaller $w'w$.

### A factor that is not a return

Consumption growth $\Delta c_t$ has no portfolio behind it. To get one, project it on the excess returns of $N$ base
assets by OLS:

$$
\Factor{f_t} = a + w'\Ret{R^e_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_t}.
$$

Here $\Factor{f_t} = \Delta c_t$, quarterly. The **mimicking portfolio** holds the weights $w$, and its return is
$w'\Ret{R^e_t}$. The curriculum writes the intercept as $c$ and the residual as $e_t$. Here $c_t$ is log
consumption, the very series being projected, and $e$ is the residual of Chapter 19's 2×3 sort, so we write $a$ and
$\varepsilon_t$. The weights are $w = \operatorname{Var}(R^e_t)^{-1}\operatorname{Cov}(R^e_t, f_t)$. On excess
returns, they need not sum to one.

Of all portfolios of the base assets, $w'R^e_t$ has the highest correlation with $f_t$. Four steps show it.

1. Pick any other portfolio of the base assets and regress $f_t$ on a constant and its return alone. The $R^2$ of
   that regression is the squared correlation between $f_t$ and the portfolio.
2. That regression is the full regression on all $N$ returns, with the weights forced to be proportional to the
   chosen portfolio's.
3. OLS on all $N$ returns picks the weights with the smallest sum of squared residuals. A forced choice cannot do
   better, so its $R^2$ is no higher.
4. The full regression's $R^2$ is the squared correlation between $f_t$ and its fitted value, $a + w'R^e_t$. The
   constant $a$ does not change a correlation.

Giglio & Xiu (2021, p. 1954) describe this maximally correlated portfolio and the use made of it. A non-traded
factor's risk premium is estimated as the mean excess return of its mimicking portfolio.

### How much of the fit is real?

With 25 portfolios, the projection fits 26 numbers. Even if consumption growth had nothing to do with returns, OLS
would find weights that track some of its noise. Under that null, with normal noise, the expected $R^2$ of a regression on 25 returns
over $T$ quarters is $25/(T - 1)$. Over all 252 quarters, that is a correlation of about $\sqrt{25/251} = 0.32$.
The fit we see, 0.40, is not far above it.

The split sample makes the same point without a formula. On the first 126 quarters, noise alone would give about
$\sqrt{25/125} = 0.45$, and the projection reaches 0.55. Apply those weights to the next 126 quarters, and the
correlation is −0.15. Move the split back to 1975-Q1, and the weights fit 46 quarters with a correlation of 0.79, then
score −0.05 afterwards. The fewer quarters per weight, the better the fit looks, and the less of it survives.

The three FF3 factors cannot overfit much, with only four numbers to fit. But they track consumption growth poorly
in any sample: 0.19 on the first 126 quarters and −0.01 on the rest.

Two quarters stand out in the plot. Consumption fell 10.1% in 2020-Q2 and rose 7.4% in 2020-Q3, so the widget cuts
its lines at ±4%. Drop the four quarters of 2020, and the 25-portfolio numbers barely move: 0.39 in sample, and
−0.07 after 1995-Q1 with the weights of the first half. The FF3 numbers do move, to 0.16 and 0.13. With a few
hundred quarters, one extreme year can change a correlation by this much.

So the base assets pull two ways. Giglio & Xiu (2021, p. 1955) show that the premium of a mimicking portfolio is
biased unless its base assets are well diversified and span all the true risk factors. Their example is a study that
built a consumption mimicking portfolio from four size–B/M portfolios, which may omit risks tied to consumption. Few
base assets risk that omission. Many base assets over a few hundred quarters risk fitting noise.

### What the 25 portfolios hide

Our characteristics are quintile scores of 25 portfolios. They never change, and they are ranks, not the portfolios'
actual size or B/M. Fama & French (1992, p. 430) ran their regressions on individual stocks instead. Size and B/M are
measured precisely for each stock, and grouping the stocks into portfolios would blur that information. Their
monthly regressions used 2,267 stocks on average (p. 439). With firms, $Z_t$ changes every month, so the slope portfolios
rebalance every month. Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) build on exactly those firm-level managed portfolios. That is the
subject of Chapter 32, *Characteristics are covariances: IPCA*. Here, the 25 portfolios stand in for firms, and every
number above holds only for them.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- Each slope of a cross-sectional regression on characteristics is the return of a portfolio. Its weights are a row
  of $(Z_t'Z_t)^{-1}Z_t'$, with exposure 1 to its own characteristic and 0 to the others.
- A sort and a regression are two weightings of the same assets. On B/M, the two earned 1.23% and 1.11% a year and
  have correlations of 0.963 and 0.967 with HML.
- Among all weights with the same exposures, the regression's have the smallest sum of squares.
- A non-traded factor gets a traded stand-in by projection: $w'R^e_t$ is its most correlated portfolio.
- With 25 base assets and a few hundred quarters, the projection overfits: 0.55 in sample, −0.15 after the split.
