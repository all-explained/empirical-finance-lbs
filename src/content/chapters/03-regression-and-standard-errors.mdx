<Callout type="note" title="What you need from Chapter 2">
Chapter 2 is not written yet, so here is the short version of the tools this chapter uses.

- The **sample mean** $\bar R = \tfrac1T \sum_t R_t$ is the average over $T$ months. The **variance** is the average
  squared distance from the mean, and the **standard deviation** $\sigma$ is its square root.
- An estimate from data is never exact. Its **standard error** is the standard deviation of the estimate across
  the samples you could have drawn. For a mean of $T$ independent months it is $\sigma/\sqrt{T}$.
- The estimate plus and minus two standard errors gives a range that holds the true value in about 95% of samples.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> How much does a stock move with the market?

Suppose you own big value stocks: large companies whose shares are cheap compared with their book value. The
market falls sharply next month. How much should you expect your stocks to fall? And how sure can you be of that
answer?

The usual tool is a regression of your stocks' excess return on the market's excess return. (An **excess
return** is a return minus the Treasury bill rate, as in Chapter 1.) The slope of that regression is the
stocks' **beta**. On the last five years of monthly data, from September 2021 to August 2026, big value stocks have
a beta of 1.14. On average, they moved 1.14 times as much as the market.

How precise is 1.14? Here the surprise starts. A statistics package gives a **standard error**, the typical size
of the estimate's error. The classical formula gives 0.140. A second formula, named after White, gives 0.175. On
all 1,202 months since July 1926, the gap is larger: the classical standard error is 0.027 and White's is 0.069,
2.6 times as large. Two respectable formulas, one data set, very different answers. This chapter shows where the
line comes from, what each formula assumes, and which one to believe.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> A line through a cloud

Each dot below is one month. Across, you see the market's excess return in that month. Up, you see the excess
return of big value stocks in the same month. The dots slope upward: when the market does well, these stocks
usually do well too.

The thick line is the **ordinary least squares** (OLS) line. Each thin vertical line is a **residual**: the gap
between a dot and the line. OLS chooses the one line that makes the sum of the squared residuals as small as
possible. Its slope is the beta.

<RegressionLab client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> find the dot farthest to the right and drag it up and down. The
line swings a lot. Now drag a dot near the middle of the cloud. The line moves up and down a little, but its slope

The experiment shows the key idea of this chapter. Months with a large market move, far to the left or right, hold
the line in place. They act like the ends of a plank resting on a pivot in the middle of the cloud. Push the end
of a plank and it tilts. Push near the pivot and it barely moves.

So the precision of the slope depends on how noisy those far-out months are. The dashed lines show the slope plus
and minus two standard errors. The classical formula assumes that every month has the same amount of noise. If
the months with big market moves are also the months with big residuals, the ends of the plank shake more than
the classical formula thinks. The White formula measures the noise month by month. On this sample its dashed lines
open wider: the slope is less precise than the classical number says.

<Callout type="key">The slope of a regression is pinned down by the observations far from the average. If those
observations are also the noisiest, the classical standard error is too small, and the White standard error
corrects it.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> The OLS estimator and its standard errors

### The line with one regressor

Write $\Ret{R^e_{i,t}}$ for the excess return of portfolio $i$ in month $t$ and $\Ret{R^e_t}$ for the market's. The
regression is

$$
\Ret{R^e_{i,t}} = a_i + \beta_i\,\Ret{R^e_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_{i,t}}, \qquad t = 1, \dots, T.
$$

We call the intercept $a_i$. Chapter 7 gives it a meaning and the name alpha. To keep the algebra short, write
$y_t$ for the left-hand return and $x_t$ for the market's. OLS picks $a$ and $b$ to minimise the sum of squared
residuals,

$$
S(a, b) = \sum_{t=1}^T (y_t - a - b\,x_t)^2.
$$

At the minimum, both derivatives are zero. The derivative with respect to $a$ gives

$$
-2 \sum_t (y_t - a - b\,x_t) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \bar y = a + b\,\bar x \quad\Rightarrow\quad a = \bar y - b\,\bar x.
$$

So the OLS line always passes through the point of means $(\bar x, \bar y)$. That is the pivot of the plank. The
derivative with respect to $b$ gives $\sum_t x_t (y_t - a - b\,x_t) = 0$. Replace $a$ by $\bar y - b\,\bar x$:

$$
\sum_t x_t\big[(y_t - \bar y) - b\,(x_t - \bar x)\big] = 0.
$$

Both $\sum_t (y_t - \bar y)$ and $\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)$ are zero, so we may replace the first $x_t$ by $x_t - \bar
x$ without changing the sum. Solve for $b$:

$$
\hat\beta = \frac{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)(y_t - \bar y)}{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2}.
$$

Divide the top and the bottom by $T$. The top becomes the sample covariance of $x$ and $y$, the average product of
their distances from their means. The bottom becomes the sample variance of $x$. So the beta is
$\operatorname{Cov}(y, x)/\operatorname{Var}(x)$: how much $y$ moves with $x$, per unit of $x$'s own movement.

### The same estimator with matrices

With $K$ right-hand variables, stack them in a row vector $x_t'$ whose first entry is 1 for the intercept. In this
chapter $x_t$ is that vector of regressors; in Part II the same letter names a predictor, which is one kind of
regressor. Stack the $T$ rows into a $T \times K$ matrix $X$, and the $y_t$ into a vector $y$. The model is
$y = X\beta + \Err{\varepsilon}$. The sum of squared residuals is $(y - X\beta)'(y - X\beta)$. Its derivative with
respect to $\beta$ is $-2X'(y - X\beta)$. Set it to zero:

$$
X'X\,\hat\beta = X'y \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y.
$$

The inverse exists when no regressor is an exact combination of the others. With $K = 2$, writing out $X'X$ and
$X'y$ gives back the two formulas above.

### How precise is the slope?

Put the true model into the estimator. In the one-regressor case, $y_t - \bar y = \beta(x_t - \bar x) +
(\Err{\varepsilon_t} - \Err{\bar\varepsilon})$. Substitute this into $\hat\beta$. The term in $\Err{\bar\varepsilon}$
drops out, because $\sum_t (x_t - \bar x) = 0$:

$$
\hat\beta = \beta + \frac{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)\,\Err{\varepsilon_t}}{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2}.
$$

The estimate is the truth plus a weighted sum of the errors. The weights grow with the distance $x_t - \bar x$:
that is the plank. Assume the errors have mean zero given the $x$'s and are uncorrelated from month to month
(Chapter 4 drops that second assumption). Let month $t$ have its own error variance $\sigma_t^2$. The variance of a
sum of uncorrelated terms is the sum of their variances, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta) = \frac{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2\,\sigma_t^2}{\Big(\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2\Big)^2}.
$$

With matrices, the same steps read $\hat\beta = \beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'\Err{\varepsilon}$, and

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta) = (X'X)^{-1}\Big(\sum_t \sigma_t^2\,x_t x_t'\Big)(X'X)^{-1}.
$$

This shape is called a **sandwich**: the same $(X'X)^{-1}$ on the outside, the noise in the middle.

### Classical standard errors

The classical formula assumes that every month has the same error variance, $\sigma_t^2 = \sigma^2$. This is called
**homoskedasticity**. Then the middle of the sandwich is $\sigma^2 X'X$. It cancels one of the outside pieces:

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta) = \hat\sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}, \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{T - K}\sum_t
\Err{\hat\varepsilon_t}^2.
$$

Here $\Err{\hat\varepsilon_t} = y_t - x_t'\hat\beta$ is the residual. We divide by $T - K$ rather than $T$
because the $K$ estimated coefficients have already been fitted to the data, which makes the residuals a little
smaller than the true errors. The standard error $\mathrm{se}(\hat\beta_k)$ is the square root of the $k$-th
diagonal entry.

### White standard errors

When the error variance changes from month to month, the errors are **heteroskedastic**. We cannot estimate 1,202
separate variances from 1,202 residuals. White's idea is that we do not need to. We only need the weighted sum in
the middle of the sandwich, and each squared residual stands in for its own month's variance:

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\text{White}}(\hat\beta) = (X'X)^{-1}\Big(\sum_t \Err{\hat\varepsilon_t}^2\,x_t
x_t'\Big)(X'X)^{-1}.
$$

One squared residual is a poor estimate of one month's variance. But the sum averages many of them, and in large
samples it is close to the true middle of the sandwich. If the errors are homoskedastic after all, the White and
classical standard errors are close in large samples. If they are not, only White's is right.

### $t$-statistics and $R^2$

The **$t$-statistic** for the hypothesis that the true coefficient is $\beta_0$ is

$$
t = \frac{\hat\beta - \beta_0}{\mathrm{se}(\hat\beta)}.
$$

The usual case is $\beta_0 = 0$, which gives $t = \hat\beta/\mathrm{se}(\hat\beta)$. In large samples, $t$ is close
to a standard normal when the hypothesis is true. So you reject it at the significance level $q = 5\%$ when $|t|$
is above 1.96, about 2.

The **$R^2$** is the share of the variance of $y$ that the line explains:

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_t \Err{\hat\varepsilon_t}^2}{\sum_t (y_t - \bar y)^2}.
$$

### What the big value numbers say

On the last five years, the intercept is 1.33% a month with a classical $t$-statistic of 2.05. Five years is a
short sample for an average return, so Chapter 7 will treat such a number with care. The slope is 1.14. Its
classical $t$-statistic is 8.18 and its White one is 6.53, so the stocks clearly move with the market. Is the beta
different from 1? The classical $t$-statistic for $\beta_0 = 1$ is 1.02, and the classical band of two standard
errors runs from 0.86 to 1.42. Five years cannot tell this beta apart from the market's. The $R^2$ is 53.6%: the
market explains about half of the monthly variance, and the rest is specific to these stocks.

On all 1,202 months, the slope is 1.30 and the $R^2$ is 66.7%. The classical $t$-statistic for $\beta_0 = 1$ is
11.5. The White one is 4.4. Both reject a beta of 1, but the classical number overstates the evidence by a
factor of 2.6. The reason is the plank. Between 1931 and 1933, the market moved by more than 25% in four separate
months. Nine of the ten largest residuals of the whole sample also fall in the 1930s. The classical formula
treats those wild months as ordinary ones.

<Callout type="warning">A large $R^2$ does not make a slope precise, and a large $t$-statistic is only as good as
the standard error behind it. Always say which standard errors you use.</Callout>

### What White standard errors cannot fix

White standard errors fix one problem: noise that changes size across observations. They assume that errors are
uncorrelated over time, which fails with overlapping returns (Chapter 4). They are a large-sample approximation,
and with few observations they tend to be a little too small. They say nothing about whether the line is the right
model, or whether the beta is stable. The five-year beta of 1.14 and the long-sample beta of 1.30 differ, but we
have not tested whether that gap is more than chance. And a regression shows that two returns move together, not
that one causes the other.

In the fanning world, the classical formula is confident for the wrong reason. It spreads the noise evenly over
all months, while the noise really sits in the months that pin the slope. When the noise is even, the two formulas
agree up to chance. That is why using White standard errors costs little and can save a lot.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- OLS picks the line that minimises the sum of squared residuals: $\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$. With one regressor,
  the slope is $\operatorname{Cov}(y, x)/\operatorname{Var}(x)$, and the line passes through the means.
- The slope is pinned down by the observations far from the average, so its precision depends on how noisy they
  are.
- Classical standard errors assume the same noise in every observation. White standard errors use each squared
  residual and stay right when the noise changes size.
- For big value stocks on all 1,202 months, the White standard error of beta is 2.6 times the classical one.
  Report which standard errors a $t$-statistic uses.
- White fixes heteroskedasticity only. It does not fix errors that are correlated over time.

**Next up:** Chapter 4 looks at errors that are correlated from one month to the next, as with overlapping
long-horizon returns, and the Newey–West standard errors that allow for them.
