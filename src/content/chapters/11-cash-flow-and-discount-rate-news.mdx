<Callout type="note" title="What you need from Chapter 10">
[Chapter 10](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/10-campbell-shiller-decomposition/) derives the first two of the
three tools this chapter uses. Here is the short version.

- **The return identity.** Write $pd_t = p_t - d_t$ for the log price–dividend ratio and $\Delta d_{t+1}$ for log
  dividend growth. Then the log return is, to a very good approximation,
  $\Ret{r_{t+1}} \approx \kappa + \rho\,pd_{t+1} + \Delta d_{t+1} - pd_t$. The constant $\rho$ is just below 1. It
  is $\rho = e^{\overline{pd}}/(1 + e^{\overline{pd}})$, where $\overline{pd}$ is the average of $pd_t$. This is
  accounting, not a model of investors.
- **Solved forward,** the identity says
  $pd_t = \tfrac{\kappa}{1-\rho} + \sum_{j \ge 1} \rho^{j-1}(\Delta d_{t+j} - \Ret{r_{t+j}})$. A high price compared
  with dividends must come before high dividend growth or low returns. (A third way out, a price that grows
  without limit, is ruled out here.)
- **A VAR(1)** from [Chapter 5](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/05-persistence-ar1-and-var/) stacks several series
  in a vector $z_t$: $z_{t+1} = c + A z_t + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}$. Its forecasts are
  $\mathbb{E}_t[z_{t+k}] - \mu = A^k (z_t - \mu)$, where $\mu$ is the long-run mean.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> What did the market learn in 2008?

In 2008, stocks in the S&P index lost 36.6%, dividends included. Investors clearly learned something bad
that year. But what, exactly?

Two different kinds of bad news can push a price down. The first is news that companies will pay smaller dividends
in the future. The second is news that investors now want a higher return before they will hold stocks. Both make
today's price fall. So here is the question you probably have: when the stock market moves, which kind of news is
it reacting to?

The answer matters. If prices move mostly on dividend news, the market is a forecast of the economy. If they move
mostly on return news, the swings of the market are mostly about how much reward investors ask for. This chapter
splits each year's surprise into the two kinds of news. Then it uses the same accounting to build a sharper test
of a question from [Chapter 9](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/09-time-varying-expected-returns/): are returns
really predictable?

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Two reasons a price can change

Think of a flat that you rent out. Its price can fall for two reasons. Tenants may pay less rent in the coming
years. Or buyers may now want a higher yield on their money, so they pay less for the same rent. The first is
**cash-flow news**: news about what the asset will pay. The second is **discount-rate news**: news about the return
that buyers expect to earn.

The second kind is less obvious, so look at it closely. Suppose buyers suddenly expect a higher return from now on.
The rent does not change. The only way the same rent can give a higher return is a lower price to start from. So
higher expected returns in the future are bad news today. Discount-rate news enters a return with a minus sign.

The next widget does this split for every year since 1927. Each bar is one year's **unexpected return**: the part
of the return that a forecast at the start of the year did not expect. The solid part is cash-flow news. The light
part is minus discount-rate news, so that the two parts add up to the surprise. The dot marks the total.

<Callout type="key">An unexpected return is news about future dividends minus news about future returns. Higher
expected returns from now on mean a lower price today.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> From the identity to news, and a test

### Step 1: the news identity

Start from the return identity of
[Chapter 10](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/10-campbell-shiller-decomposition/), written with the price–dividend ratio $pd_t$:

$$
\Ret{r_{t+1}} = \kappa + \Delta d_{t+1} + \rho\,pd_{t+1} - pd_t.
$$

Now replace $pd_{t+1}$ with its forward solution. Multiply that solution by $\rho$ and shift the index, so that
$\rho\,pd_{t+1} = \tfrac{\rho\kappa}{1-\rho} + \sum_{j \ge 1} \rho^{j}(\Delta d_{t+1+j} - \Ret{r_{t+1+j}})$. Put it
into the identity and collect the dividend terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Ret{r_{t+1}} = \frac{\kappa}{1-\rho} &+ \sum_{j \ge 0} \rho^{j}\,\Delta d_{t+1+j} \\
&- \sum_{j \ge 1} \rho^{j}\,\Ret{r_{t+1+j}} - pd_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

The constant comes from $\kappa + \tfrac{\rho\kappa}{1-\rho} = \tfrac{\kappa}{1-\rho}$. This equation holds in every
year. So it also holds for forecasts. Take the forecast made at the end of year $t+1$, $\mathbb{E}_{t+1}$, and
subtract the forecast made one year earlier, $\mathbb{E}_t$. That difference is the **news** that arrived during
year $t+1$. Three things happen:

- The constant and $pd_t$ are known at $t$. Both forecasts agree on them, so they drop out.
- $\Ret{r_{t+1}}$ is known at $t+1$, so $\mathbb{E}_{t+1}\Ret{r_{t+1}} = \Ret{r_{t+1}}$. The left side becomes the
  surprise $\Ret{r_{t+1}} - \mathbb{E}_t\Ret{r_{t+1}}$.
- On the right, each sum becomes the change in its forecast.

The result is the **news identity**:

$$
\Ret{r_{t+1}} - \mathbb{E}_t\Ret{r_{t+1}} = N_{CF,t+1} - N_{DR,t+1},
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
N_{CF,t+1} &= (\mathbb{E}_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t)\sum_{j \ge 0} \rho^j\,\Delta d_{t+1+j}, \\
N_{DR,t+1} &= (\mathbb{E}_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t)\sum_{j \ge 1} \rho^j\,\Ret{r_{t+1+j}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$N_{CF}$ is cash-flow news and $N_{DR}$ is discount-rate news. (With a subscript, $N$ is news. Alone, $N$ is still
the number of assets.) Campbell and Shiller (1988, p. 4) built the linear identity behind this split. It says the
log dividend–price ratio is a constant plus the discounted sum of expected future returns minus dividend growth.

### Step 2: measuring the news with a VAR

The news identity needs forecasts of all future returns. A VAR(1) gives them. Stack the log return and the log
dividend–price ratio in $z_t = (\Ret{r_t}, \Signal{dp_t})'$ and fit $z_{t+1} = c + A z_t + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}$.
The long-run mean is $\mu = (I - A)^{-1}c$, so $z_{t+1} - \mu = A(z_t - \mu) + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}$.

How does the forecast of $z_{t+1+j}$ change between $t$ and $t+1$? At $t+1$ it is $\mu + A^{j}(z_{t+1} - \mu)$. At
$t$ it is $\mu + A^{j+1}(z_t - \mu)$. Subtract:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(\mathbb{E}_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t)\,z_{t+1+j} \\
&\quad = A^{j}\big[(z_{t+1} - \mu) - A(z_t - \mu)\big] \\
&\quad = A^{j}\,\Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The return is the first entry of $z$. Write $e_1' = (1, 0)$ for the row that picks it. Then
$(\mathbb{E}_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t)\Ret{r_{t+1+j}} = e_1' A^j \Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}$. Put this into $N_{DR}$ and sum
the powers of $\rho A$ like a geometric series:

$$
\begin{aligned}
N_{DR,t+1} &= e_1' \sum_{j \ge 1} (\rho A)^j\,\Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}} \\
&= e_1'\rho A (I - \rho A)^{-1}\,\Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The sum works because $I + \rho A + (\rho A)^2 + \dots = (I - \rho A)^{-1}$ when the powers of $\rho A$ shrink to
zero. They do here: $\rho < 1$ and the VAR is stable. The surprise in the return is $e_1'\Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}$.
So the news identity gives cash-flow news as what is left:

$$
\begin{aligned}
N_{CF,t+1} &= \Ret{r_{t+1}} - \mathbb{E}_t\Ret{r_{t+1}} + N_{DR,t+1} \\
&= \big(e_1' + e_1'\rho A (I - \rho A)^{-1}\big)\Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

On goyal-welch-annual, with returns from 1927 to 2025 and $\rho = 0.969$, the row $e_1'\rho A(I - \rho A)^{-1}$ is
$(0.28, 0.78)$. A surprise rise in $\Signal{dp}$ is mostly a fall in the price. The VAR reads it as news of higher
future returns, because a high $\Signal{dp}$ forecasts high returns and stays high for years.

Finally, take the variance of both sides of the news identity:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{Var}(\Ret{r_{t+1}} - \mathbb{E}_t\Ret{r_{t+1}}) \\
&\quad = \operatorname{Var}(N_{CF}) + \operatorname{Var}(N_{DR}) \\
&\qquad - 2\operatorname{Cov}(N_{CF}, N_{DR}).
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by the left side to get the three shares in the widget.

### What the split can and cannot show

Be careful with these shares. First, cash-flow news is not measured directly. It is what is left after the VAR
forecasts returns. Anything the VAR gets wrong about expected returns lands in $N_{CF}$. Second, the VAR has only
two variables. Another predictor would change the forecasts, and so the split. Third, the sample matters a lot.
From 1873 to 2025 the shares are 64.5% for cash flows, 9.8% for discount rates and 25.8% for the covariance. The
VAR coefficients are estimates, and the widget reports no standard errors for the shares. Read them as a rough
picture, not as precise facts.

### Step 3: one identity, three slopes

Now regress each term of the return identity on today's $\Signal{dp_t}$. In $dp$ terms, the identity is
$\Ret{r_{t+1}} \approx \kappa + \Delta d_{t+1} - \rho\,\Signal{dp_{t+1}} + \Signal{dp_t}$. Run three regressions, each
with an intercept, on the same years:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Ret{r_{t+1}} &= a_r + b_r\,\Signal{dp_t} + \Err{\varepsilon^r_{t+1}}, \\
\Delta d_{t+1} &= a_d + b_d\,\Signal{dp_t} + \Err{\varepsilon^d_{t+1}}, \\
\Signal{dp_{t+1}} &= a_{dp} + \phi\,\Signal{dp_t} + \Err{\varepsilon^{dp}_{t+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

An OLS slope is linear in the left-hand variable. The slope of a sum is the sum of the slopes, when the right side
is the same. The constant $\kappa$ has slope 0, and $\Signal{dp_t}$ on itself has slope 1. So the slope of the
right side of the identity is $b_d - \rho\phi + 1$, and

$$
b_r = 1 - \rho\phi + b_d.
$$

On our data, with returns from 1927 to 2025, $b_r = 0.046$ (standard error 0.038, $t = 1.21$),
$b_d = -0.059$ (standard error 0.022, $t = -2.75$) and $\phi = 0.924$ (standard error 0.043). These are classical
OLS standard errors on 99 years. The identity holds to within 0.0002, the linearisation error.

### Step 4: the dog that did not bark

The identity turns the question around. Suppose returns are **not** predictable, so $b_r = 0$. Then the identity
forces $b_d = \rho\phi - 1$. With our $\rho$ and $\phi$, that is $-0.105$. A high $\Signal{dp}$ would have to
forecast low dividend growth, and strongly. If returns are not predictable, dividend growth must be.

Cochrane (2008, p. 9) builds a world where this null is true. In it, $\Signal{dp}$ is an AR(1) with persistence
$\phi$. Dividend growth has slope $\rho\phi - 1$ on $\Signal{dp_t}$, and returns follow from the identity. The
shocks have the covariance seen in the data. He then simulates many samples of the data's length and asks how often
a sample looks like ours. In Sherlock Holmes's story, the clue was a dog that did not bark in the night. Here the
clue is the dividend forecast that the null needs and the data do not show.

In 2,000 seeded samples with $\phi = 0.924$, the return slope is at least our 0.046 in 34.1% of them. On its own,
the return forecast is weak evidence. But the dividend slope is at least our $-0.059$ in only 15 of the 2,000
samples, 0.8%. The null predicts dividend forecasts that the data do not have. Cochrane (2008, p. 11) finds the
same pattern in CRSP data for 1927 to 2004: about one sample in five beats the return slope, and fewer than one in
fifty beats the dividend slope.

Our dividend slope is not zero. In Cochrane's data it is 0.008 (Cochrane (2008, p. 8)). On the S&P index dividends of goyal-welch-annual, a high
$\Signal{dp}$ does forecast somewhat lower dividend growth. The dog growls a little. It just does not bark as loudly
as the null requires.

## <span class="section-tag">Playground</span> Where does the data sit in the null?

Each grey dot is one simulated sample under the null of no return predictability. Its position shows the two slopes
that OLS estimates from that sample. The big dot is the data. The dashed lines through it mark the tails: dots to
the right have a larger return slope, dots above have a larger dividend slope. The other dashed line is
$b_r = 1 - \rho\phi + b_d$, with the $\phi$ of the null. The identity holds in every sample, but with that
sample's own estimate $\hat\phi$. A sample lands on the line when $\hat\phi = \phi$. Samples with a smaller $\hat\phi$ lie
to its right, with a larger return slope.

The first prompt shows the weak point of the test. It depends on $\phi$. If $\Signal{dp}$ is very persistent, the
null needs only a small dividend forecast, and our data look normal again. With $\phi = 0.95$, already 7.4% of the
samples beat the dividend slope. We do not know $\phi$ well: its standard error is 0.043. Cochrane (2008, p. 4)
answers with the long-run slope $b_r/(1 - \rho\phi)$, which combines the return slope and the persistence. On our
data it beats the null in 14.6% of the samples at $\phi = 0.924$. That is weaker than the dividend test, so on these
data the dividend slope carries most of the evidence.

<Callout type="warning">The dog test is strong evidence only if $\Signal{dp}$ is not too close to a random walk. Report
the $\phi$ that the null assumes next to every probability.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- An unexpected return splits exactly into cash-flow news minus discount-rate news. Higher expected returns from now
  on lower today's price.
- A VAR(1) measures the news: $N_{DR} = e_1'\rho A(I - \rho A)^{-1}\varepsilon$, and $N_{CF}$ is what is left.
- On goyal-welch-annual returns from 1927 to 2025, the two kinds of news each have about 40% of the variance. The
  split depends on the sample and the VAR, so read it as rough.
- The same identity ties three slopes: $b_r = 1 - \rho\phi + b_d$. If returns were not predictable, dividend growth
  would have to be, with $b_d = \rho\phi - 1$.
- The missing dividend forecast is stronger evidence than the return forecast, but only if $\Signal{dp}$ is not too
  persistent.
