## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Four moments, two parameters, four answers

Take 200 seeded draws $x_t$ of a variable whose true mean is 0.5 and whose true variance is 1. The draws are normal.
You want the mean $\mu$ and the variance $\sigma^2$. Chapter 24, *The generalized method of moments*, estimates
parameters like these from **moment conditions**: averages that should be zero at the true parameters. Here you
know four of them. The deviation $x_t - \mu$ averages zero. So does $(x_t - \mu)^2 - \sigma^2$. Normal data add
two more: the third power $(x_t - \mu)^3$ averages zero, and the fourth power averages $3\sigma^4$.

Four conditions, but only two unknowns. No $(\mu, \sigma^2)$ can make all four sample averages exactly zero, so
GMM makes them *small*. It minimises a weighted sum of their squares. And the answer depends on the weights.
Weight all four moments equally, and you get $\hat\mu = 0.500$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = 1.052$. Weight them by how
precisely each one is measured, and you get $\hat\mu = 0.466$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = 0.985$. Same data, same
moments, different estimates. The standard error of $\hat\sigma^2$ falls from 0.131 to 0.102.

Then there is a second surprise. With four moments and two parameters, two conditions are left over, and GMM has
a test for them, the $J$ test. Run it at 5% on 400 seeded samples of 100 draws in which every condition is true.
It rejects 13.3% of them, not 5%.

So you have two questions. **Which weights should you use? And how far can you trust the test that comes with
them?** This chapter answers both.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Listen hardest to the moments you measure best

Some moments are measured much more precisely than others. On the sample above, the variance of the first moment
across draws is 1.01. The variance of the fourth is 138.08. A fourth power swings wildly, because one large draw
raised to the fourth power is huge. So a sample average of fourth powers is a noisy number. When it misses zero,
that tells you little. When the sample mean misses zero by the same amount, that tells you a lot.

Equal weights ignore this. They let the noisiest moment pull the estimate as hard as the most precise one. Better
weights listen hardest to the moments you measure best. They also take account of the correlations between
moments, so two moments that carry the same news do not count it twice. This is the same idea as the GRS test of
[Chapter 16](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/16-time-series-tests-and-grs/), which weighs each alpha by how well
you know it.

With two moments and two parameters, the model is **just identified**. Both averages can be set exactly to zero,
so the weights have nothing to trade off. Weighting only matters when there are more moments than parameters.

efficient weighting trusts each moment in proportion to how precisely it is measured.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> The best weighting, made feasible, and its test

### The setting

We use the notation of Part VI. The moment function is $g(x_t, \theta)$, with $\dim g$ entries, and the model
says $\mathbb{E}[g(x_t, \theta_0)] = 0$ at the true $\theta_0$, which has $\dim\theta$ entries. Its sample mean
is $g_T(\theta) = \tfrac1T\sum_t g(x_t, \theta)$. For a weighting matrix $W$, GMM chooses

$$
\Model{\hat\theta} = \arg\min_\theta\; g_T(\theta)'\,W\,g_T(\theta).
$$

Two more objects carry the argument. $D = \partial g_T/\partial\theta'$ is the $\dim g \times \dim\theta$
Jacobian at $\theta_0$. $S$ is the long-run covariance of the moments, so that
$\operatorname{Var}(\sqrt T\,g_T(\theta_0)) \to S$. Its estimate $\hat S$ is the Newey–West sum of
[Chapter 4](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/04-autocorrelation-and-newey-west/), applied to the moments
$g(x_t, \hat\theta)$ instead of regression terms. Our toy draws are independent, so we use no lags.

### The variance for any weighting

The minimum satisfies the first-order condition $D_T(\hat\theta)'\,W\,g_T(\hat\theta) = 0$, where $D_T$ is the
sample Jacobian. Here are the steps from it to the variance.

1. Expand the moments around the truth: $g_T(\hat\theta) \approx g_T(\theta_0) + D(\hat\theta - \theta_0)$.
2. Put this into the condition, with $D_T(\hat\theta) \approx D$: $D'W\,[g_T(\theta_0) + D(\hat\theta - \theta_0)] \approx 0$.
3. Solve for the error: $\hat\theta - \theta_0 \approx -A_W\,g_T(\theta_0)$, with $A_W = (D'WD)^{-1}D'W$.
4. Take the variance, using $\operatorname{Var}(g_T(\theta_0)) \approx S/T$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(\Model{\hat\theta}) &\approx \frac1T\,A_W S A_W' \\
&= \frac1T\,(D'WD)^{-1}D'W \\
&\qquad \times SWD\,(D'WD)^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the **sandwich**: $S$ is the filling, and $(D'WD)^{-1}D'W$ is the bread on each side. It holds for any
$W$, so it is also the right standard error for a fixed weighting such as the identity.

Note one fact for later. $A_W D = (D'WD)^{-1}D'WD = I$ for every $W$.

### Why $W = S^{-1}$ gives the smallest variance

Set $W = S^{-1}$ and call the matrix in step 3 $A_* = (D'S^{-1}D)^{-1}D'S^{-1}$. The sandwich collapses:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1T\,A_* S A_*' &= \frac1T\,(D'S^{-1}D)^{-1}D'S^{-1} \\
&\qquad \times S\,S^{-1}D\,(D'S^{-1}D)^{-1} \\
&= \frac1T\,(D'S^{-1}D)^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now compare any other $W$ with it. Write $A_W = A_* + \Delta$, with $\Delta = A_W - A_*$.

1. Expand: $A_W S A_W' = A_* S A_*' + \Delta S A_*' + A_* S \Delta' + \Delta S \Delta'$.
2. The cross term: $\Delta S A_*' = \Delta S S^{-1}D(D'S^{-1}D)^{-1} = \Delta D\,(D'S^{-1}D)^{-1}$.
3. By the fact above, $\Delta D = A_W D - A_* D = I - I = 0$. So both cross terms vanish.
4. What is left: $A_W S A_W' = A_* S A_*' + \Delta S \Delta'$.
5. $S$ is a covariance matrix, so $c'\Delta S\Delta' c = (\Delta'c)'S(\Delta'c) \ge 0$ for every vector $c$.

So

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}_W(\Model{\hat\theta}) &- \frac1T\,(D'S^{-1}D)^{-1} \\
&= \frac1T\,\Delta S \Delta' \;\ge\; 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

In words: every other weighting adds a piece that is never negative, for every linear combination of the
parameters. $W = S^{-1}$ is the **efficient** weighting, and $\tfrac1T(D'S^{-1}D)^{-1}$ is the smallest variance
any weighting of these moments can reach. Scaling $W$ by a constant changes nothing, so any multiple of $S^{-1}$
is efficient too.

### Feasible GMM: three ways to estimate $S$

$S^{-1}$ is unknown. To estimate $S$ you need $\hat\theta$, and to get the efficient $\hat\theta$ you need $S$.
There are three ways out of the circle.

- **Two-step.** Estimate once with a fixed weighting, usually $W = I$, to get $\hat\theta_1$. Compute
  $\hat S(\hat\theta_1)$. Estimate again with $W = \hat S(\hat\theta_1)^{-1}$.
- **Iterated.** Repeat the second step: $\hat\theta_{k+1}$ minimises $g_T'\hat S(\hat\theta_k)^{-1}g_T$, until the
  estimate stops moving. If there is only one point where it can stop, that point does not depend on the
  first-stage weighting.
- **Continuously updated (CUE).** Let $\hat S$ move with $\theta$ inside the objective:
  $\hat\theta = \arg\min_\theta\; g_T(\theta)'\,\hat S(\theta)^{-1}g_T(\theta)$. This is no longer a fixed
  quadratic form, so its surface in the widget is not a fixed bowl.

All three use an $\hat S$ that converges to $S$. So they share one large-sample variance,
$\tfrac1T(D'S^{-1}D)^{-1}$, which we estimate with $\hat D$ and $\hat S$. Any difference between them is a
finite-sample effect. On the 200 draws of the hook, the three give $\hat\mu$ of 0.466, 0.467 and 0.464. That is
small next to a standard error of 0.070. The differences grow in shorter samples and in false models, as the
Playground shows.

### The $J$ test of the leftover moments

GMM sets $\dim\theta$ combinations of the moments to zero to find $\hat\theta$. The other
$\dim g - \dim\theta$ combinations are free. If the model is true, they should be close to zero too. Hansen's
$J$ statistic measures how far they are:

$$
J = T\,g_T(\hat\theta)'\,\hat S^{-1}g_T(\hat\theta) \;\to\; \chi^2_{\dim g - \dim\theta}.
$$

Here is why the limit is $\chi^2$, step by step, for efficient GMM.

1. From steps 1 and 3 above with $W = S^{-1}$: $g_T(\hat\theta) \approx (I - DA_*)\,g_T(\theta_0)$.
2. Standardise. Let $z = \sqrt T\,S^{-1/2}g_T(\theta_0)$. By the central limit theorem, $z$ is approximately
   $N(0, I)$ with $\dim g$ entries.
3. Let $H = S^{-1/2}D$. Then $\sqrt T\,S^{-1/2}g_T(\hat\theta) \approx (I - P)\,z$, with
   $P = H(H'H)^{-1}H'$. Multiply it out to check: $S^{-1/2}DA_* S^{1/2} = P$.
4. $P$ projects onto the $\dim\theta$ columns of $H$. So $I - P$ is symmetric, satisfies $(I - P)^2 = I - P$, and
   has rank $\dim g - \dim\theta$.
5. So $J \approx z'(I - P)'(I - P)z = z'(I - P)z$.
6. Write $I - P = Q\Lambda Q'$, with $Q$ orthogonal and $\Lambda$ diagonal. Its diagonal holds
   $\dim g - \dim\theta$ ones and zeros elsewhere. Then $Q'z$ is again $N(0, I)$, and $J$ is the sum of
   $\dim g - \dim\theta$ independent squared standard normals. That is the $\chi^2_{\dim g - \dim\theta}$.

In our example, $\dim g = 4$ and $\dim\theta = 2$, so $J$ is compared with $\chi^2_2$, whose 5% cut-off is 5.99.
On the 200 draws of the hook, two-step GMM gives $J = 0.54$, with a $p$-value of 0.76. The data do not reject
the model.

Two warnings. First, step 6 needs $W = \hat S^{-1}$. With the identity, $T\,g_T'Wg_T$ is a weighted sum of
squared normals, not a $\chi^2$, so it cannot be read from the $\chi^2$ table. Second, the limit is a
large-sample result. In the hook, at $T = 100$, the 5% test rejected 13.3% of true models.

### When a fixed weighting is better

The theorem compares weightings when $S$ is known. In practice $\hat S$ is estimated, and that has two costs.

**$\hat S$ is noisy.** With four moments, $\hat S$ has ten distinct entries, and the variance of a fourth power
needs eighth powers of the data. In a short sample these entries are poorly measured, and the "efficient" weights
inherit the noise. A seeded study of 300 samples of 50 draws shows it. The root mean squared error of
$\hat\sigma^2$ is 0.210 with the identity and 0.254 with two-step GMM. The fixed weighting wins. Two-step GMM is
also too sure of itself. Its average reported standard error is 0.144, well below its actual error of 0.254. With
1,000 draws, the order flips back: 0.051 for the identity, 0.049 for two-step GMM.

**The moments that matter can lose their weight.** $S^{-1}$ chooses weights for statistical reasons. It may judge
a model mostly on combinations of moments that no one cares about. Nagel (2013, p. 20) observes that many
researchers report how well a model prices the portfolios they find economically interesting, instead of the
statistically chosen mixes that a formal test weighs most. He also shows (p. 21) how far apart the two
weightings can be. When a traded factor is among the moments, the efficient weighting makes the model price that
factor exactly in large samples, while the identity may hardly look at it. So the choice of $W$ is also a choice
of which moments the model must fit.

A fixed $W$ is a valid choice. Its estimate is consistent, its standard errors come from the sandwich, and you
choose which moments matter. What you give up is the smallest asymptotic variance, and the simple $\chi^2$ form
of $J$.

<Callout type="warning">Efficient GMM is efficient only in large samples. When $T$ is short relative to the number
of moments, a fixed weighting with sandwich standard errors can give the more accurate estimate and the more
honest standard error.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Playground</span> How honest is the $J$ test?

The widget draws 400 seeded samples from the toy model, estimates each one with all four moments, and plots the
histogram of $J$ against the $\chi^2_2$ density it should follow. With normal tails, every moment is true, so the
share of samples above the 5% cut-off is the **size** of the test, and it should be 5%. With fat tails, the mean,
the variance and the third moment are still right, but the fourth central moment is 9.75 instead of 3. The model
is false, so the share rejected is the test's **power**, and higher is better.

The prompts show three things. The $J$ test over-rejects true models in short samples, by a lot at $T = 50$. In
short samples it also has almost no power against this false moment. And the three feasible estimators, which
share one large-sample theory, behave very differently with 100 draws. We do not have a simple story for why
iterated GMM and CUE lose so much power here. The lesson is to simulate the test in a world like your data before
you trust its $p$-value.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- With more moments than parameters, the weighting matrix $W$ decides how the moments trade off. For any $W$, the
  variance is the sandwich $\tfrac1T(D'WD)^{-1}D'WSWD(D'WD)^{-1}$.
- $W = S^{-1}$ is efficient. Its variance $\tfrac1T(D'S^{-1}D)^{-1}$ is the smallest, because every other $W$ adds
  $\tfrac1T\Delta S\Delta' \ge 0$.
- Two-step, iterated and continuously-updated GMM make $W = \hat S^{-1}$ feasible. They share one large-sample
  theory but differ in short samples.
- $J = T\,g_T'\hat S^{-1}g_T \to \chi^2_{\dim g - \dim\theta}$ tests the leftover moments. In the toy model it
  rejects 13.3% of true models at 5% with 100 draws, so simulate its size before you trust it.
- A fixed weighting, with sandwich standard errors, is the better choice when $\hat S$ is noisy or when the
  moments that matter must keep their weight.
