<Callout type="note" title="What you need from Chapters 1 and 7">
[Chapter 1](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/01-returns/) defines the simple return $\Ret{R}$, the gross return
$1 + \Ret{R}$ and the excess return $\Ret{R^e} = \Ret{R} - \Rf{R^f}$. Chapter 7 is not written yet, so here is the
short version of the three tools this chapter uses from it.

- **Covariance and correlation.** $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ is positive when $X$ and $Y$ tend to be high
  together. The correlation $\operatorname{corr}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)/(\sigma(X)\sigma(Y))$ is the same
  idea on a scale from $-1$ to $1$.
- **The Sharpe ratio** of an asset is its mean excess return divided by its standard deviation,
  $SR = \mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e}]/\sigma(\Ret{R^e})$: the reward for each unit of risk.
- **A beta** is a regression slope. $\beta_{i,M}$ is the slope of asset $i$'s excess return on a variable $M$:
  $\operatorname{Cov}(\Ret{R^e_i}, \SDF{M})/\operatorname{Var}(\SDF{M})$.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> What does the equity premium say about investors?

From July 1926 to August 2026, the US stock market paid 8.3% a year more than one-month Treasury bills, on
average. The data is the market excess return in ff3-monthly, 1,202 months. That reward came with risk: the
volatility of the excess return was 18.4% a year. Divide the first number by the second and you get the market's
**Sharpe ratio**, 0.45.

So here is the question. That reward was paid to the people who held the stocks. Why did they need it? A price is
a deal between a buyer today and a payoff later. Somewhere inside that deal sits a rule that says how much a
risky dollar next year is worth today. If you could see the rule, could you tell from 0.45 what kind of
investors set it?

This chapter builds that rule from one decision: how much to save. It gives one formula for the price of any asset,
$p = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}x]$. At the end, the number 0.45 turns into a fact about the rule itself. The discount
factor $\SDF{M}$ must swing by at least 45% of its mean every year.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Not every dollar is worth the same

Think of next year as two possible **states of the world**: bad times and good times. In bad times, jobs are lost
and you consume little. In good times, you consume a lot. A payoff is a list of what an asset pays in each state.

An extra dollar is worth more to you when you have few of them. So a dollar that arrives in bad times is worth
more today than a dollar that arrives in good times. The **stochastic discount factor** (SDF) $\SDF{M}$ puts a
number on this. It is a list with one value per state: what one dollar in that state is worth to you today, per
unit of probability. "Stochastic" means random: you do not know today which value applies.

The price of a payoff then follows from one rule. For each state, multiply the payoff by $\SDF{M}$ and by the
probability of the state. Then add up the states. That is an expected value: $p = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}x]$.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> the widget opens with an SDF of 1.10 in bad times and 0.80 in good
times, each state with probability one half. Click "stock", "bond" and "insurance" in turn. All three payoffs have

The bond pays 1.05 in both states. Its price, 0.998, is the expected payoff discounted at the risk-free rate. Here
that rate is 5.3%. The bond is the benchmark: it carries no risk, so its expected return is exactly the risk-free
rate.

The stock pays 0.80 in bad times and 1.30 in good times. It pays least when a dollar is worth most. So it is
worth less than the bond. Its price falls to 0.960, and its expected return rises to 9.4%. The extra 4.1% is its
**risk premium**: the pay for holding an asset that lets you down in bad times.

The insurance pays 1.30 in bad times and 0.80 in good times. It pays most when a dollar is worth most. So you pay
more for it than for the bond: 1.035. Its expected return is only 1.4%, and its risk premium is −3.8%. People
accept a low return because insurance pays off in exactly the states they fear.

<Callout type="key">An asset's price depends on *when* it pays, not only on how much it pays on average. An asset
that pays in bad times is expensive and has a low expected return. An asset that pays in good times is cheap and
has a high expected return.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> From a saver's choice to $p = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}x]$

### The Euler equation

Take an investor who lives for two dates, $t$ and $t+1$. Their utility is

$$
u(C_t) + \delta\,\mathbb{E}_t[u(C_{t+1})].
$$

Here $u$ is a utility function: it rises with consumption $C$, but each extra unit adds less. $\delta$ is the
**time-discount factor**, a number just below 1 that says how impatient the investor is. $\mathbb{E}_t$ is the
expectation given what the investor knows at $t$.

The investor has an income $e_t$ today and $e_{t+1}$ next period. They can buy $\xi$ units of an asset at price
$P_t$, and each unit pays $X_{t+1}$ at $t+1$. So

$$
C_t = e_t - P_t\,\xi, \qquad C_{t+1} = e_{t+1} + X_{t+1}\,\xi.
$$

The investor picks $\xi$ to make utility as large as possible. At the best $\xi$, the derivative of utility with
respect to $\xi$ is zero. Use the chain rule. Today's part gives $u'(C_t) \times (-P_t)$. Next period's part
gives $\delta\,\mathbb{E}_t[u'(C_{t+1}) \times X_{t+1}]$, because the derivative of an expectation is the
expectation of the derivative. Set the sum to zero:

$$
-P_t\,u'(C_t) + \delta\,\mathbb{E}_t[u'(C_{t+1})\,X_{t+1}] = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P_t\,u'(C_t) = \mathbb{E}_t[\delta\,u'(C_{t+1})\,X_{t+1}].
$$

This is the **consumption Euler equation**. Read it as a balance. On the left is the cost of one more unit: you
give up $P_t$ of consumption today, and each unit of it is worth $u'(C_t)$. On the right is the benefit: $X_{t+1}$
more consumption next period, each unit worth $\delta\,u'(C_{t+1})$, on average.

Now divide both sides by $u'(C_t)$. It is known at $t$, so it can move inside $\mathbb{E}_t$:

$$
P_t = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\SDF{M_{t+1}}\,X_{t+1}\right],
\qquad
\SDF{M_{t+1}} = \delta\,\frac{u'(C_{t+1})}{u'(C_t)}.
$$

This is $p = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}x]$ from the widget, with dates on it. $\SDF{M_{t+1}}$ is high when consumption next
period is low, because $u'$ is then high. That is the "bad times" of the widget.

### Power utility

A common choice is **power utility**, $u(C) = C^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$. Its derivative is $u'(C) = C^{-\gamma}$.
Put this into the ratio:

$$
\SDF{M_{t+1}} = \delta\,\frac{C_{t+1}^{-\gamma}}{C_t^{-\gamma}} = \delta \left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{-\gamma}.
$$

The number $\gamma$ is **relative risk aversion**: the larger it is, the more a fall in consumption hurts. For
example, take $\delta = 0.99$ and $\gamma = 5$. If consumption grows by 2%, then $\SDF{M} = 0.99 \times
1.02^{-5} = 0.90$. If it falls by 2%, then $\SDF{M} = 0.99 \times 0.98^{-5} = 1.10$. A bad year raises $\SDF{M}$.

### Returns and the risk-free rate

Divide $P_t = \mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M_{t+1}}X_{t+1}]$ by $P_t$. Again $P_t$ is known at $t$, and $X_{t+1}/P_t$ is the
gross return $1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}}$:

$$
1 = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\SDF{M_{t+1}}\,(1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}})\right].
$$

Take the unconditional expectation of both sides. The average of a conditional expectation is the plain
expectation (the law of iterated expectations), so $1 = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R})]$ holds too. From here on
we drop the dates. We also treat $\Rf{R^f}$ as a constant. That is a simplification: the real risk-free rate moves,
and the conditional version above allows for it.

The risk-free asset pays the same $1 + \Rf{R^f}$ in every state. So it comes out of the expectation:

$$
1 = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]\,(1 + \Rf{R^f})
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
1 + \Rf{R^f} = \frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]}.
$$

In the widget, $\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}] = 0.95$, so $\Rf{R^f} = 1/0.95 - 1 = 5.3\%$.

### Risk premia are covariances

Write the pricing equation for a risky asset and for the risk-free asset, and subtract the second from the
first. The excess return $\Ret{R^e} = \Ret{R} - \Rf{R^f}$ costs nothing to hold:

$$
0 = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R})] - \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}(1 + \Rf{R^f})] = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}\,\Ret{R^e}].
$$

By the definition of covariance, $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y] + \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$.
Apply it here, then solve for the mean excess return, and use $1/\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}] = 1 + \Rf{R^f}$:

$$
0 = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]\,\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e}] + \operatorname{Cov}(\SDF{M}, \Ret{R^e})
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e}] = -\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\SDF{M}, \Ret{R^e})}{\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]}
= -(1 + \Rf{R^f})\operatorname{Cov}(\SDF{M}, \Ret{R^e}).
$$

This is the key result of the chapter. An asset earns a positive premium when its return is low in the states where
$\SDF{M}$ is high: a negative covariance. The stock in the widget is such an asset. The insurance has a positive
covariance, so its premium is negative. Its own volatility does not enter at all. Only its covariance with
$\SDF{M}$ is priced.

### The Hansen–Jagannathan bound

A covariance is a correlation times two standard deviations:
$\operatorname{Cov}(\SDF{M}, \Ret{R^e}) = \operatorname{corr}(\SDF{M}, \Ret{R^e})\,\sigma(\SDF{M})\,\sigma(\Ret{R^e})$.
Put this into the premium, divide both sides by $\sigma(\Ret{R^e})$, and use $1 + \Rf{R^f} = 1/\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]$:

$$
\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e}]}{\sigma(\Ret{R^e})} = -\operatorname{corr}(\SDF{M}, \Ret{R^e})\,
\frac{\sigma(\SDF{M})}{\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]}.
$$

The left side is the Sharpe ratio. A correlation is never larger than 1 in absolute value. So take absolute values
on both sides:

$$
\frac{\sigma(\SDF{M})}{\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]} \;\ge\; \frac{|\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e}]|}{\sigma(\Ret{R^e})} = |SR|.
$$

This is the **Hansen–Jagannathan bound**. Any SDF that prices an asset must be volatile enough: its standard
deviation, as a share of its mean, is at least the asset's Sharpe ratio. A high Sharpe ratio in the data demands
a volatile SDF.

### The beta form

Start again from $\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_i}] = -\operatorname{Cov}(\SDF{M}, \Ret{R^e_i})/\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]$ for an
asset $i$. Multiply and divide by $\operatorname{Var}(\SDF{M})$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_i}]
= \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\Ret{R^e_i}, \SDF{M})}{\operatorname{Var}(\SDF{M})}
\times \left(-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\SDF{M})}{\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]}\right)
= \beta_{i,M}\,\lambda_M.
$$

The first factor, $\beta_{i,M}$, is the slope of a regression of the asset's excess return on $\SDF{M}$. It is
specific to the asset. The second factor, $\lambda_M$, is the same for every asset, and it is negative. For the
stock in the widget, $\beta_{i,M} = -1.74$ and $\lambda_M = -0.024$, and their product is the 4.1% premium. Every
factor model in this course has this shape: expected return equals a beta times a price of risk.

<Callout type="key">$p = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}x]$ gives three results in a few lines. $1 + \Rf{R^f} =
1/\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]$. Risk premia are minus covariances with $\SDF{M}$. And no asset's Sharpe ratio can exceed
$\sigma(\SDF{M})/\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]$.</Callout>

### What the bound can and cannot show

The power-utility SDF in this simulation needs a risk aversion above 20 to price the market. With that much risk
aversion it also implies a risk-free rate far above any rate in the data. That is the start of a famous puzzle,
which Part IX studies with real consumption data. Here, keep the limits of the evidence in view.

First, 0.45 is an estimate. Its standard error is about 0.10 a year, since it is close to $\sqrt{12/T}$ with
$T = 1{,}202$ months. Two standard errors on each side give a range from 0.25 to 0.65. The subsamples agree on
the level: 0.47 since 1963 and 0.48 since 2000. But with fewer months their standard errors grow to 0.13 and 0.19.
The annual figures also assume that monthly returns are independent, so that the ratio scales with
$\sqrt{12}$.

Second, the bound uses one asset. Other assets or portfolios may have higher Sharpe ratios, and each of them
raises the line. So the market alone gives a lower limit on how volatile the SDF must be, not the full demand.

Third, the bound is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. An SDF above the line is volatile enough. It
still has to have the right correlation with returns. A model inside the region can still misprice the market.

Even so, the lower limit is large. A risk-free rate of 3.2% a year, the ff3-monthly average, gives
$\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}] = 1/1.032 = 0.97$. Then $\sigma(\SDF{M}) \ge 0.97 \times 0.45 = 0.44$. The value of a dollar
across states must vary by about 44% a year.

<Callout type="warning">The Hansen–Jagannathan bound is a test that any model of the SDF must pass, not a proof that
a model is right. It rests on an estimated Sharpe ratio, so report the standard error with it.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- A saver's choice gives the Euler equation $P_t\,u'(C_t) = \mathbb{E}_t[\delta\,u'(C_{t+1})X_{t+1}]$, so every
  price is $p = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}x]$ with $\SDF{M} = \delta\,u'(C_{t+1})/u'(C_t)$. With power utility,
  $\SDF{M} = \delta(C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\gamma}$.
- The risk-free rate is $1 + \Rf{R^f} = 1/\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]$.
- Risk premia are covariances: $\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e}] = -(1 + \Rf{R^f})\operatorname{Cov}(\SDF{M}, \Ret{R^e})$.
  The same premium in beta form is $\beta_{i,M}\lambda_M$.
- The Hansen–Jagannathan bound: $\sigma(\SDF{M})/\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}] \ge |SR|$. On ff3-monthly, 1926–2026, the
  market's Sharpe ratio is 0.45 a year, with a standard error of 0.10.
- A power-utility SDF with smooth consumption needs a very high risk aversion to pass the bound.
