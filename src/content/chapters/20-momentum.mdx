## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> A rule that beats the model, until it doesn't

Here is a trading rule you could follow with nothing but past prices. At the start of each month, rank stocks by
how much they rose over the past year. Buy the top group, the **winners**. Sell short the bottom group, the
**losers**. Hold for one month, then rank again.

The Kenneth French Data Library publishes the return of this rule as a factor called Mom. From 1927-01 to
2026-08, 1,196 months, it earned 7.35% a year on average, with a volatility of 16.3% a year. The market earned
8.31% a year over T-bills, with a volatility of 18.4%. So the two have the same Sharpe ratio, 0.45 a year. That
already is a puzzle: Mom has negative betas on the market and on value, so the three-factor model predicts that it
should *lose* money. Its alpha against that model is 11.2% a year, with a $t$-statistic of 7.77.

Now look at the worst month. In 1932-08, Mom lost 52.6% in one month. The market rose 37.1% in the same month. In
2009-04 Mom lost 34.4% while the market rose 10.2%. Why does a rule with such a large alpha fall apart exactly
when the market recovers? And how much of its good record is luck of the sample? This chapter builds the factor,
measures its alpha, and then explains its crashes.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Two paths to the same Sharpe ratio

The widget from [Chapter 1](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/01-returns/) now has a third line. It invests one
dollar in T-bills and adds the momentum position on top: each month the dollar earns the T-bill rate plus Mom.
Momentum is a long–short position, so it needs no money of its own. The T-bills are the collateral.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> keep the log scale and the start in 1927. The market dollar ends
at \$17,985 and the momentum dollar at \$8,231. Look at the early 1930s: the momentum line drops by more than
half in two summer months. Then move the start to 1945. From there the momentum line climbs steadily until 2009,
and then it goes flat.</p>

The two lines have the same Sharpe ratio over the full sample, but they get there in different ways. The market
has bad years spread across the century. Momentum climbs smoothly for long stretches and then falls off a cliff.
One number shows the difference: **skewness**, which measures whether the big surprises are on the upside or the
downside (a negative value means the big ones are losses). For monthly market excess returns it is 0.15, close to
zero. For Mom it is −3.02. Most months are calm, and a few months are disasters.

This changes what the Sharpe ratio means. A Sharpe ratio from a sample is honest only if the sample contains the
rare bad months in their true proportion. Take the 768 months from 1945-01 to 2008-12. Momentum's Sharpe ratio
there is 0.82 a year, almost twice the full-sample value, and its skewness is only −0.60. That sample misses the
crashes of the 1930s and of 2009. From 2009-01 to 2026-08, the average return of Mom is −0.84% a year.

Why should a rule as simple as "buy winners" work at all? Cochrane (2011, p. 1062–1063) puts it in terms of
prediction. A tiny serial correlation in individual stock returns, too small to notice in a regression, is enough.
Last year's winners rose a lot. If a small part of that move carries on into next year, a portfolio of winners has
a large expected return. So momentum is return predictability, as in Part II, but across stocks rather than over
time.

<Callout type="key">Momentum earns a large average return that the three-factor model cannot explain. It earns it
with negative skewness: long calm stretches and rare, very large losses. A sample without those losses makes the
strategy look far better than it is.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Signal, factor, alpha and the crash beta

### The formation signal

Write $\Ret{R_{i,s}}$ for the simple return of stock $i$ in month $s$. To choose the position for month $t$, the
rule compounds the returns from month $t-12$ to month $t-2$:

$$
\Ret{R_{i,t-12 \to t-2}} = \prod_{s=t-12}^{t-2} (1 + R_{i,s}) - 1.
$$

The sign $\prod$ multiplies the terms, here $(1 + R_{i,t-12})(1 + R_{i,t-11}) \cdots (1 + R_{i,t-2})$. That is 11
monthly returns. Month $t-1$, the most recent one, is left out on purpose. Here is an example. A stock
gains 2% in each of months $t-12$ to $t-2$, and then loses 5% in month $t-1$. Its signal is
$1.02^{11} - 1 = 24.3\%$. The loss of the last month plays no part.

Why skip the last month? At a one-month horizon, stock returns tend to partly reverse, not continue: a stock that
jumped last month tends to give some of it back. Part of that is a measurement effect. A price recorded at the
bid one month and at the ask the next shows a return that no one could earn. If the signal included month $t-1$,
the winners would carry this reversal with them, and the strategy would be weaker. Our data cannot show the size
of the effect, because the published portfolios already skip the month. Horizon matters at the other end too.
Fama & French (1992, p. 451) find that a stock's past *three-year* return has no power to explain average
returns in their sample. Momentum lives at the one-year horizon.

### The factor from a 2×3 sort

Sorting stocks on the signal alone could let size creep in: the winner and loser groups could hold more small
stocks than the middle group does. So the factor uses two sorts, as SMB and HML do in Chapter 19, *Size and value*. Each month, stocks are split at
the median size into small (S) and big (B). Each half is then split on the signal into losers (L, the bottom
30%), a middle group (M), and winners (W, the top 30%), with NYSE breakpoints, as the data library describes.
That gives six value-weighted portfolios. The factor is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Factor{WML_t} = {} &\tfrac12\big(R_{SW,t} + R_{BW,t}\big) \\
&- \tfrac12\big(R_{SL,t} + R_{BL,t}\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

The name stands for winners minus losers. The data library calls it Mom, and the library code calls the rebuilt
version UMD (up minus down). They are the same thing. Both legs hold equal weights in small and big stocks, so
the factor is a bet on past returns, with size averaged out. The middle group plays no part.

### The three-factor alpha

Is momentum just a mix of known factors? Regress it on the three Fama–French factors $\Factor{f_t}$ (Mkt-RF, SMB
and HML), exactly as each test portfolio was regressed in
[Chapter 16](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/16-time-series-tests-and-grs/):

$$
\Factor{WML_t} = \Err{\alpha} + \beta'\Factor{f_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_t}.
$$

Take means of both sides. The residuals average zero in sample, so

$$
\overline{WML} = \Err{\hat\alpha} + \hat\beta'\bar f.
$$

The model's prediction for momentum's mean is $\hat\beta'\bar f$, and the alpha is what is left. From 1927-01 to
2026-08 the betas are −0.22 on the market, −0.06 on SMB and −0.46 on HML. All three point the wrong way. The
predicted mean is −3.89% a year, but the actual mean is 7.35%, so $\hat\alpha = 7.35\% - (-3.89\%) = 11.2\%$ a
year, with a $t$-statistic of 7.77 (8.13 with White standard errors, from
[Chapter 3](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/03-regression-and-standard-errors/)). The three factors explain only 23%
of momentum's monthly variance. From 1963-07, the sample of Chapter 16, the alpha is 9.80% a year, with a
$t$-statistic of 5.47.

Cochrane (2011, p. 1060) lists momentum first among the strategies whose average returns do not line up with
market, size and value betas. He also points to a second puzzle (p. 1075). If momentum were only a slow reaction
of each stock to its own news, why would all winners rise and fall together, as if exposed to one common risk?
That common movement is what lets us treat momentum as a factor. Adding Mom to the three factors gives the
four-factor model named after Carhart (1997). Chapter 21 asks how to decide when a new factor really adds
something.

### A separate beta in bear markets

The full-sample market beta of momentum is small. But it is not constant. Define a bear-market indicator from the
market excess return over the 24 months before month $t$:

$$
I_{B,t-1} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \prod_{s=t-24}^{t-1} (1 + R^e_{m,s}) < 1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

It is known at the end of month $t-1$. Now let both the intercept and the beta depend on it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Factor{WML_t} = {} &\Err{\alpha_0} + \Err{\alpha_B}\, I_{B,t-1} \\
&+ \big(\beta_0 + \beta_B\, I_{B,t-1}\big)\,\Ret{R^e_{m,t}} + \Err{\varepsilon_t}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Read it in two steps. In a normal month, $I_{B,t-1} = 0$ and the beta is $\beta_0$. After a bear market,
$I_{B,t-1} = 1$ and the beta is $\beta_0 + \beta_B$. So $\beta_B$ is the change in beta. In OLS, this is one
regression of $WML_t$ on a constant, $I_{B,t-1}$, $R^e_{m,t}$ and the product $I_{B,t-1} R^e_{m,t}$. The regression
carries the names of Daniel & Moskowitz (2016). We apply it to our data with White standard errors. The first 24 months
only feed the indicator, so the fit runs from 1929-01, 1,172 months, of which 275 follow a bear market.

The estimates are $\hat\beta_0 = 0.04$ ($t = 1.18$) and $\hat\beta_B = -0.69$ ($t = -6.84$). In normal months
momentum has almost no market exposure. After a bear market its beta is $0.04 - 0.69 = -0.64$. The intercepts are
0.78% a month in normal months ($t = 7.05$) and 0.40% a month lower after bear markets ($t = -1.19$), a difference we
cannot separate from zero.

Why does the beta flip? After two bad years, the losers are the stocks that fell most with the market: the
high-beta stocks. The winners are the stocks that held up: the low-beta ones. So WML is now short high beta and
long low beta. Its beta is negative. When the market then rebounds hard, the losers jump the most, and the short
side destroys the position. Momentum crashes are not random. They are what a negative beta does in a sharp rally.

The regression explains part of a crash, not all of it. In 2009-04, the market excess return was 10.2%, and the
fitted value of the regression is −6.2%. The actual return was −34.4%. In 1932-08 the fitted value is −23.5%
against an actual −52.6%. The regression shows *when* the risk is high. It does not capture its full size.

### What the evidence can and cannot show

The alpha of momentum is large and precisely measured. A $t$-statistic near 8 over 1,196 months is hard to explain
as luck. But three limits apply.

First, the Sharpe ratio depends on the sample. Over the full sample it equals the market's. A study that began in
1945 and ended in 2008 would report almost twice as much, because the sample left out the crashes. Since 2009 the
average return has been slightly negative.

Second, the factor returns are before trading costs. The rule trades many stocks every month, and some of the
losers it sells short may be hard to borrow. Our data has no costs, so we cannot say how much of the alpha
survives them.

Third, a large alpha with large negative skewness does not have to be a free lunch. An investor who fears
sudden losses in a market rebound may demand a high average return for holding them. Whether that, or slow
reaction to news, explains momentum is still debated. The data in this chapter cannot decide between them.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- The momentum signal compounds a stock's returns from month $t-12$ to $t-2$, skipping the last month, where
  returns tend to reverse.
- $WML_t$ buys winners and sells losers within small and within big stocks, so size is averaged out. From 1927-01
  to 2026-08 it earned 7.35% a year.
- Its betas on the three Fama–French factors are negative, so its three-factor alpha, 11.2% a year
  ($t = 7.77$), is larger than its mean.
- After a bear market, momentum's market beta turns strongly negative, −0.64 against 0.04 in normal months. A
  sharp rally then becomes a crash: all ten worst months came after a bear market.
- The Sharpe ratio is 0.45 a year over the full sample and 0.82 from 1945 to 2008. A sample without the crashes
  overstates the strategy.
