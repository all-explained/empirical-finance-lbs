## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Prices at 87 times dividends

At the end of 2025, the S&P composite index cost 86.7 times the dividends its companies paid that year. Since
1872 its typical level is 26.4 (the average of the log ratio, turned back into a ratio). Only once was it higher: 88.0, at the end of 1999. Its
lowest value was 9.9, at the end of 1917.

So here is the question you probably have. If you pay 87 dollars today for each dollar of dividends, what has to
happen next? There are only three ways out. The dividends can grow fast, so the price was fair after all. The
returns can be low, because you paid a lot for what you get. Or the price can keep rising faster than the
dividends forever, which is a bubble.

There is no fourth way. This chapter proves that with a short piece of algebra due to Campbell and Shiller
(1988). Then it asks the data which of the three ways history took. The answer depends on which century you look
at, and the chapter is honest about that.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> A return is a yield plus a change in the ratio

Think of a flat that you rent out. Your return in one year has two parts: the rent, and the change in the price
of the flat. The price of the flat is the rent times a price–rent ratio. So the price can change for two reasons:
the rent grows, or the ratio changes.

A stock works the same way, with dividends as the rent. The return in a year is the dividend yield plus the growth
in the price. The growth in the price is the growth in dividends plus the change in the price–dividend ratio.
That is pure accounting. It holds every year, with no model of investors behind it.

The problem is that this accounting is not linear in the ratio. Campbell and Shiller found a straight-line version
that is almost exact. Here is the test of "almost". The solid line is the exact log return of the S&P composite,
year by year since 1873, computed from prices and dividends. The dashed line is the straight-line rule that the
next section derives. It uses only the price–dividend ratio at the start and the end of the year and the growth of
dividends.

Now the logic of the hook becomes clear. Run the rule forward, year after year. Today's ratio then equals the sum
of all the future dividend growth, minus all the future returns, plus the ratio far in the future. If today's ratio
is high, at least one of those three terms must be large. The identity cannot tell you which one. Only data can.

<Callout type="key">A high price–dividend ratio must be followed by high dividend growth, low returns, or an even
higher ratio later. This is accounting, not a theory. The open question is how much each of the three carries.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Linearise, iterate, decompose

### The exact identity

Write $P_t$ for the price at the end of year $t$ and $D_{t+1}$ for the dividends paid during year $t+1$. The
gross return is $1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}} = (P_{t+1} + D_{t+1})/P_t$. Multiply and divide by $D_{t+1}$ and by $D_t$:

$$
1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}} = \left(\frac{P_{t+1}}{D_{t+1}} + 1\right)\frac{D_{t+1}}{D_t}\,\frac{D_t}{P_t}.
$$

Take logs. Use lower-case letters for logs, so $\Signal{pd_t} = p_t - d_t$ is the log price–dividend ratio,
$\Delta d_{t+1} = d_{t+1} - d_t$ is log dividend growth and $\Ret{r_{t+1}} = \log(1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}})$. Since
$P_{t+1}/D_{t+1} = e^{\Signal{pd_{t+1}}}$,

$$
\Ret{r_{t+1}} = \log\!\left(1 + e^{\Signal{pd_{t+1}}}\right) + \Delta d_{t+1} - \Signal{pd_t}.
$$

This holds exactly. It is the solid line in the widget. Note that $\Ret{r}$ here is the total return, not the
excess return of [Chapter 9](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/09-time-varying-expected-returns/), because the identity is about prices and dividends only.

### The straight-line approximation

Only the first term is not linear. Call it $f(x) = \log(1 + e^x)$. Its slope is

$$
f'(x) = \frac{e^x}{1 + e^x}.
$$

Replace $f$ by its tangent line at the sample mean $\overline{pd}$. That is a first-order Taylor expansion:

$$
f(\Signal{pd_{t+1}}) \approx f(\overline{pd}) + f'(\overline{pd})\,(\Signal{pd_{t+1}} - \overline{pd}).
$$

Give the slope and the constant their own names:

$$
\rho = \frac{e^{\overline{pd}}}{1 + e^{\overline{pd}}}, \qquad \kappa = \log\!\left(1 + e^{\overline{pd}}\right) -
\rho\,\overline{pd}.
$$

Then $f(\Signal{pd_{t+1}}) \approx \kappa + \rho\,\Signal{pd_{t+1}}$, and the return identity becomes

$$
\Ret{r_{t+1}} \approx \kappa + \rho\,\Signal{pd_{t+1}} + \Delta d_{t+1} - \Signal{pd_t}.
$$

On our data the mean log ratio $\overline{pd}$ is the log of 26.4, so $\rho = 26.4/27.4 = 0.964$ and $\kappa = 0.157$. The number $\rho$ is a
little below 1. It works like a discount factor, as you will see in a moment.

The function $f$ curves upward, and a tangent line lies below a curve that bends up. So the approximation is never
above the exact return, and the error is never negative. It grows when $\Signal{pd_{t+1}}$ is far from the mean.
Campbell and Shiller (1988, p. 5) also linearise around a sample mean, and they too find the error small.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> in the widget above, choose "error" and move the slider. It moves
the point of the tangent away from the mean. At $-1.0$, the tangent sits at a ratio of 9.7, and the largest error
grows to 11.9%, in 1999. At $+1.0$, the tangent sits at 71.8, and the largest error is 5.6%. The rule is only good
near the ratios it was built for.</p>

### Solve forward

Rearrange the approximation to put today's ratio on the left:

$$
\Signal{pd_t} \approx \kappa + \Delta d_{t+1} - \Ret{r_{t+1}} + \rho\,\Signal{pd_{t+1}}.
$$

The same equation holds one year later, for $\Signal{pd_{t+1}}$. Substitute it in:

$$
\Signal{pd_t} \approx \kappa(1 + \rho) + (\Delta d_{t+1} - \Ret{r_{t+1}}) + \rho\,(\Delta d_{t+2} - \Ret{r_{t+2}})
+ \rho^2\,\Signal{pd_{t+2}}.
$$

Each substitution adds one more year, discounted by one more power of $\rho$. After $k$ steps, and with the
geometric sum $1 + \rho + \dots + \rho^{k-1} = (1 - \rho^k)/(1 - \rho)$,

$$
\Signal{pd_t} \approx \kappa\,\frac{1 - \rho^k}{1 - \rho} + \sum_{j=1}^{k} \rho^{j-1}\left(\Delta d_{t+j} -
\Ret{r_{t+j}}\right) + \rho^k\,\Signal{pd_{t+k}}.
$$

The last term is the **bubble term**. If the ratio does not explode faster than $1/\rho$ per year, then
$\rho^k\,\Signal{pd_{t+k}}$ goes to zero as $k$ grows, and

$$
\Signal{pd_t} = \frac{\kappa}{1 - \rho} + \sum_{j \ge 1} \rho^{j-1}\left(\Delta d_{t+j} - \Ret{r_{t+j}}\right).
$$

This holds for the realised future, so it also holds for its expectation given what you know at $t$. A high
$\Signal{pd_t}$ today must come with high expected dividend growth, low expected returns, or both.

### From the identity to variance shares

The predictability literature uses $\Signal{dp_t} = -\Signal{pd_t}$, the log dividend–price ratio of Chapter 9.
Multiply the $k$-step equation by $-1$:

$$
\Signal{dp_t} \approx \text{constant} + \sum_{j=1}^{k} \rho^{j-1}\Ret{r_{t+j}} - \sum_{j=1}^{k}
\rho^{j-1}\Delta d_{t+j} + \rho^k\,\Signal{dp_{t+k}}.
$$

Take the covariance of both sides with $\Signal{dp_t}$. The left side gives $\operatorname{Var}(\Signal{dp_t})$,
and the constant gives zero. Divide by $\operatorname{Var}(\Signal{dp_t})$:

$$
1 \approx \underbrace{\frac{\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(\Signal{dp_t}, \sum_j \rho^{j-1}\Ret{r_{t+j}}\right)}
{\operatorname{Var}(\Signal{dp_t})}}_{b_r^{(k)}} - \underbrace{\frac{\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(\Signal{dp_t},
\sum_j \rho^{j-1}\Delta d_{t+j}\right)}{\operatorname{Var}(\Signal{dp_t})}}_{b_d^{(k)}} + \rho^k
\underbrace{\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\Signal{dp_t}, \Signal{dp_{t+k}})}{\operatorname{Var}(\Signal{dp_t})}}_{b_{dp}^{(k)}}.
$$

Each ratio is the slope of a regression on $\Signal{dp_t}$, with an intercept. So the three slopes must add up
to 1. Read them as shares: $b_r^{(k)}$ is the share of the variance of $\Signal{dp}$ that future returns carry,
$-b_d^{(k)}$ the share of dividend growth, and $\rho^k b_{dp}^{(k)}$ the share of the bubble term.

### The long-run version with an AR(1)

You can also get the shares from one-year regressions. Regress $\Ret{r_{t+1}}$, $\Delta d_{t+1}$ and
$\Signal{dp_{t+1}}$ on $\Signal{dp_t}$, and call the three slopes $b_r$, $b_d$ and $\phi$. The last one is the
persistence of an AR(1), as in [Chapter 5](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/05-persistence-ar1-and-var/). Write
the one-year identity in $\Signal{dp}$ form, $\Ret{r_{t+1}} \approx \kappa - \rho\,\Signal{dp_{t+1}} + \Delta
d_{t+1} + \Signal{dp_t}$, and regress both sides on $\Signal{dp_t}$:

$$
b_r \approx -\rho\phi + b_d + 1, \qquad \text{so} \qquad 1 \approx b_r - b_d + \rho\phi.
$$

Now suppose $\Signal{dp}$ is an AR(1), so that $\mathbb{E}_t[\Signal{dp_{t+j-1}}] = \text{constant} +
\phi^{j-1}\Signal{dp_t}$. The forecast of the return in year $t+j$ is $b_r$ times that, so its slope on
$\Signal{dp_t}$ is $b_r\phi^{j-1}$. Sum over all years with weights $\rho^{j-1}$:

$$
b_r^{\text{lr}} = b_r\sum_{j \ge 1} (\rho\phi)^{j-1} = \frac{b_r}{1 - \rho\phi}, \qquad b_d^{\text{lr}} =
\frac{b_d}{1 - \rho\phi}.
$$

The bubble term has slope $\rho^k\phi^k$, which goes to zero. Divide the one-year identity $b_r - b_d \approx 1 -
\rho\phi$ by $1 - \rho\phi$, and you get the long-run identity

$$
1 \approx b_r^{\text{lr}} - b_d^{\text{lr}}.
$$

In words: over the long run, every bit of variation in $\Signal{dp}$ is either a forecast of returns or a forecast
of dividend growth.

### What the data say

On goyal-welch-annual, with $\Signal{dp}$ at the end of 1872 to 2024 and the following year's return, the one-year
slopes are $b_r = 0.024$, $b_d = -0.088$ and $\phi = 0.92$. The identity $b_r - b_d + \rho\phi$ comes to 1 up to
a gap of 0.003, which is the linearisation error. The long-run slopes are $b_r^{\text{lr}} = 0.21$ and
$b_d^{\text{lr}} = -0.76$. Over the full sample, most of the variation in the ratio forecast dividend growth: low
prices came before falling dividends.

Now start with $\Signal{dp}$ at the end of 1946. On the same data, the long-run slopes become
$b_r^{\text{lr}} = 1.11$ and $b_d^{\text{lr}} = 0.10$. After the war, the ratio forecast returns almost one for
one, and dividend growth hardly at all. Cochrane (2011, p. 1050) reports the same post-war pattern on CRSP data
for 1947–2009. He reads it to mean that the variation in the ratio comes from expected returns, not from
expected dividends or bubbles.

<Callout type="warning">These are point estimates. The library reports no standard errors for them, and the
long-run slopes divide by $1 - \rho\phi$, a small and imprecise number. The full sample and the post-war sample
give opposite answers. Neither is a precise measurement.</Callout>

### What the playground can and cannot show

Even at 20 years the bubble term does not vanish. In the full sample it still carries 27%. That is not proof of a
bubble. It means the ratio is so persistent that 20 years is not yet the long run: $\rho^{20}$ is still 0.48. The
AR(1) version above extends the sum to infinity, but only by assuming that the AR(1) is right.

The long sums also share most of their years. A 20-year sum that starts in 1950 and one that starts in 1951 have
19 years in common. As in Chapter 9, the data contain only a handful of separate 20-year windows. So the shares
move a lot when the sample changes, as the two samples show.

Finally, the identity cannot say *why* expected returns or dividends change. It only says that one of them must,
whenever the ratio moves. Explaining why is the job of the asset pricing models later in the course.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- The log return is exactly $\log(1 + e^{pd_{t+1}}) + \Delta d_{t+1} - pd_t$. Its tangent line at the mean,
  $\kappa + \rho\,pd_{t+1} + \Delta d_{t+1} - pd_t$, misses by 0.39% a year on average since 1873.
- Solved forward, the identity says that today's ratio equals discounted future dividend growth minus discounted
  future returns, plus a bubble term that must vanish if the ratio does not explode.
- So the variance of $dp$ splits into a return share, a dividend-growth share and a bubble share that add up to
  1, and in the long run $1 \approx b_r^{\text{lr}} - b_d^{\text{lr}}$.
- On goyal-welch-annual since 1872, dividend growth carries the larger share. After the war, returns carry most
  of it. These are point estimates from few independent decades.
