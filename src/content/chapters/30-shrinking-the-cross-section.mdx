## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> The best portfolio you could not have held

Take 30 well-known long–short portfolios: the 25 size and book-to-market portfolios of Fama and French, with the
factors SMB, HML, RMW, CMA and momentum. Remove the market from each one, so that only the part the market does
not explain is left. Now stand in December 2004 and use every month since July 1963 to build the tangency
portfolio of Chapter 7. In those months it earned a Sharpe ratio of 1.84 a year.

Then hold the same weights from January 2005 to August 2026. The Sharpe ratio falls to 0.82. Repeat the exercise
with 108 published anomaly portfolios, and the drop is bigger still: 8.85 a year before 2005, and 2.18 after.

So here is the question. Asset pricing wants the stochastic discount factor, and with many factors the SDF
coefficients are the tangency weights. If the estimate loses more than half its Sharpe ratio on new data, how
should you estimate an SDF on 30, or 108, factors at once? This chapter follows Kozak, Nagel & Santosh (2020). Their
answer is to *shrink* the estimate toward zero, and to shrink most where the data are least informative.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Where the noise hides

Every asset in this chapter is a zero-cost portfolio: long one group of stocks, short another. So each one is a
factor and a test asset at the same time. The SDF must price the very portfolios it is built from.

The naive estimate of the SDF coefficients is the tangency direction, $\hat b = \hat\Sigma^{-1}\bar\mu$. It uses
the sample means $\bar\mu$, and sample means are noisy. Chapter 6 showed a way to see where that noise does damage.
Rotate the 30 portfolios onto their principal components. The first three components carry 70.5% of the total
variance. The last ones carry almost none.

Now think about a component with almost no variance. Its sample mean is still noisy. Divide a noisy mean by a tiny
variance, and you get a huge Sharpe ratio and a huge weight. The naive estimate bets most heavily exactly where the
signal is weakest. Those bets look great in the months that chose them and then fail.

The widget below shows this. The solid line is the in-sample frontier: zero-cost portfolios can be scaled up or
down, so the best ones lie on a line from the origin whose slope is the highest Sharpe ratio before 2005. The dot is
the tangency portfolio in the months that chose it. The ring is the same portfolio after 2005. The slider adds a
penalty $\nu_2$ that pulls the weights toward zero.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> the slider starts at $\nu_2 = 0$, the naive estimate: 1.84 in sample
and 0.82 out of sample. Move it to $10^{-3}$. The in-sample Sharpe ratio falls to 1.64, which is the price of

So on these 30 portfolios, shrinkage does not rescue the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio. Their average returns after
2005 did not look like those before, and no estimate from the earlier months could know that.
On the 108 anomalies the result is different. The same penalty of $10^{-3}$ raises the out-of-sample Sharpe
ratio from 2.18 to 2.36. Shrinkage removes noise. It cannot create a premium that the later months do not have.

<Callout type="key">The naive SDF estimate puts its largest weights on the directions with the least variance,
where sample means are least reliable. Shrinkage pulls those weights back.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> A prior, a ridge and a lasso

### The SDF and its naive estimate

Let the $N$ factors be the zero-cost portfolios themselves, with mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[\Factor{f}]$ and
covariance $\Sigma$. The SDF is

$$
\SDF{M_{t+1}} = 1 - b'\Factor{\tilde f_{t+1}}, \qquad \Factor{\tilde f_{t+1}} = \Factor{f_{t+1}} - \mathbb{E}[\Factor{f}].
$$

It must price each factor, an excess return with price zero: $\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M_{t+1}}\Factor{f_{t+1}}] = 0$.
Put the SDF in:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 &= \mathbb{E}[\Factor{f}] - \mathbb{E}[\Factor{f}\Factor{\tilde f}']\,b \\
&= \mu - \Sigma b,
\end{aligned}
$$

because $\mathbb{E}[f\tilde f'] = \operatorname{Cov}(f, f') = \Sigma$ when $\tilde f$ has mean zero. So $b = \Sigma^{-1}\mu$:
the SDF coefficients are the tangency weights of Chapter 7. The naive estimate replaces $\mu$ with the sample mean
$\Data{\bar\mu}$ and $\Sigma$ with $\hat\Sigma$: $\hat b = \hat\Sigma^{-1}\Data{\bar\mu}$ (Kozak, Nagel & Santosh
2020, p. 276). From here on we treat $\Sigma$ as known. The paper argues that the main source of error is the
uncertainty about the means, so the noise that matters is in $\bar\mu$.

In the principal-component directions, $\Sigma = QLQ'$ and $\Sigma^{-1} = QL^{-1}Q'$. So the naive estimate is

$$
\hat b = \sum_{j=1}^{N} q_j\,\frac{q_j'\Data{\bar\mu}}{\ell_j}.
$$

The term $q_j'\bar\mu$ is the sample mean of component $j$. Its sampling error has variance $\ell_j/T$, so the
error in its coefficient has variance $1/(\ell_j T)$. A component with a small $\ell_j$ gets a coefficient that is
almost all noise. This is the overfitting from the Hook, in one formula.

### A prior on expected returns gives a ridge

Before you see the data, what do you believe about $\mu$? A natural belief is that a direction with little
variance should not have a large expected return. Otherwise it would offer a near-arbitrage, and investors would
trade it away. Kozak, Nagel & Santosh (2020, p. 277) write this belief as a normal prior:

$$
\mu \sim N\!\left(0,\ \frac{SR^2_{\text{prior}}}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}\,\Sigma^2\right).
$$

The scale $SR_{\text{prior}}$ has a plain reading. The expected squared highest Sharpe ratio under this prior is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[\mu'\Sigma^{-1}\mu] &= \operatorname{tr}\big(\Sigma^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}[\mu\mu']\big)
= \frac{SR^2_{\text{prior}}}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}\,\operatorname{tr}\big(\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma^2\big) \\
&= \frac{SR^2_{\text{prior}}}{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}\,\operatorname{tr}\Sigma = SR^2_{\text{prior}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

So $SR_{\text{prior}}$ is how high you expect the best Sharpe ratio to be. The power 2 on $\Sigma$ is a choice of
the paper. In the direction $q_j$, the prior variance of the mean is proportional to $\ell_j^2$, so the prior
Sharpe ratio of component $j$ is proportional to $\ell_j$. Low-variance directions are expected to earn little.

Combine the prior with the data. Given $\mu$, the sample mean is $\Data{\bar\mu} \sim N(\mu, \Sigma/T)$. The
posterior mean of $b = \Sigma^{-1}\mu$ is

$$
\hat b = (\Sigma + \nu_2 I)^{-1}\,\Data{\bar\mu}, \qquad
\nu_2 = \frac{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{SR^2_{\text{prior}}\,T}.
$$

<details>
<summary>The algebra: from the prior to the ridge</summary>

Write $c = SR^2_{\text{prior}}/\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$. The prior is $\mu \sim N(0, c\Sigma^2)$ and the data
say $\bar\mu \mid \mu \sim N(\mu, \Sigma/T)$. For two normal vectors like these, the posterior mean is the prior
variance times the inverse of the total variance, times the data:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\mu \mid \bar\mu] = c\Sigma^2\,\big(c\Sigma^2 + \Sigma/T\big)^{-1}\,\bar\mu.
$$

Factor $\Sigma$ out of the bracket: $c\Sigma^2 + \Sigma/T = \Sigma\,(c\Sigma + I/T)$, so its inverse is
$(c\Sigma + I/T)^{-1}\Sigma^{-1}$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[b \mid \bar\mu] &= \Sigma^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}[\mu \mid \bar\mu]
= c\,\Sigma\,(c\Sigma + I/T)^{-1}\,\Sigma^{-1}\bar\mu.
\end{aligned}
$$

$\Sigma$, $\Sigma^{-1}$ and $(c\Sigma + I/T)^{-1}$ all have the eigenvectors $Q$, so they commute. Move
$\Sigma^{-1}$ next to $\Sigma$, and they cancel:

$$
\mathbb{E}[b \mid \bar\mu] = c\,(c\Sigma + I/T)^{-1}\bar\mu = \Big(\Sigma + \frac{1}{cT}\,I\Big)^{-1}\bar\mu.
$$

Finally, $1/(cT) = \operatorname{tr}\Sigma/(SR^2_{\text{prior}}T) = \nu_2$.

</details>

This is a **ridge regression**: the same estimate solves a penalised problem (p. 278). Look for the $b$ that
minimises

$$
(\Data{\bar\mu} - \Sigma b)'\,\Sigma^{-1}\,(\Data{\bar\mu} - \Sigma b) + \nu_2\, b'b.
$$

The first term is the squared pricing error $\bar\mu - \Sigma b$, weighted by $\Sigma^{-1}$ as in the
Hansen–Jagannathan distance of Chapter 26. The second term is a penalty on large coefficients. Set the derivative
to zero:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-2\,\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}(\bar\mu - \Sigma b) + 2\nu_2 b &= 0 \\
\Longrightarrow\quad (\Sigma + \nu_2 I)\,b &= \bar\mu.
\end{aligned}
$$

A tighter prior (small $SR_{\text{prior}}$) means a larger $\nu_2$ and more shrinkage. More data (large $T$) means
less. Kozak, Nagel & Santosh write $\gamma$ for the penalty, $\kappa$ for the prior scale and $\tau$ for
$\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$. This course keeps $\gamma$ for risk aversion, $\kappa$ for a Campbell–Shiller constant and
$\tau$ for an option's life, so it writes $\nu_2$, $SR_{\text{prior}}$ and $\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$.

### What the ridge does to each component

Put $\Sigma + \nu_2 I = Q(L + \nu_2 I)Q'$ into the ridge estimate:

$$
q_j'\hat b = \frac{q_j'\Data{\bar\mu}}{\ell_j + \nu_2}
= \underbrace{\frac{\ell_j}{\ell_j + \nu_2}}_{\text{shrink factor}} \times \frac{q_j'\Data{\bar\mu}}{\ell_j}.
$$

Each component keeps its naive coefficient times a factor between 0 and 1 (p. 278). A component with
$\ell_j \gg \nu_2$ keeps almost all of it. A component with $\ell_j \ll \nu_2$ loses almost all of it. On the 108
anomalies, at the penalty that the next section picks, the factor is 0.835 for the first component, 0.383 for
the fifth, 0.216 for the tenth and 0.002 for the last. The ridge does not drop components. It fades them by
their variance.

### Sparsity: the elastic net

Ridge keeps every coefficient. If you want an SDF with only a few non-zero coefficients, add an $L^1$ penalty.
The **elastic net** of Kozak, Nagel & Santosh (2020, p. 279) minimises

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(\Data{\bar\mu} - \Sigma b)'\,\Sigma^{-1}\,(\Data{\bar\mu} - \Sigma b) \\
&\quad + \nu_2\, b'b + \nu_1 \textstyle\sum_i \lvert b_i\rvert.
\end{aligned}
$$

The absolute value has a kink at zero, so a coefficient whose evidence is weak stops at exactly zero. The
larger $\nu_1$, the fewer coefficients survive. The widget fixes the total penalty
$\nu_2 + \nu_1 = \operatorname{tr}\Sigma/(SR^2_{\text{prior}}T)$ and gives a share $s$ of it to the $L^1$ term,
so $\nu_1 = s(\nu_2 + \nu_1)$. This split is our device, not the paper's.

Sparsity depends on the space you work in. **Sparse in assets** means that a few of the original portfolios, and so
a few characteristics, make up the SDF. That is the idea behind a model with three or five factors. **Sparse in
PCs** means that a few principal components make up the SDF. Each component mixes all the portfolios, so a
PC-sparse SDF still uses every characteristic. The ridge gives the same fit in both spaces, because it treats all
directions alike up to rotation. The $L^1$ penalty does not, because "a coefficient is zero" depends on the axes.

### Choosing the penalty by cross-validation

How do you pick the penalty? Not by the in-sample fit, which always prefers no penalty. Split the months into
$K_{CV} = 3$ blocks of consecutive months. Estimate $\hat b$ on two blocks and score it on the third with the
**cross-validated $R^2$**:

$$
R^2_{CV} = 1 - \frac{\lVert \Data{\bar\mu_{\text{out}}} - \Sigma_{\text{out}}\hat b\rVert^2}{\lVert \Data{\bar\mu_{\text{out}}}\rVert^2},
$$

averaged over the three choices of the withheld block (p. 279). This is not the forecast $R^2_{OS}$ of
[Chapter 13](../13-out-of-sample-predictability/). It scores how well the SDF prices the cross-section of mean
returns in months it did not see. The benchmark is $\hat b = 0$, an SDF that prices nothing, which scores 0. Like
$R^2_{OS}$, it can be negative.

<Callout type="warning">The chosen penalty is itself an estimate. It comes from three blocks of one sample, and it
would move with a different split. Treat the best $R^2_{CV}$ as optimistic, not as a promise.</Callout>

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> read the ridge first. $R^2_{CV}$ peaks at 0.520, at a prior scale of
0.158 a month, or 0.55 a year. The largest coefficient there is short-term reversal. Slide the penalty to the

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this too:</strong> set the $L^1$ share to 0.9 and move the penalty one step right
of the ridge's best ($SR_{\text{prior}} = 0.112$). In the asset space, 20 coefficients are not zero and
$R^2_{CV}$ is 0.29. Switch to PCs. Now only 14 components are not zero, and $R^2_{CV}$ is 0.44.</p>

This is the main lesson of Kozak, Nagel & Santosh (2020, p. 271). An SDF built from a few characteristics
prices the anomalies poorly out of sample. An SDF built from a few principal components does much better, even
though each component holds every anomaly. On the 108 anomalies, the first five components carry 61.7% of the
variance, and the largest coefficients at the best penalty sit among the first 15 components, not only the first
few. In the asset space, with the $L^1$ share at 0.9, the best $R^2_{CV}$ is 0.485, but it needs 44 non-zero
coefficients.

Now switch to the 30 FF portfolios. $R^2_{CV}$ is negative at every penalty. It is −3.52 with the weakest
shrinkage and climbs to −0.006 with the strongest, at the edge of the grid. Shrinking all the way to $\hat b = 0$
wins. The problem is the one in the Hook: weights fitted on some decades price other decades badly, so the
safest SDF prices nothing. The paper's own result on the 25 size and book-to-market portfolios uses daily data
from July 1926 to December 2017 and finds the best $R^2_{CV}$ inside the grid, at a prior scale of about 0.23
(p. 281). On monthly data since 1963, with the market removed, we do not find that.

Last, test how stable the chosen penalty is. On the 108 anomalies with the ridge, three blocks pick
$SR_{\text{prior}} = 0.158$ with a best $R^2_{CV}$ of 0.52. Five blocks pick 0.079, with 0.21. Ten blocks pick
0.112, with 0.24. The shape is the same each time, a hump, but its peak moves, and so does its height.

<Callout type="key">With many factors, the naive SDF overfits through its low-variance directions. A prior that
expects little from those directions gives a ridge, which fades each component by its variance. On 108 anomalies a
handful of principal components prices the cross-section out of sample better than a handful of anomalies.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- With the factors as test assets, the linear SDF $\SDF{M_{t+1}} = 1 - b'\Factor{\tilde f_{t+1}}$ has
  $b = \Sigma^{-1}\mu$, the tangency weights. The naive $\hat\Sigma^{-1}\bar\mu$ bets most on the directions
  with the least variance, and overfits.
- The prior $\mu \sim N(0, (SR^2_{\text{prior}}/\operatorname{tr}\Sigma)\,\Sigma^2)$ turns the estimate into the ridge
  $\hat b = (\Sigma + \nu_2 I)^{-1}\bar\mu$, which shrinks component $j$ by $\ell_j/(\ell_j + \nu_2)$.
- The elastic net adds $\nu_1\sum_i\lvert b_i\rvert$ and sets weak coefficients to zero. Sparse in PCs is not
  sparse in assets.
- On 108 anomalies, $R^2_{CV}$ peaks at 0.52. On 30 FF portfolios since 1963 it never rises above zero. The
  chosen penalty moves with the number of blocks.
