## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> A factor made of noise

Take the 25 portfolios sorted on size and book-to-market, in Kenneth French's monthly data from 1963-07 to
2026-08: 758 months. Now invent a factor. Draw 758 random numbers from a standard normal distribution, one per
month, with a fixed seed. This factor has nothing to do with stock returns. Its true beta on every portfolio is
zero.

Run the two-pass regression of
[Chapter 17](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/17-cross-sectional-regressions-and-fama-macbeth/) with it, with an
intercept, and read the Fama–MacBeth $t$-statistic of its premium. Repeat with 200 fresh random factors. A test at
the 5% level should call about 5% of them priced. It calls 28.0% of them priced. The median cross-sectional $R^2$
is 0.070, and the market itself gets 0.078 on the same portfolios. In 46.5% of the draws, pure noise fits the 25
average returns better than the market does. The best draw reaches an $R^2$ of 0.625.

Gospodinov, Kan & Robotti (2019), GKR below, report the same trap in a sharper form. They add one simulated normal
factor to the CAPM on the same 25 portfolios, in monthly data from 1967 to 2012. The pseudo-$R^2$ of their CU-GMM estimate
jumps from 22.77% to 99.28% (table 1, pp. 2–3). So, **how can a factor unrelated to returns look priced, and
how do you tell it from a real one?**

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Dividing by noise

Recall what the second pass does. It regresses the 25 average excess returns on the 25 estimated betas, and calls
the slope $\hat\lambda$. The betas come from the first pass, so each one is an estimate with its own error, as
[Chapter 18](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/18-errors-in-variables-and-shanken/) showed.

For a useless factor, the true betas are all zero, so the estimated betas are *only* error. Each is the slope of a
portfolio's return on random numbers: small, but not zero. And portfolios that move together get similar slopes.
So the estimated betas line up with the big common movements of the 25 portfolios, the market, size and value,
which also line up in part with the average returns.

Now the second pass draws a line through 25 points whose horizontal positions are this patterned noise. The
betas are tiny, so the slope, the change in average return per unit of beta, is huge. The standard error does not
grow to match. And the $R^2$ only asks how well the points line up, which does not change when all the betas
shrink. The noise looks like a real factor's betas, so the $R^2$ looks like a real factor's too.

The widget runs the experiment. Each draw makes a factor $\Factor{f_t} = s\,m_t + e_t$: fresh noise $e_t$ plus
a strength $s$ times $m_t$, the standardised average return of the 25 portfolios. At $s = 0$ the factor is
useless. The top chart shows the $t$-statistics of the premium, the bottom chart the $R^2$.

<UselessFactor client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> start at strength 0. Under the Fama–MacBeth standard error, 28.0% of
the draws have $|t| > 1.96$. Switch to the Shanken $t$: only 7.5% do, because the Shanken correction grows with
$\hat\lambda^2$. Then drag the strength to 0.25. The factor now has real betas, and the false signals vanish: no
draw has $|t| > 1.96$ under either standard error, and the median $R^2$ falls to 0.009.</p>

At strength 0.25 the factor tracks the average portfolio, so its betas are close to 1 everywhere. Equal betas
cannot explain different average returns, and the regression now says so. The danger is a factor too weak to have
measurable betas: its noise does the fitting.

<Callout type="key">With a useless factor, the second pass divides by betas that are pure noise. The premium
estimate blows up, the Fama–MacBeth $t$-test calls it priced far too often, and the $R^2$ stays high.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Identification, the rank test and GRS-FAR

### The rank condition

Write the beta model with a zero-beta rate $\lambda_0$ as in Chapter 17,
$\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_i}] = \lambda_0 + \beta_i'\lambda$ for $i = 1, \dots, N$, with $K$ factors. Stack the assets.
The $N$ means are $\mathbb{E}[R^e] = \mathbf{1}\lambda_0 + B\lambda$, where row $i$ of the $N \times K$ matrix $B$
is $\beta_i'$. The data pin down $\mathbb{E}[R^e]$ and $B$. They pin down $(\lambda_0, \lambda)$ only if no other
pair gives the same means. That holds when the $N \times (K + 1)$ matrix $(\mathbf{1}, B)$ has full column rank
$K + 1$.

In the SDF form of Part VI, $M_t = 1 - b'\tilde f_t$ with $\tilde f_t = f_t - \bar f$, the moments are
$\mathbb{E}[R^e] = \mathbb{E}[R^e\tilde f']\,b$. Chapter 26, *GMM for asset pricing models*, estimates $b$ from
them. There, $b$ is identified when

$$
\operatorname{rank}\big(\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e}\Factor{\tilde f}']\big) = K .
$$

The matrix $\mathbb{E}[R^e\tilde f']$ is $\operatorname{Cov}(R^e, f') = B\,\Sigma_f$, and $\Sigma_f$ is invertible.
So the condition is $\operatorname{rank}(B) = K$: every factor must move some combination of the assets. With one
factor, it says that not every beta is zero. With a zero-beta rate, the condition is stricter: the betas must also
differ across assets. A factor whose betas are all equal looks just like the constant.

A useless factor breaks both versions, because $B = 0$. A **weak** factor has betas that are small next to their
sampling error: the condition holds in theory and fails in practice.

### Why $\hat\lambda$ does not converge and $R^2$ does not vanish

Take one useless factor, independent of the returns, with variance $\sigma_f^2$. Its first-pass slope for asset $i$
is $\hat\beta_i = \sum_t \tilde f_t (R^e_{i,t} - \bar R^e_i) / \sum_t \tilde f_t^2$. The true slope is 0, so by
the central limit theorem

$$
\sqrt{T}\,\hat\beta \;\to\; z \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; \Sigma/\sigma_f^2\right),
$$

where $\Sigma$ is the covariance matrix of the $N$ returns. So $\hat\beta \approx z/\sqrt{T}$: a random vector
that shrinks with $T$, with a fixed random direction $z$. The covariance $\Sigma$ of the 25 portfolios is dominated
by a few common factors, so $z$ mostly points along them. That is the patterned noise of the Intuition.

The second pass with an intercept is an OLS slope across assets. Write $\bar z$ and $\bar\mu$ for the averages
across assets of $z_i$ and of the means $\mu_i = \mathbb{E}[R^e_i]$, and use $\bar R^e_i \to \mu_i$:

1. The slope is
   $\hat\lambda = \sum_i(\hat\beta_i - \bar{\hat\beta})(\bar R^e_i - \bar{\bar R})\big/\sum_i(\hat\beta_i - \bar{\hat\beta})^2$.
2. Put $\hat\beta_i = z_i/\sqrt{T}$. The numerator carries one factor $1/\sqrt{T}$ and the denominator two, so
   $\hat\lambda \approx \sqrt{T}\,\sum_i(z_i - \bar z)(\mu_i - \bar\mu)\big/\sum_i(z_i - \bar z)^2$.
3. The ratio is a random number that does not shrink, unless the means $\mu_i$ are all equal. So
   $\hat\lambda$ grows like $\sqrt{T}$, in a random direction. It does not converge to anything.
4. The $R^2$ is the squared cross-sectional correlation of the betas and the means,
   $R^2 = \big[\sum_i(z_i - \bar z)(\mu_i - \bar\mu)\big]^2 \big/ \big[\sum_i(z_i - \bar z)^2 \sum_i(\mu_i - \bar\mu)^2\big]$.
   The factors $\sqrt{T}$ cancel. The $R^2$ tends to a random number between 0 and 1, not to 0.

GKR state this $\sqrt T$ divergence for two-pass estimates on a spurious factor in a misspecified model, citing
earlier work (fn. 13, p. 12, working-paper pages). For the invariant ML and CU-GMM
estimators they prove more: the $R^2$ tends to 1 (theorem 1, p. 11).

The $t$-statistic follows from the same scaling. Each monthly slope $\hat\lambda_t$ divides one month's returns by
betas of size $1/\sqrt{T}$, so it has size $\sqrt{T}$. The Fama–MacBeth standard error, its standard deviation
over $\sqrt{T}$, stays of order one. So $t_{FM}$ grows like $\sqrt{T}$, and rejections become more frequent. The Shanken factor $\zeta = \hat\lambda^2/\hat\sigma_f^2$ grows like
$T$, so the Shanken standard error grows like $\sqrt{T}$ too. That keeps the Shanken $t$ of order one, but not
standard normal, which is why it rejected 7.5% and not 5% in the widget.

### The rank test

So test the rank condition before you trust $\hat\lambda$. With one factor and a zero-beta rate, Kleibergen & Zhan
(2020), KZ below, remove $\lambda_0$ by a difference. Subtract the last asset from each of the others:
$\check R_t = (R^e_{1,t} - R^e_{N,t}, \dots, R^e_{N-1,t} - R^e_{N,t})'$, an $(N - 1)$-vector. Its betas are
$\check\beta = (\beta_1 - \beta_N, \dots, \beta_{N-1} - \beta_N)'$, and its means are
$\mathbb{E}[\check R_t] = \check\beta\lambda$. The constant is gone, and identification is now $\check\beta \neq 0$:
not all betas equal. KZ show that the choice of the last asset does not matter (p. 518).

Regress $\check R_t$ on a constant and $f_t$. Call the slopes $\hat{\check\beta}$, the residual covariance
$\hat\Sigma_\varepsilon$ and the factor variance $\hat\Sigma_f$, both with divisor $T$. The test of
$H_0\colon \check\beta = 0$ is the joint test that all $N - 1$ slopes are zero (KZ, eqs. 17–19, pp. 518–519):

$$
F = \frac{T - N}{N - 1}\;\hat\Sigma_f\;\hat{\check\beta}{}'\,\hat\Sigma_\varepsilon^{-1}\hat{\check\beta}
\;\sim\; F(N - 1,\; T - N) \quad\text{under } H_0 .
$$

It is exact with normal residuals and a fixed factor, and it has the shape of the GRS test
of [Chapter 16](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/16-time-series-tests-and-grs/), with slopes for intercepts.

On the quarterly 25 portfolios from 1963-Q3 to 2026-Q2, consumption growth $\Delta c_{t+1}$ gives
$F(24, 227) = 1.61$, with a p-value of 0.039. The test rejects equal betas at 5%, but not at 1%. The market factor
gives $F = 9.27$ and a p-value of $1.8 \times 10^{-22}$: its betas are clearly not all equal.

### The GRS-FAR test at a hypothesised $\lambda^{\circ}$

The rank test says whether $\lambda$ is identified, not where it is. For that, test one candidate value at a time:
a hypothesised premium $\lambda^{\circ}$ and the null $H_0\colon \lambda = \lambda^{\circ}$.

1. The time-series regression is $\check R_t = a + \check\beta f_t + u_t$. Its sample means give
   $\bar{\check R} = \hat a + \hat{\check\beta}\bar f$.
2. Under $H_0$, $\mathbb{E}[\check R_t] = \check\beta\lambda^{\circ}$. Write the regression as
   $\check R_t = \alpha(\lambda^{\circ}) + \check\beta(f_t - \bar f + \lambda^{\circ}) + u_t$. Then $H_0$ says
   $\alpha(\lambda^{\circ}) = 0$ (KZ, eqs. 21–22, p. 526). The shifted factor $f_t - \bar f + \lambda^{\circ}$ acts
   like a traded return with mean $\lambda^{\circ}$, and the null is a set of zero intercepts.
3. The estimate of $\alpha(\lambda^{\circ})$ is the pricing error $\Err{\bar{\check R} - \hat{\check\beta}\lambda^{\circ}}$.
   Under $H_0$, with a fixed factor, $\bar{\check R} = \check\beta\lambda^{\circ} + \bar u$ and
   $\hat{\check\beta} = \check\beta + \sum_t \tilde f_t u_t/(T\hat\Sigma_f)$. Subtract:
   $\bar{\check R} - \hat{\check\beta}\lambda^{\circ} = \bar u - \lambda^{\circ}\sum_t \tilde f_t u_t/(T\hat\Sigma_f)$.
4. The true $\check\beta$ has cancelled. The two terms are uncorrelated, because $\sum_t \tilde f_t = 0$. Their
   variances are $\Sigma_\varepsilon/T$ and $\lambda^{\circ 2}\Sigma_\varepsilon/(T\hat\Sigma_f)$. So the pricing
   error has covariance $\Sigma_\varepsilon(1 + \lambda^{\circ 2}/\hat\Sigma_f)/T$, whatever $\check\beta$ is.
5. Scale by that covariance, as GRS does:

$$
\text{GRS-FAR}(\lambda^{\circ}) = \frac{T\,\Err{(\bar{\check R} - \hat{\check\beta}\lambda^{\circ})}'\,\hat\Sigma_\varepsilon^{-1}\,\Err{(\bar{\check R} - \hat{\check\beta}\lambda^{\circ})}}{1 + \lambda^{\circ\prime}\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\lambda^{\circ}},
\qquad
\frac{T - K - N + 1}{T(N - 1)}\,\text{GRS-FAR} \sim F(N - 1,\; T - K - N + 1).
$$

This is the second of the two equal forms of KZ's eq. (23), and eq. (25) gives its exact $F$ distribution
(pp. 527–528). The denominator is the $1 + SR^2_f$ of GRS, for a factor whose mean is $\lambda^{\circ}$. Step 4 is
the point: the distribution of the statistic does not depend on $\check\beta$. The test keeps its size when the
betas are small, and even when they are zero.

### Inverting the test into a confidence set

The 95% GRS-FAR set collects every $\lambda^{\circ}$ that the test does not reject at 5% (KZ, eq. 27, p. 528):

$$
\mathrm{CS}_{95\%} = \big\{\lambda^{\circ} : \text{the p-value of GRS-FAR}(\lambda^{\circ}) \ge 0.05\big\}.
$$

It contains the true $\lambda$ whenever the test at the true $\lambda$ does not reject, so 95% of the time, for any
$\check\beta$. Now let $\lambda^{\circ}$ grow without bound. In the numerator, $\hat{\check\beta}\lambda^{\circ}$
dominates, so the numerator is close to $T\lambda^{\circ 2}\,\hat{\check\beta}{}'\hat\Sigma_\varepsilon^{-1}\hat{\check\beta}$.
The denominator is close to $\lambda^{\circ 2}/\hat\Sigma_f$. The $\lambda^{\circ 2}$ cancels:

$$
\text{GRS-FAR}(\lambda^{\circ}) \;\to\; T\,\hat\Sigma_f\,\hat{\check\beta}{}'\hat\Sigma_\varepsilon^{-1}\hat{\check\beta}
\quad\text{as } \lambda^{\circ} \to \pm\infty .
$$

With $K = 1$, the $F$ scaling $\tfrac{T - N}{T(N - 1)}$ turns this into the rank-test $F$ above, with the same
degrees of freedom (KZ, fn. 13, p. 528). So the far ends of the line get the rank test's p-value. If the rank test
does not reject at 5%, very large premia are accepted, and the set is **unbounded**: a whole line, a ray, or two
rays. KZ read an unbounded set as a sign of small betas (p. 528). If the model is rejected at every
$\lambda^{\circ}$, the set is **empty**. For the market on the quarterly 25 portfolios, it is empty.

The Wald set $\hat\lambda \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{se}$ is always bounded, so it can never give the right answer for
weak betas: "almost anything". Its normal approximation fails when the betas are zero or close to it (KZ,
pp. 515–516).

The GRS-FAR coverage is the same at every spread. This is step 4 at work: at the true $\lambda$ the statistic does
not depend on the betas, and one seed gives the same residuals. As the spread shrinks, the Wald set stays narrow
and becomes wrong. The GRS-FAR set becomes wide and stays right.

### What our data show

Now switch the widget to consumption growth: the 25 portfolios in quarters and $\Delta c_{t+1}$ from our $cay$
data, as in [Chapter 22](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/22-conditional-factor-models/), over 252 quarters from
1963-Q3 to 2026-Q2. The two-pass estimate is $\hat\lambda = 0.89\%$ a quarter. The 95% Wald sets are $[0.14\%, 1.63\%]$ with the
Fama–MacBeth standard error and $[-0.14\%, 1.91\%]$ with the Shanken one.

The 95% GRS-FAR set is $[0.6\%, 43.2\%]$ a quarter. It is bounded, because the rank test rejects at 5%. It
excludes zero, so the data do say that the premium on consumption risk is positive. But it is 29 times as wide as
the Fama–MacBeth interval, and a premium of 43% a quarter on consumption growth is not an economic answer. At 99%,
the rank test no longer rejects, and the set is unbounded: everything below $-8.6\%$ or above $0.5\%$.

So, plainly: consumption betas on these portfolios differ, but only just. The data cannot say how large the
premium on consumption risk is. The narrow Wald interval treats weak betas as strong ones.

Over Lettau and Ludvigson's 141 quarters, 1963-Q3 to 1998-Q3, the verdict is weaker still. The rank test gives a
p-value of 0.120, and the 95% GRS-FAR set is unbounded: everything below $-0.6\%$ or above $0.7\%$ a quarter. KZ
find unbounded sets over the same quarters for the scaled consumption model of Chapter 22, whose rank test has a
p-value of 0.274 (table V, p. 535).

Nagel (2013) adds a broader warning: the 25 portfolios have a strong factor structure, so a high $R^2$ on them
means little (pp. 19–20, working-paper pages). A proposed factor fits them as long as its correlation with their
common factors is not exactly zero (fn. 9, p. 19).

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- Identification needs $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbb{E}[R^e\tilde f']) = K$, and with a zero-beta rate the betas
  must also differ. A useless factor breaks this; a weak one nearly does.
- A useless factor's betas are noise of size $1/\sqrt{T}$. So $\hat\lambda$ grows like $\sqrt{T}$, the
  Fama–MacBeth $t$ rejects far too often, and the $R^2$ stays high.
- Test the rank condition first. GRS-FAR then tests one $\lambda^{\circ}$ at a time, with a distribution free of
  the betas. Its set keeps 95% coverage and is unbounded when the rank test does not reject.
- Consumption growth on the quarterly 25 portfolios passes the rank test at 5% but not at 1%. Its 95% GRS-FAR set,
  0.6% to 43.2% a quarter, says the premium is positive and little more.
