## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Do households know something about returns?

The dividend–price ratio of [Chapter 9](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/09-time-varying-expected-returns/) is
slow. It says little about next year's return: on a century of annual data, it explains 1.2% of the variance of
the one-year excess return. It says much more about the next ten years. But expected returns may also move with the
business cycle, over a few quarters. Is there a signal for that?

Lettau & Ludvigson (2001) proposed one. It comes from data that has nothing to do with stock prices: what
households spend, what they own and what they earn. The idea is that people like a smooth path of spending. So
the way they spend today tells you what they expect their wealth to earn.

We rebuilt their signal, called $\Signal{cay}$, from public data. On quarterly data from 1952 to 1998, the
sample of their paper, $\Signal{cay}$ explains 7.6% of the variance of next quarter's excess stock return. The
Newey–West $t$-statistic is 3.96. That is a lot for one quarter. Now use all our data, from 1952-Q1 to 2026-Q2,
and estimate again. The share falls to 0.07%, with a $t$-statistic of 0.38.

So you probably have two questions. Why should spending forecast returns? And why does the evidence look so
different with more data? This chapter answers both.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Two trends on one leash

Think of a person who walks a dog in a big park. Both wander wherever they like, and after an hour either one
can be far from the gate. But the leash keeps the dog close to the person. Their positions drift; the distance
between them does not.

Economists call two series like these **cointegrated**. Each one wanders like a random walk: a shock to it lasts
forever, so it has no fixed level to return to. But one weighted difference of the two is **stationary**, the
property from [Chapter 5](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/05-persistence-ar1-and-var/): its mean and variance are
the same at every date, so it keeps coming back.

The widget makes this concrete. It draws a random walk $x_t$ and a second series $y_t = 0.6\,x_t + u_t$, where
the gap $u_t$ dies out quickly. In this demo only, $x_t$ and $y_t$ are two made-up series. From the
Formalisation on, $y_t$ is labour income. The bottom panel shows the combination $\Signal{y_t - \theta\,x_t}$ for the
weight $\theta$ that you choose. Its **persistence** is the slope of an AR(1) fitted to it: near 1, it trends; well
below 1, it keeps returning to its mean.

Only one weight removes the trend. Any other weight leaves a part of the random walk in the combination, and a
random walk, however small its share, wanders off in the end. That is why the right weight is easy to find in a
long sample: the wrong ones fail in a way that grows with time.

Now replace the walker and the dog with three household series: log consumption $c_t$, log asset wealth $n_t$
(stocks, bonds, houses, minus debts: the households' net worth) and log labour income $y_t$. The paper writes
asset wealth as $a_t$. We use $n_t$, because $a$ is the intercept of a predictive regression; the name $cay$ keeps
the paper's letters. Each series grows over the decades. But consumption cannot drift far from what wealth and
income can pay for. The gap between consumption and a weighted sum of the other two is $\Signal{cay_t}$.

Why should that gap forecast returns? Suppose households start to expect high returns for the next few years.
Prices fall today, so their wealth looks low. But they know that the high returns will rebuild it. So they cut
their spending by less than their wealth fell. Consumption sits *above* its usual relation with wealth and
income, and $\Signal{cay_t}$ is high. A high $\Signal{cay_t}$ then comes before high returns.

<Callout type="key">Consumption, wealth and income share one long-run trend. When households expect high
returns, consumption runs above that trend, so the gap $\Signal{cay_t}$ is a measure of expected returns.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> From the budget constraint to $cay$

### The budget constraint

Let $W_t$ be total wealth at the start of period $t$: asset wealth plus **human capital**, the value today of all
future labour income. Households consume $C_t$ and invest the rest, which earns the return $\Ret{R_{w,t+1}}$ on
total wealth. So wealth grows as Lettau & Ludvigson (2001, eq. 1, p. 818) write it:

$$
W_{t+1} = (1 + \Ret{R_{w,t+1}})(W_t - C_t).
$$

This is an accounting identity. It needs no model of how people choose $C_t$.

### Log-linearising it

Divide both sides by $W_t$:

$$
\frac{W_{t+1}}{W_t} = (1 + \Ret{R_{w,t+1}})\left(1 - \frac{C_t}{W_t}\right).
$$

Take logs, with lower-case letters for logs and $\Ret{r_{w,t+1}} = \log(1 + \Ret{R_{w,t+1}})$. So in this
chapter $w_t = \log W_t$ is log total wealth, not a portfolio weight. Then $C_t/W_t = e^{c_t - w_t}$, and

$$
\Delta w_{t+1} = \Ret{r_{w,t+1}} + \log\!\left(1 - e^{c_t - w_t}\right).
$$

The last term is not linear in $c_t - w_t$. As in
[Chapter 10](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/10-campbell-shiller-decomposition/), replace it by its tangent line
at the mean $\bar x$ of $c_t - w_t$. Write $f(x) = \log(1 - e^x)$. Its slope is
$f'(x) = -e^x/(1 - e^x)$. Define

$$
\rho_w = 1 - e^{\bar x},
$$

the usual share of wealth that is not consumed, $(W - C)/W$. Then $e^{\bar x} = 1 - \rho_w$ and the slope at the
mean is $f'(\bar x) = -(1 - \rho_w)/\rho_w = 1 - 1/\rho_w$. The tangent line gives Lettau & Ludvigson's eq. (2):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta w_{t+1} \approx{} &\text{const} + \Ret{r_{w,t+1}} \\
&+ \left(1 - \frac{1}{\rho_w}\right)(c_t - w_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

From here on we drop the constants, as the paper does.

### Solving forward

Wealth growth is consumption growth minus the change in the ratio $c - w$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta w_{t+1} = {} &\Delta c_{t+1} \\
&- (c_{t+1} - w_{t+1}) + (c_t - w_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

Put this on the left of the linearised constraint. The term $c_t - w_t$ appears on both sides and cancels,
which leaves $-(1/\rho_w)(c_t - w_t)$ on the right. Move it to the left and multiply by $\rho_w$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_t - w_t = {} &\rho_w\left(\Ret{r_{w,t+1}} - \Delta c_{t+1}\right) \\
&+ \rho_w\,(c_{t+1} - w_{t+1}).
\end{aligned}
$$

The same equation one period later replaces the last term by
$\rho_w(\Ret{r_{w,t+2}} - \Delta c_{t+2}) + \rho_w(c_{t+2} - w_{t+2})$. Repeat this $J$ times. If
$\rho_w^J(c_{t+J} - w_{t+J})$ goes to zero, which rules out a ratio that explodes:

$$
c_t - w_t = \sum_{j \ge 1} \rho_w^{\,j}\left(\Ret{r_{w,t+j}} - \Delta c_{t+j}\right).
$$

This holds for the values that happen. The left side is known at $t$, so it also equals its expectation:

$$
c_t - w_t = \mathbb{E}_t \sum_{j \ge 1} \rho_w^{\,j}\left(\Ret{r_{w,t+j}} - \Delta c_{t+j}\right).
$$

Compare it with the dividend–price identity of Chapter 10. Consumption plays the role of the dividend that
total wealth pays (Lettau & Ludvigson (2001, p. 821)). If $c_t - w_t$ moves, it must forecast returns on wealth,
consumption growth, or both.

### From total wealth to things we can measure

Human capital is not observed. The paper uses three approximations (pp. 819–820). The symbol $\omega$ is the
average share of assets in total wealth:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_t &\approx \omega\,n_t + (1 - \omega)\,h_t, \\
\Ret{r_{w,t}} &\approx \omega\,\Ret{r_{n,t}} + (1 - \omega)\,\Ret{r_{h,t}}, \\
h_t &= \text{const} + y_t + z_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

The third one says that human capital $h_t$ moves with labour income in the long run, up to a stationary gap
$z_t$. Put the first and third into the left side of the forward solution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_t - w_t \approx{} &c_t - \omega\,n_t - (1 - \omega)\,y_t \\
&- (1 - \omega)\,z_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

Put the second into the right side, and move $(1 - \omega)z_t$ across. This is eq. (9) of the paper:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&c_t - \omega\,n_t - (1 - \omega)\,y_t \\
&\quad = \mathbb{E}_t \sum_{j \ge 1} \rho_w^{\,j}
\Big[\omega\,\Ret{r_{n,t+j}} + (1 - \omega)\,\Ret{r_{h,t+j}} \\
&\qquad\qquad - \Delta c_{t+j}\Big] + (1 - \omega)\,z_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Why the left side is stationary

Each of $c_t$, $n_t$ and $y_t$ has a trend that never dies out. The right side holds only returns, growth rates
and the gap $z_t$. None of them trends, so the right side is stationary. Then the left side must be stationary
too. So $c$, $n$ and $y$ are cointegrated, with the weights $(1, -\omega, -(1-\omega))$. The left side is
$\Signal{cay_t}$.

The equation also says when $\Signal{cay_t}$ measures expected *stock* returns. It does so as long as expected
returns on human capital and expected consumption growth do not move much (pp. 820–821). One detail changes the
weights. Measured consumption covers only nondurables and services, a part of the total. So the paper lets the two
weights be free, as $c_t - \theta_n n_t - \theta_y y_t$, and they need not add up to one (p. 822).

### Estimating the weights: dynamic OLS

The regressors $n_t$ and $y_t$ are not independent of the error, so ordinary OLS looks unsafe. With cointegration,
it is not. A wrong weight leaves a trend in the residual, so its sum of squares grows much faster with $T$ than at
the right weight. The widget showed this. The estimate converges at the rate $T$, not $\sqrt T$: it is
**superconsistent** (p. 823).

In a finite sample, the correlation between the error and the shocks to $n$ and $y$ still biases the estimate.
**Dynamic OLS** removes it. It adds the changes of the regressors before, at and after $t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_t = {} &\text{const} + \theta_n\,n_t + \theta_y\,y_t \\
&+ \sum_{j=-k}^{k}\left(d_{n,j}\,\Delta n_{t+j} + d_{y,j}\,\Delta y_{t+j}\right) \\
&+ \Err{\varepsilon_t}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The changes take up the part of the error that moves with the shocks to $n$ and $y$. What is left is unrelated to
them, so the bias in $\hat\theta_n$ and $\hat\theta_y$ becomes much smaller. Lettau & Ludvigson (2001, p. 822) take this method
from Stock & Watson (1993). They use $k = 8$ quarters (p. 823, fn. 8), and so do we. The standard errors are
Newey–West, as in [Chapter 4](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/04-autocorrelation-and-newey-west/), because the
error is autocorrelated. Then $\Signal{cay_t} = c_t - \hat\theta_n n_t - \hat\theta_y y_t - \text{const}$; the
changes are dropped.

On 1952-Q4 to 1998-Q3, the paper reports $\hat\theta_n = 0.31$ and $\hat\theta_y = 0.59$ (eq. 12, p. 823). On the
same quarters, our public data give $\hat\theta_n = 0.39$ (standard error 0.034) and $\hat\theta_y = 0.61$ (0.032).
On all our data, 1952-Q1 to 2026-Q2, they give $\hat\theta_n = -0.03$ (0.053) and $\hat\theta_y = 0.98$ (0.058).
With the later data, the weight on wealth goes to zero.

### The predictive regression

The test is the predictive regression of Chapter 9, with $\Signal{cay_t}$ as the predictor:

$$
\Ret{r^e_{t \to t+k}} = a + b\,\Signal{cay_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+k}}.
$$

The paper finds the most power at one to five quarters (p. 816). On 1952 to 1998, our quarterly $\Signal{cay_t}$
has a slope of 1.14 for next quarter's log excess return. Its standard deviation is 0.019, so a move of one
standard deviation changes the expected return of the next quarter by 2.2 percentage points. The paper reports a
similar size, about 2.2 points for the real return (p. 829).

$\Signal{cay}$ is also much less persistent than $\Signal{dp}$. On annual data from 1945 to 2025, the published
$\Signal{cay}$ has an AR(1) persistence of 0.73, and $\Signal{dp}$ has 0.95 on the same years. So $\Signal{cay}$
moves at the speed of the business cycle. The widget below is the scatter of Chapter 9, with the published annual
$\Signal{cay}$ in place of $\Signal{dp}$.

### Two honest limits

First, **look-ahead**. The full-sample weights use every quarter, including the quarters after $t$. So
$\Signal{cay_t}$ in 1975 uses data that nobody had in 1975. The paper names this problem (p. 831). The cure is the
real-time idea of [Chapter 13](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/13-out-of-sample-predictability/): at each date,
estimate the weights with the data up to that date only. The paper does this from a first window that ends in
1968-Q1 (p. 833). It finds that $\Signal{cay}$ still beats its benchmarks, but by less than with fixed full-sample
weights (p. 833).

Second, **our $cay$ is not the published one**. Our consumption keeps shoes and clothing, which the paper
removes. Our labour income is before tax, while the paper subtracts a labour share of taxes. Our wealth series is
not seasonally adjusted. Our sample runs to 2026, through the pandemic. The widget above uses the published annual
series, and the next one uses ours. Their numbers differ for these reasons.

The real-time line does not change when you switch, because each of its dates uses only the past. Only the
dashed line moves. That is the clearest picture of look-ahead: a full-sample signal changes its own history when
new data arrive.

The second lesson is about the weights. Superconsistency assumes one fixed relation between consumption, wealth
and income. If the relation itself shifts, more data does not pin it down. On our data, the weight on wealth falls
as the sample grows. So the evidence can and cannot show two things. It shows that $\Signal{cay}$ with full-sample
weights forecast quarterly returns well on 1952 to 1998. It cannot show that an investor with real-time weights
would have seen that forecast, on our public version of the data.

<Callout type="warning">A predictor built from estimated weights carries the future in its weights. Before you
trust its forecasts, rebuild it in real time, and check that the weights stay stable.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- The budget constraint, log-linearised and solved forward, gives
  $c_t - w_t = \mathbb{E}_t \sum_{j \ge 1} \rho_w^j (\Ret{r_{w,t+j}} - \Delta c_{t+j})$: consumption is the
  dividend of total wealth.
- With human capital tied to labour income, $c_t - \omega n_t - (1-\omega) y_t$ is stationary, so $c$, $n$ and $y$
  are cointegrated. The gap is $\Signal{cay_t}$.
- Dynamic OLS estimates the weights with leads and lags of the changes. On 1952 to 1998 our weights are 0.39 and
  0.61; on all our data the weight on wealth is −0.03.
- On the paper's sample, full-sample $\Signal{cay}$ forecasts next quarter's return well. Real-time weights and later
  data weaken the evidence a lot.
