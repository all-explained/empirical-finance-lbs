- An AR(1) series keeps the share $\phi$ of its last value. Its first-order autocorrelation is $\phi$
  ([Chapter 5](../05-persistence-ar1-and-var/)).
- With power utility and lognormal consumption and returns, the market's Euler equation gives
  $\mathbb{E}[\Ret{r_{t+1}} - \Rf{r^f_t}] + \tfrac12\sigma_i^2 = \gamma\,\sigma_{ic}$, with $i$ the market. Here
  $\sigma_{ic}$ is the covariance of the market's log return with consumption growth $\Delta c_{t+1}$. So the
  risk aversion $\gamma$ that prices the equity premium is the premium, plus half the variance, over that
  covariance. Chapter 35, *The equity premium and risk-free rate puzzles*, builds this formula.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Is the puzzle in the ruler?

Take US consumption of nondurable goods and services per person, after inflation, from the national accounts.
From 1931 to 2025 its growth has a volatility of 2.08% a year. Its correlation with the real return of the
stock market is 0.03. Put those numbers into the Euler equation above, and the risk aversion that prices the
equity premium is 849. A person with that risk aversion would refuse almost any gamble. Nobody is like that.

Now look at a different ruler. Kroencke (2017) reports that Savov (2011) used garbage as a measure of
consumption, and that it prices the equity premium far better (working paper p. 1; page numbers here are
those of his 2013 working paper). The idea is simple: households that consume more throw more away. Kroencke
reports that, after the war, garbage growth has a correlation of 0.58 with stock returns. The official series
has 0.01 over his postwar sample. Garbage is also more than twice as volatile (p. 16).
Both series try to measure the same thing: how much American households consume.

So here is the question. **Is consumption really this smooth and this deaf to the stock market, or does the
way we measure it make it so?** This chapter shows two ways the measurement smooths consumption. It then
undoes them on our data and asks how much of the puzzle goes away.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> A flow, smoothed twice

The consumption-based model wants consumption at a point in time: the consumption of the investor on the day the
return is paid. The national accounts give something else in two ways.

First, they report a **flow**: the total spending over a quarter or a year. A yearly number is an average of
the days inside the year. Averages move more slowly than the thing they average.

Second, the accounts are **built**, not observed. The statisticians combine many surveys of different
frequencies. They benchmark these to large censuses every few years and fill the gaps between them. Their
goal is the best estimate of the *level* of consumption. When a noisy new reading comes in, a careful
estimator moves only part of the way toward it. So the published series reacts to news slowly, over more than
one period.

Start with the first effect. In the widget below, log consumption follows a random walk, one step a day. Its
growth from one day to the next is pure news, with no memory. The thick dashes show the average over each
quarter of 90 days.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> set the steps a period to 1. Then the average is the level

With the default seed and 90 steps, the sample autocorrelation over 399 quarters is 0.288 and the exact value
is 0.250. The variance of the averaged growth is 0.610 of that of the point-to-point growth, and the exact
ratio is 0.667. No shock in the walk has any memory. The memory comes from the averaging alone.

Why? Two neighbouring quarterly averages share news. A shock on the last day of a quarter barely moves that
quarter's average. But it lifts every day of the next quarter, so most of its effect shows up one quarter
later. One shock lands in two growth rates, so neighbouring growth rates move together. That shared news is
also spread out, so each growth rate is less volatile than the true change.

The second effect does the same thing more strongly. An estimator that moves part of the way each period
spreads a shock over many periods. This makes measured growth smoother. It also makes it look less related to
returns: a return moves today, but measured consumption takes years to catch up.

<Callout type="key">Measured consumption is an average over a period, built by a careful estimator. Both steps
smooth it: less volatility, a positive autocorrelation, and a smaller covariance with returns. A smaller
covariance means a larger implied risk aversion.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Averaging, filtering, and the way back

### What the national accounts measure

Our consumption is personal consumption expenditure on nondurable goods (food, clothing, energy) and on
services (housing, health care, transport and so on). Each part is deflated by its own price index, then the
two are added and divided by the population. Durable goods, such as cars and furniture, are left out: a household buys them rarely but uses them for
years, so their purchases are closer to investment than to consumption.

What the series misses matters as much. It is a flow over the year, not consumption on a date. It is an
estimate, built from many sources with interpolation between benchmarks (Kroencke, working paper pp. 3–4).
And it is an average over all households, not just the ones who hold stocks.

### Time averaging: where the 1/4 comes from

Let log consumption follow a random walk at a fine step, $x_s = x_{s-1} + e_s$, with independent shocks of
variance 1. The scale cancels in every ratio below. A period holds $n_{\text{avg}}$ steps, and the measured
level of period $t$ is the average $A_t = \tfrac{1}{n_{\text{avg}}} \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{avg}}} x_{(t-1)n_{\text{avg}}+j}$.

Step 1. Measured growth is the gap between two averages, day by matching day:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_t - A_{t-1} = \frac{1}{n_{\text{avg}}} \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{avg}}} \big(&x_{(t-1)n_{\text{avg}}+j} \\
&- x_{(t-2)n_{\text{avg}}+j}\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Step 2. Each gap in the brackets is the sum of the $n_{\text{avg}}$ shocks between those two days. Count how
many gaps contain each shock. The $2n_{\text{avg}} - 1$ shocks after the first day of period $t - 1$ appear
with weights $1, 2, \dots, n_{\text{avg}}, \dots, 2, 1$, all divided by $n_{\text{avg}}$. Shocks early in period
$t-1$ fall in few gaps. Shocks near its end fall in almost all of them.

Step 3. The shocks are independent with variance 1, so the variance is the sum of the squared weights:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(A_t - A_{t-1}) &= \frac{1}{n_{\text{avg}}^2}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{avg}}} i^2 \\
&\qquad + \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{avg}}-1} i^2\Big) \\
&= \frac{2n_{\text{avg}}^2 + 1}{3n_{\text{avg}}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Step 4. Growth from the end of one period to the end of the next sums $n_{\text{avg}}$ shocks, so its
variance is $n_{\text{avg}}$. The ratio is

$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}(A_t - A_{t-1})}{n_{\text{avg}}} = \frac{2n_{\text{avg}}^2 + 1}{3n_{\text{avg}}^2} \;\to\; \frac23.
$$

Step 5. The next growth, $A_{t+1} - A_t$, uses the same weights moved on by $n_{\text{avg}}$ shocks. The two
share the $n_{\text{avg}} - 1$ shocks of period $t$ after its first step. In the first growth these have the
falling weights $n_{\text{avg}}-1, \dots, 1$. In the second they have the rising weights
$1, \dots, n_{\text{avg}}-1$. So

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{Cov}(A_t - A_{t-1},\, A_{t+1} - A_t) \\
&= \frac{1}{n_{\text{avg}}^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{avg}}-1} i\,(n_{\text{avg}} - i) \\
&= \frac{n_{\text{avg}}^2 - 1}{6n_{\text{avg}}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Step 6. Divide by the variance of Step 3:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{corr}(A_t - A_{t-1},\, A_{t+1} - A_t) \\
&= \frac{n_{\text{avg}}^2 - 1}{2(2n_{\text{avg}}^2 + 1)} \;\to\; \frac14.
\end{aligned}
$$

Growth rates two periods apart share no shock, so their correlation is zero.

<details>
<summary>The sums in Steps 3 and 5</summary>

Use the two sums $\sum_{i=1}^{k} i = k(k+1)/2$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{k} i^2 = k(k+1)(2k+1)/6$, which hold for any
whole number $k$. For Step 3, take $k = n_{\text{avg}}$ and then $k = n_{\text{avg}} - 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{avg}}} i^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{avg}}-1} i^2 \\
&= \tfrac16 n_{\text{avg}}(n_{\text{avg}}+1)(2n_{\text{avg}}+1) \\
&\quad + \tfrac16 (n_{\text{avg}}-1)n_{\text{avg}}(2n_{\text{avg}}-1) \\
&= \tfrac16 n_{\text{avg}}\,(4n_{\text{avg}}^2 + 2) \\
&= \tfrac13 n_{\text{avg}}(2n_{\text{avg}}^2 + 1).
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by $n_{\text{avg}}^2$ to get $(2n_{\text{avg}}^2 + 1)/(3n_{\text{avg}})$. For Step 5, take
$k = n_{\text{avg}} - 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{avg}}-1} i\,(n_{\text{avg}} - i) \\
&= \tfrac12 n_{\text{avg}}(n_{\text{avg}}-1)n_{\text{avg}} \\
&\quad - \tfrac16 (n_{\text{avg}}-1)n_{\text{avg}}(2n_{\text{avg}}-1) \\
&= \tfrac16 n_{\text{avg}}(n_{\text{avg}}-1) \\
&\quad \times \big(3n_{\text{avg}} - 2n_{\text{avg}} + 1\big) \\
&= \tfrac16 n_{\text{avg}}(n_{\text{avg}}^2 - 1).
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by $n_{\text{avg}}^2$ to get $(n_{\text{avg}}^2 - 1)/(6n_{\text{avg}})$.
</details>

Kroencke reports the two limits, $2/3$ and $1/4$, from Working (1960) and Breeden, Gibbons & Litzenberger
(1989). He also reports that averaging halves the covariance with a return (working paper p. 8).

### The filter, and what it does to a covariance

Kroencke models the statistician as an estimator who moves part of the way toward each new reading (working
paper pp. 6–7). In growth rates, with filter weight $\varphi$ between 0 and 1, reported growth is

$$
\Data{\Delta c^{\text{NIPA}}_t} = (1 - \varphi)\,\Delta c_t + \varphi\,\Data{\Delta c^{\text{NIPA}}_{t-1}}.
$$

A share $1 - \varphi$ of this year's true growth gets in now. The rest is last year's reported growth. This is
an AR(1), as in Chapter 5, driven by true growth. We write the weight $\varphi$, not $\phi$, because $\phi$ is
the persistence of a predictor in this course.

Let true growth be serially uncorrelated with variance $\sigma_c^2$, as for a random walk. Three facts follow.

1. **Variance.** Take the variance of both sides. The two terms on the right are uncorrelated, and both sides
   have the same variance $V$: $V = (1-\varphi)^2\sigma_c^2 + \varphi^2 V$. So
   $V = \frac{(1-\varphi)^2}{1 - \varphi^2}\sigma_c^2 = \frac{1-\varphi}{1+\varphi}\,\sigma_c^2$.
2. **Autocorrelation.** Multiply both sides by $\Data{\Delta c^{\text{NIPA}}_{t-1}}$ and take expectations.
   True growth this year is uncorrelated with the past, so the covariance is $\varphi V$ and the
   autocorrelation is $\varphi$.
3. **Covariance with the return.** Do the same with this year's return $\Ret{r_t}$. If returns are not
   predictable from past consumption growth, the second term drops out:
   $\operatorname{Cov}(\Data{\Delta c^{\text{NIPA}}_t}, \Ret{r_t}) = (1 - \varphi)\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta c_t, \Ret{r_t})$.

The third fact is the one that matters for the puzzle. Implied $\gamma$ divides by $\sigma_{ic}$, so measured
data inflate it by $1/(1 - \varphi)$. With $\varphi = 0.6$, that is a factor of 2.5. The correlation falls by
less, by $(1-\varphi)\big/\sqrt{(1-\varphi)/(1+\varphi)} = \sqrt{1 - \varphi^2}$.

### Undoing it

The filter is easy to run backwards. Solve its equation for true growth:

$$
\Model{\Delta c_t} = \frac{\Data{\Delta c^{\text{NIPA}}_t} - \varphi\,\Data{\Delta c^{\text{NIPA}}_{t-1}}}{1 - \varphi}.
$$

This **unfiltered** series needs last year's reported growth, so it loses the first year. Kroencke takes the
idea from real-estate research, where appraisal-based price indices are smoothed in the same way (working
paper p. 3).

Kroencke writes his unfilter parameter as one minus our weight, and builds it from two parts (working paper
pp. 9–10). One part undoes time averaging. Fact 1 says a filter keeps $(1-\varphi)/(1+\varphi)$ of the
variance, and averaging keeps $2/3$. Set them equal: $\varphi = 1/5$. So a weight of 0.20, his 0.80, treats
averaging as a filter. Its autocorrelation, 0.20, is close to the 0.25 of averaging. The other part is a pure
filter with a weight of 1/2, which he finds fits the data. He multiplies the two parts of his parameter,
$0.80 \times 1/2 = 0.40$, which is our $\varphi = 0.60$. Treating two smoothing steps as one filter is an
approximation, and he says so. Calibrated this way, his unfiltered series matches garbage closely (working
paper p. 17).

### Three measures on our data

A third measure avoids most of the averaging without any model: growth from the fourth quarter of one year to
the fourth quarter of the next. It still averages over three months, but it compares the end of each year
rather than its middle. We build it from the quarterly `fred-cay-quarterly` data. Their first fourth quarter
with a level is 1952, so the first growth is 1953. Each measure goes with the same year's log real return of the market from
`goyal-welch-annual`. Here are the four series on the 73 years from 1953 to 2025:

| Measure | Volatility | Autocorrelation | Corr. with market | Implied $\gamma$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Annual averages, as reported | 1.50% | 0.12 | 0.01 | 2,313 |
| Fourth quarter to fourth quarter | 1.54% | 0.07 | 0.30 | 100 |
| Unfiltered, $\varphi = 0.20$ | 1.86% | −0.09 | 0.08 | 313 |
| Unfiltered, $\varphi = 0.60$ | 4.13% | −0.38 | 0.19 | 59 |

Read the reported $\gamma$ as "enormous", not as 2,313. A covariance near zero makes the ratio swing wildly.
Start the full sample in 1930 instead of 1931, and the reported $\gamma$ falls from 849 to 271.

Three things stand out. The fourth-quarter measure has the highest correlation with the market, 0.30, and
cuts $\gamma$ to 100 with no change in volatility. Unfiltering with Kroencke's 0.60 cuts $\gamma$ to 59, but
it overshoots: an autocorrelation of −0.38 says we removed more smoothing than was there. And the pandemic
years weigh a lot. Stop the sample in 2019, and the reported autocorrelation is 0.47, while the 0.60
unfiltered series has −0.10, a correlation of 0.23 and a $\gamma$ of 71.

The timing points the same way. Take reported growth from 1930 to 2025. Paired with the same year's return,
its correlation is 0.07. Paired with the *previous* year's return, it is 0.53. Measured consumption seems to
hear the stock market a year late, which is what a filter does.

Kroencke, with his own estimator and data to 2011, reports a $\gamma$ between 19 and 34 for unfiltered
consumption after the war, close to the 15 to 25 of garbage (working paper p. 2). Our simple moment formula
is not his estimator, and our sample is longer. So our numbers are not a test of his.

So say plainly what our data show. Undoing the smoothing raises the covariance of consumption with stocks, and
with Kroencke's weight of 0.60 the implied $\gamma$ falls by a factor of more than 10. But it never falls near the single digits that most
economists find plausible. And the answer depends a lot on a weight that the data do not pin down: across
the weights Kroencke recommends, $\gamma$ runs from 260 to 58 on the full sample. These are point estimates
from one sample, with no standard errors. A correlation of 0.15 over 95 years is about 1.5 standard errors
from zero.

<Callout type="warning">Unfiltering is a model of how the statisticians work. It can only remove the smoothing
that the model describes, and the weight you choose drives the answer. Measurement explains part of the
puzzle, not all of it.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- National-accounts consumption is a flow over a period, built by statisticians who estimate its level. Both
  steps smooth it.
- Averaging a random walk over $n_{\text{avg}}$ steps gives growth with autocorrelation
  $(n_{\text{avg}}^2 - 1)/(2(2n_{\text{avg}}^2 + 1)) \to 1/4$ and $2/3$ of the variance.
- A filter with weight $\varphi$ keeps $1 - \varphi$ of the covariance with returns, so it inflates the
  implied $\gamma$ by $1/(1-\varphi)$. Its inverse is
  $\Delta c_t = (\Delta c^{\text{NIPA}}_t - \varphi\,\Delta c^{\text{NIPA}}_{t-1})/(1 - \varphi)$. Kroencke's 0.80 and
  0.40 are our $\varphi = 0.20$ and $0.60$.
- On our data from 1931 to 2025, unfiltering with $\varphi = 0.60$ cuts the implied $\gamma$ from 849 to 58.
  From 1953, the fourth-quarter measure gives 100. Neither is a plausible risk aversion, and both depend on
  choices the data do not pin down.
