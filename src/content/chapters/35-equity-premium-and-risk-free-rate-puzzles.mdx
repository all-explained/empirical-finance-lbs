## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Smooth consumption, rich stocks

From 1930 to 2025, the US stock market earned a log real return of 6.52% a year, on average. Treasury bills
earned 0.24%. So stocks beat bills by 6.28% a year, with a volatility of 19.2%. The data are the CRSP
value-weighted return, the T-bill rate and inflation in goyal-welch-annual, 96 years.

Over the same 96 years, real consumption per person (nondurables and services, from fred-consumption-annual)
grew by 1.83% a year. Its growth had a standard deviation of only 2.17%. In most years, the typical household
consumed about 2% more than the year before, give or take 2%.

So here is the question. Chapter 8 says that investors demand a premium for assets that pay badly when
consumption falls. If consumption hardly ever falls by much, why did investors need 6.3% a year to hold stocks?
And if they really were that afraid of risk, why did they accept a real return of almost zero on bills?

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> One curve, two failures

Take the power-utility SDF of Chapter 8 and feed it the measured consumption moments. For each $\gamma$ you get
one SDF, with a mean $\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]$ and a volatility $\sigma(\SDF{M})$. As $\gamma$ rises from 0, these
points trace a curve. The widget draws that curve against the region that the market's Sharpe ratio allows.

<HJBound client:visible preset="power-utility" />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> at $\gamma = 5$, the ratio $\sigma(\SDF{M})/\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]$
of the annual sample is only 0.11, far below the market's Sharpe ratio of 0.45. Move the slider up and find where
the dot enters the shaded region. Then switch to "Postwar" and "Quarterly".</p>

The curve fails in two ways at once.

The first failure is **height**. The SDF's volatility is about $\gamma$ times the volatility of consumption
growth. With $\sigma_c = 2.17\%$, the curve reaches the bound only at $\gamma = 20.0$. That is a lot of fear.
With $\gamma = 20$, a year in which consumption falls by 2% makes a dollar worth half as much again
($0.98^{-20} = 1.50$). With the $\gamma = 5$ of Chapter 8's example, it is worth a tenth more ($0.98^{-5} = 1.11$).
This is the **equity premium puzzle**. Campbell (2014, working paper, pdf p. 10) explains it with exactly this
bound and traces it to Mehra and Prescott (1985). His round numbers, a Sharpe ratio of 0.4 and $\sigma_c$ near 1%,
need $\gamma$ near 40.

The second failure is **position**. As $\gamma$ rises, the curve also moves to the left. A lower
$\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]$ means a higher risk-free rate, because $\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}] = e^{-r^f}$ here. At
$\gamma = 20.0$, the implied log real rate is 28.2% a year. The data say 0.24%. This is the
**risk-free rate puzzle**, the name Ludvigson (2013, working paper, pdf p. 4) gives to a low real rate next
to a high equity premium.

<Callout type="key">With measured consumption, power utility reaches the Hansen–Jagannathan bound only at a large
$\gamma$. At that $\gamma$, the same model predicts a risk-free rate about 28 percentage points above the
data.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Lognormal pricing

### One fact about the normal distribution

If $x$ is normal with mean $\mu_x$ and variance $\sigma_x^2$, then

$$
\mathbb{E}[e^x] = e^{\mu_x + \frac12\sigma_x^2}.
$$

The extra half variance appears because $e^x$ is convex: a high draw of $x$ raises $e^x$ more than a low draw
lowers it. This fact turns the pricing rule into a linear equation.

### The pricing rule in logs

Assume that log consumption growth $\Delta c_{t+1}$ and the log return $\Ret{r_{i,t+1}}$ of asset $i$ are jointly
normal, with constant moments. Write $\mu_c$ and $\sigma_c$ for the mean and standard deviation of
$\Delta c_{t+1}$, $\sigma_i$ for the standard deviation of $\Ret{r_{i,t+1}}$, and $\sigma_{ic}$ for their
covariance. With power utility,

$$
\log\SDF{M_{t+1}} = \log\delta - \gamma\,\Delta c_{t+1}.
$$

The pricing rule is $\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M_{t+1}}(1 + \Ret{R_{i,t+1}})] = 1$. Since $1 + \Ret{R_{i,t+1}} =
e^{\Ret{r_{i,t+1}}}$, the product inside the expectation is $e^x$ with

$$
x = \log\delta - \gamma\,\Delta c_{t+1} + \Ret{r_{i,t+1}}.
$$

A sum of jointly normal variables is normal. Its mean is $\log\delta - \gamma\mu_c + \mathbb{E}[\Ret{r_i}]$. Its
variance follows from $\operatorname{Var}(a + b) = \operatorname{Var}(a) + \operatorname{Var}(b) +
2\operatorname{Cov}(a, b)$, with $a = -\gamma\Delta c_{t+1}$ and $b = \Ret{r_{i,t+1}}$:

$$
\sigma_x^2 = \gamma^2\sigma_c^2 + \sigma_i^2 - 2\gamma\sigma_{ic}.
$$

Now use the fact above. $\mathbb{E}[e^x] = 1$ means $\mu_x + \tfrac12\sigma_x^2 = 0$, because $\log 1 = 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 = {} & \log\delta - \gamma\mu_c + \mathbb{E}[\Ret{r_i}] \\
& + \tfrac12\left(\gamma^2\sigma_c^2 + \sigma_i^2 - 2\gamma\sigma_{ic}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

### The risk-free rate

A riskless asset has $\sigma_i = 0$ and $\sigma_{ic} = 0$, and its log return is $\Rf{r^f}$. Put these into the
equation and solve for the rate:

$$
\Rf{r^f} = -\log\delta + \gamma\mu_c - \tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma_c^2.
$$

Each term has a story. $-\log\delta$ is impatience: a saver who discounts the future needs a reward to wait.
$\gamma\mu_c$ is growth: when consumption grows, a saver with a high $\gamma$ wants to borrow against the richer
future, and only a high rate stops them. $-\tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma_c^2$ is precautionary saving: risk about the
future makes people save more, which pushes the rate down.

### The equity premium

Subtract the riskless equation from the one for asset $i$. The terms in $\delta$, $\mu_c$ and $\sigma_c$ cancel:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\Ret{r_i} - \Rf{r^f}] + \tfrac12\sigma_i^2 = \gamma\,\sigma_{ic}.
$$

The half variance on the left turns a log premium into an arithmetic one. The right side says that only the
covariance with consumption growth is priced. Since $\sigma_{ic} = \operatorname{corr}(\Delta c, \Ret{r_i})\,
\sigma_c\,\sigma_i$, solve for risk aversion:

$$
\gamma = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\Ret{r_i} - \Rf{r^f}] + \tfrac12\sigma_i^2}{\operatorname{corr}(\Delta c, \Ret{r_i})
\,\sigma_c\,\sigma_i}.
$$

### The Hansen–Jagannathan ratio

$\SDF{M}$ is itself lognormal, with $\log\SDF{M}$ of mean $\log\delta - \gamma\mu_c$ and variance
$\gamma^2\sigma_c^2$. Apply the fact to $\SDF{M}$ and to $\SDF{M}^2 = e^{2\log\SDF{M}}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}] &= \delta e^{-\gamma\mu_c + \frac12\gamma^2\sigma_c^2} = e^{-\Rf{r^f}}, \\
\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}^2] &= \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]^2\,e^{\gamma^2\sigma_c^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\operatorname{Var}(\SDF{M}) = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}^2] - \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]^2 = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]^2
(e^{\gamma^2\sigma_c^2} - 1)$. Take the square root and divide by the mean. For a small $z$, $e^z - 1 \approx
z$, so

$$
\frac{\sigma(\SDF{M})}{\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}]} = \sqrt{e^{\gamma^2\sigma_c^2} - 1} \approx \gamma\,\sigma_c.
$$

The bound of Chapter 8 says this ratio must be at least the Sharpe ratio $SR$. So $\gamma \ge SR/\sigma_c$,
roughly. This lower limit is what $\gamma$ would be if consumption and returns were perfectly correlated.

<Callout type="note" title="An approximation, and its limits">
These formulas assume joint lognormality and constant moments. We then plug in sample moments. Ludvigson (2013,
working paper, pdf p. 22) reports that the data reject joint lognormality, so read the numbers below as a
careful approximation, not as exact model values. Every rate here is a log real rate, a year.
</Callout>

### The numbers

The table puts three samples into these formulas. Quarterly consumption is the series behind $cay$ in
[Chapter 14](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/14-consumption-wealth-ratio/), with returns from goyal-welch-monthly.
Its means are times 4 and its standard deviations times 2, to give values a year.

| | Annual 1930–2025 | Postwar 1947–2025 | Quarterly 1952–2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Periods | 96 | 79 | 295 |
| $\mu_c$ | 1.83% | 1.85% | 1.94% |
| $\sigma_c$ | 2.17% | 1.53% | 1.77% |
| $\operatorname{corr}(\Delta c, \Ret{r})$ | 0.072 | 0.037 | 0.046 |
| $\mathbb{E}[\Ret{r} - \Rf{r^f}]$ | 6.28% | 7.13% | 6.72% |
| $\sigma_i$ | 19.2% | 16.7% | 16.1% |
| $\gamma$ from $\sigma_{ic}$ | 271 | 898 | 618 |
| $\gamma$ from $SR/\sigma_c$ | 15.0 | 28.6 | 23.9 |
| Mean $\Rf{r^f}$ | 0.24% | 0.47% | 0.61% |

Here $SR$ is the Sharpe ratio of each sample's own log excess return: 0.33 a year for the annual sample. It is
lower than the 0.45 of the widget, which comes from monthly simple returns in ff3-monthly. That is why the widget
needs $\gamma = 20.0$ where the table says 15.0.

The two rows of $\gamma$ split the puzzle in half. The $SR/\sigma_c$ row is the smooth-consumption half: even
with a perfect correlation, $\gamma$ must be 15 to 29. The $\sigma_{ic}$ row adds the low-correlation half.
Consumption growth and stock returns barely move together, with correlations from 0.04 to 0.07. So the
covariance is tiny, and $\gamma$ must be in the hundreds. [Chapter 26](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/26-gmm-for-asset-pricing-models/)
met the same problem from another side: its GMM estimate of the consumption CAPM put a huge weight on
consumption growth, because a series that barely moves can only price returns if it is scaled up a lot.

The samples differ for clear reasons. The annual sample includes the Depression and the Second World War, when
consumption swung hard. Drop those years and $\sigma_c$ falls from 2.17% to 1.53%, so every $\gamma$ rises. The
quarterly sample covers almost the same postwar years, so it tells the same story. Its $\sigma_c$, scaled to a
year, is higher than the postwar annual one (1.77% against 1.53%). Part of the reason is that an annual figure is
an average of four quarters, and averaging smooths out some of the quarter-to-quarter changes.

<Callout type="warning" title="What the evidence can and cannot show">
The 6.28% premium has a standard error of 1.97%, since it is a mean of 96 volatile years. With 96 years, a
correlation of 0.07 is well inside two standard errors of zero (about $\pm 2/\sqrt{96} = \pm 0.20$). And the
timing of annual consumption matters: the national accounts (NIPA) report the average over the year, not its level at the end. Pair the
return of each year with the *next* year's consumption growth, and the correlation rises to 0.53 and the
covariance $\gamma$ falls to 36. That is still large, and the $SR/\sigma_c$ value of 14.3 hardly moves.
</Callout>

### The risk-free rate puzzle in numbers

Now take the most generous risk aversion, $\gamma = 15.0$ for the annual sample, and $\delta = 0.99$. The formula
gives $\Rf{r^f} = 23.2\%$ a year, against 0.24% in the data. Even log utility, $\gamma = 1$, gives 2.81%.

Turn the question around. Which $\delta$ would match the data at $\gamma = 15.0$? Solve the formula for it:
$\delta = e^{-\Rf{r^f} + \gamma\mu_c - \frac12\gamma^2\sigma_c^2}$. The answer is 1.245. A $\delta$ above 1
means that people value the future *more* than the present. In the postwar sample, at its own $\gamma$ of
28.6, it is 1.536.

At the covariance $\gamma$ of 271, the precautionary term takes over. Then $\tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma_c^2$ is so
large that the matching $\delta$ falls to $4.5 \times 10^{-6}$, and nobody that impatient would save at all.
No $\gamma$ fits both the premium and the rate.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- With lognormal power utility, $\mathbb{E}[\Ret{r_i} - \Rf{r^f}] + \tfrac12\sigma_i^2 = \gamma\sigma_{ic}$ and
  $\Rf{r^f} = -\log\delta + \gamma\mu_c - \tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma_c^2$.
- The SDF's ratio $\sigma(\SDF{M})/\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}] \approx \gamma\sigma_c$. With $\sigma_c$ near 2% a
  year, the Hansen–Jagannathan bound needs $\gamma$ of 15 to 29.
- The low correlation of consumption with returns is the other half of the puzzle: the covariance needs $\gamma$
  in the hundreds.
- A $\gamma$ high enough for the bound gives a risk-free rate of 23% to 44% a year, against under 1% in the
  data. Only a $\delta$ above 1 would fix it.
