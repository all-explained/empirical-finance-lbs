## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Same model, new fit

Take the 25 portfolios sorted on size and book-to-market, in quarterly data from 1963-Q3 to 1998-Q3: 141
quarters. Ask the consumption CAPM of
[Chapter 8](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/08-euler-equation-and-sdf/) to explain their average excess returns.
Run the two-pass regression of
[Chapter 17](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/17-cross-sectional-regressions-and-fama-macbeth/), with consumption
growth as the only factor. The cross-sectional $R^2$ is 0.07. The model explains almost none of the spread.

Now change one thing. Let the model's coefficients move with one number dated at the start of each quarter:
the consumption–wealth ratio $cay$. The same 25 portfolios, the same quarters and the same consumption
data now give an $R^2$ of 0.55. Lettau & Ludvigson (2001, JPE) ran this experiment over the same 141 quarters
(p. 1255) and reported a jump from 0.16 to 0.70 (table 3, p. 1260).

Why should a model that moves over time fit *average* returns better? And is a model that holds period by period
the same as a model that holds on average? This chapter shows that it is not, and how to test the first kind
with the tools you already have.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> A beta that rises in bad times

Start with a toy world where the CAPM holds every period. There are two kinds of periods, good and bad, and each
happens half the time. In good times the market risk premium is 4% and a stock has a beta of 0.5. In bad times the
premium is 8% and the beta is 1.5. Each period, the stock's expected excess return is beta times premium: 2% in
good times and 12% in bad times. On average, it earns 7%.

Now look only at averages. The average beta is 1 and the average premium is 6%. Their product is 6%, not 7%. The
missing 1% comes from timing: the stock has its high beta exactly when risk pays the most. An econometrician who
fits one constant beta sees a stock that earns more than its average beta explains, and calls the gap an alpha.
Jagannathan & Wang (1996) give a story for such a stock: in bad times, firms with weak balance sheets and high
leverage get riskier, just when the market premium is high (staff report, p. 6).

The widget below simulates this world over 600 months. A state variable $z_t$, known at the start of each month,
moves slowly. The beta $\beta_t$ (top) and the premium $\lambda_t$ (bottom) both move with it. The shaded months
are the bad times, when the premium is above its mean. Every month, the expected excess return of the stock is
exactly $\beta_t\lambda_t$. The conditional CAPM is true by construction.

1.72%, so its $t$-statistic is only 0.80. Fifty years of data do not reveal it. The conditional alpha, which uses
the true $\beta_t$, is −0.18% ± 1.50%: no pricing error, as it should be. Both facts matter. A conditional model
can leave a real unconditional alpha, and that alpha can be too small to see.

<Callout type="key">A factor model can hold every period and still leave an alpha on average. The alpha is the
covariance between the asset's beta and the price of risk.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> From period by period to on average

### The conditional CAPM

Write $\mathbb{E}_t[\cdot]$ for an expectation given what you know at $t$. The conditional CAPM says that for every
asset $i$ and every period,

$$
\mathbb{E}_t[\Ret{R^e_{i,t+1}}] = \beta_{it}\,\lambda_t, \qquad
\beta_{it} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}_t(R^e_{i,t+1}, R^e_{m,t+1})}{\operatorname{Var}_t(R^e_{m,t+1})}, \qquad
\lambda_t = \mathbb{E}_t[\Ret{R^e_{m,t+1}}].
$$

Both the beta and the premium carry the date $t$: they are known at $t$, and they can change from one period to the
next. In this chapter $\beta_{it}$ is a conditional beta. A beta without a date, such as $\beta_i$, is a constant.

### What the model says about averages

You estimate with averages over time, so take the unconditional expectation of both sides. Three steps.

1. The law of iterated expectations says that the average of conditional expectations is the unconditional
   expectation: $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}_t[R^e_{i,t+1}]\big] = \mathbb{E}[R^e_{i,t+1}]$. So
   $\mathbb{E}[R^e_i] = \mathbb{E}[\beta_{it}\lambda_t]$.
2. For any two random variables, $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$.
3. Put $X = \beta_{it}$ and $Y = \lambda_t$, and rearrange:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_i}] = \mathbb{E}[\beta_{it}]\,\mathbb{E}[\lambda_t] + \operatorname{Cov}(\beta_{it}, \lambda_t).
$$

The first term looks like the CAPM with the average beta. The second term is new. It is positive for an asset
whose beta is high when the premium is high, and negative for an asset whose beta is low then. The readout of the
widget checks this identity in sample, with each average dividing by $T$. Jagannathan & Wang (1996) start from the
same split (staff report, p. 6, eq. 4).

### Why a constant-beta regression shows an alpha

The unconditional regression of [Chapter 16](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/16-time-series-tests-and-grs/) fits
$R^e_{i,t+1} = \alpha^u_i + \beta^u_i R^e_{m,t+1} + \varepsilon_{i,t+1}$. Its slope $\beta^u_i$ is the
unconditional covariance over the unconditional variance. That is not the average of $\beta_{it}$. In the widget's
world, $\beta_t = \beta_0 + s_\beta z_t$ and $\lambda_t = \lambda_0 + s_\lambda z_t$, where $s_\beta$ and
$s_\lambda$ are how much the beta and the premium move per standard deviation $\sigma_z$ of the state. The market
excess return is $\lambda_t$ plus a shock with standard deviation $\sigma_m$, and the state is normal and
independent of the shocks. Write $c = s_\beta s_\lambda\sigma_z^2 = \operatorname{Cov}(\beta_t, \lambda_t)$ and
$V = \operatorname{Var}(R^e_m) = s_\lambda^2\sigma_z^2 + \sigma_m^2$. Then:

1. $\mathbb{E}[R^e_m] = \lambda_0$, and from the split above, $\mathbb{E}[R^e_i] = \beta_0\lambda_0 + c$.
2. $\mathbb{E}[R^e_i R^e_m] = \mathbb{E}[\beta_t (R^e_m)^2]$, because the asset's own shock is independent of the
   market. Given $z_t$, $\mathbb{E}_t[(R^e_m)^2] = \lambda_t^2 + \sigma_m^2$. Expand
   $\mathbb{E}[(\beta_0 + s_\beta z_t)((\lambda_0 + s_\lambda z_t)^2 + \sigma_m^2)]$. A normal $z_t$ with mean zero
   has $\mathbb{E}[z_t^3] = 0$, so this is $\beta_0(\lambda_0^2 + V) + 2c\lambda_0$.
3. $\operatorname{Cov}(R^e_i, R^e_m) = \beta_0(\lambda_0^2 + V) + 2c\lambda_0 - (\beta_0\lambda_0 + c)\lambda_0
   = \beta_0 V + c\lambda_0$. So $\beta^u = \beta_0 + c\lambda_0/V$.
4. $\alpha^u = \mathbb{E}[R^e_i] - \beta^u\,\mathbb{E}[R^e_m] = \beta_0\lambda_0 + c - \beta_0\lambda_0 -
   c\lambda_0^2/V$, which gives

$$
\Err{\alpha^u} = \operatorname{Cov}(\beta_t, \lambda_t)\left(1 - \frac{\mathbb{E}[R^e_m]^2}{\operatorname{Var}(R^e_m)}\right).
$$

The bracket is close to 1, because the squared monthly mean of the market is tiny next to its variance. So the
unconditional alpha is almost exactly the covariance term. With the defaults, $s_\beta = 0.4$ and
$s_\lambda = 4.8\%$ a year, the covariance is $0.4 \times 4.8\% = 1.92\%$ a year and the population alpha is 1.90%.
The alpha is zero when either $s_\beta$ or $s_\lambda$ is zero: the beta must move, and it must move with the
premium. One sample of 600 months estimated 1.38% with a standard error of 1.72%. To get a $t$-statistic of 2 on an
alpha of 1.90% with that noise, you would need about 160 years of data.

### Scaled factors

You rarely see $\beta_{it}$ or $\lambda_t$. The stochastic discount factor of
[Chapter 8](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/08-euler-equation-and-sdf/) gives a way around this. For an excess
return, $\mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1}(1 + R_{t+1})] = 1$ and $\mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1}(1 + R^f_t)] = 1$; subtract them to get
$\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M_{t+1}}\,\Ret{R^e_{i,t+1}}] = 0$. A conditional linear factor model says
$M_{t+1} = a_t + b_t f_{t+1}$, with coefficients that are known at $t$ but can change. Model the coefficients as
linear in a variable $\Signal{z_t}$ known at $t$: $a_t = a_0 + a_1 z_t$ and $b_t = b_0 + b_1 z_t$. Multiply out:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\SDF{M_{t+1}} &= (a_0 + a_1 \Signal{z_t}) + (b_0 + b_1 \Signal{z_t})\,\Factor{f_{t+1}} \\
&= a_0 + a_1\,\Signal{z_t} + b_0\,\Factor{f_{t+1}} + b_1\,\Signal{z_t}\Factor{f_{t+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line has four constant coefficients, $a_0$, $a_1$, $b_0$ and $b_1$, on the "factors" $1$, $z_t$,
$f_{t+1}$ and $z_t f_{t+1}$. Take the unconditional expectation of $\mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1}R^e_{i,t+1}] = 0$: by the
law of iterated expectations, $\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}R^e_{i,t+1}] = 0$. Nothing in $M_{t+1}$ changes over time except
the factors. So the conditional one-factor model is an *unconditional* three-factor model, with factors $z_t$,
$f_{t+1}$ and $z_t f_{t+1}$. Lettau & Ludvigson (2001, JPE) write the model this way (eq. 2, p. 1246). You can test
it with the time-series and cross-sectional tools of Chapters 16 and 17, which need constant coefficients.

The product $z_t f_{t+1}$ is the **scaled factor**: the factor, multiplied by the lagged state. In the scaled
consumption CAPM, $f_{t+1}$ is consumption growth $\Delta c_{t+1}$ and $z_t$ is $cay_t$. That ratio (the topic of
Chapter 14, *The consumption–wealth ratio*) is the gap between log consumption and a weighted sum of log asset
wealth and log labour income. It is high when consumption is high compared with wealth and income. Lettau &
Ludvigson (2001, JPE) chose it because it forecasts excess stock returns (p. 1243, p. 1251).

Two cautions. The step from conditional to unconditional is exact only if the coefficients really are linear in
$z_t$. And it holds only for the $z_t$ you chose. A model that is true given everything investors know need not
be true given only $z_t$.

### Managed portfolios

The same idea works on the assets. $z_t$ is known at $t$, so you can take it out of a conditional expectation:

$$
\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M_{t+1}}\,\Signal{z_t}\Ret{R^e_{i,t+1}}] = \Signal{z_t}\;\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M_{t+1}}\Ret{R^e_{i,t+1}}] = 0.
$$

Average over time: $\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}\,z_t R^e_{i,t+1}] = 0$. The product $z_t R^e_{i,t+1}$ is the excess return of
a **managed portfolio**: at each $t$, hold $z_t$ dollars of asset $i$, financed at the risk-free rate. A correct
conditional model must price these returns too. So the full set of test assets is

$$
\Ret{R^e_{t+1}} \otimes (1, \Signal{z_t}) = \big(R^e_{1,t+1},\; z_t R^e_{1,t+1},\; \dots,\; R^e_{N,t+1},\; z_t R^e_{N,t+1}\big),
$$

where $\otimes$ (the Kronecker product) pairs each return with each entry of $(1, z_t)$. Scaled factors change the
model. Managed portfolios change the test. A full test of a conditional model uses both.

### What the fit does and does not show

Look at the readout for the scaled model in the first sample. Its constant is 14.3% a year, while the actual
averages run from 3.5% to 14.2%. Lettau & Ludvigson (2001, JPE) read the constant as the expected return of a
zero-beta portfolio, which need not be zero (p. 1247). Still, a zero-beta asset that beats every test portfolio
is hard to believe. The betas explain the differences between the portfolios, not their level. Here are three more
limits.

**The choice of $z_t$ is free.** Theory says that the coefficients of $M_{t+1}$ move with what investors know. It
does not say which variable to use. Each candidate gives new factors, and with 25 points and four coefficients a
search over many candidates will find one that fits by luck. A fair test fixes $z_t$ before it looks at the
cross-section.

**Full-sample $cay$ uses future data.** The weights inside $cay$ come from one regression on every quarter from
1952 to 2026. So the value of $cay$ in 1970 depends on data from after 1970, which no investor had. This is the
look-ahead problem of [Chapter 13](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/13-out-of-sample-predictability/). Here it
flatters the state variable, not a forecast.

**A high $R^2$ on these portfolios is a low bar.** The 25 portfolios line up along one or two dimensions, so a
model with three slopes can fit them well (Chapter 17 makes the same point). Managed portfolios show how easy it is
to raise the $R^2$. Add the 25 managed portfolios $cay_t R^e_{i,t+1}$, with $cay_t$ standardised, next to the 25
plain ones, and fit the plain CAPM to all 50. Its $R^2$ jumps from 0.02 to 0.62. Nothing about the CAPM got better.
The plain portfolios have market betas from 0.79 to 1.61, and the managed ones from −0.60 to −0.15. A single line
through two clouds fits well, even when it misses the pattern inside each cloud.

The $R^2$ in the widget has no standard error. It describes the fit; it does not test the model. Lettau & Ludvigson
(2001, JPE) say the same of their cross-sectional $R^2$ (p. 1254).

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- A conditional model, $\mathbb{E}_t[R^e_{i,t+1}] = \beta_{it}\lambda_t$, holds period by period. On average it
  gives $\mathbb{E}[R^e_i] = \mathbb{E}[\beta_{it}]\,\mathbb{E}[\lambda_t] + \operatorname{Cov}(\beta_{it}, \lambda_t)$.
- A constant-beta regression reads the covariance term as an alpha. In a seeded world where the conditional CAPM
  is true, that alpha is real but can be too small to detect in 50 years.
- SDF coefficients linear in $z_t$ turn a conditional model into an unconditional one with factors $z_t$,
  $f_{t+1}$ and $z_t f_{t+1}$. Managed portfolios $R^e_{t+1} \otimes (1, z_t)$ are the matching test assets.
- On the 25 portfolios, scaling the consumption CAPM by $cay$ raises the $R^2$ from 0.07 to 0.55 over 1963-Q3 to
  1998-Q3. The choice of $z_t$, the look-ahead in $cay$ and the low bar of the $R^2$ all limit what that shows.

and whether it is large enough to explain value and momentum.
