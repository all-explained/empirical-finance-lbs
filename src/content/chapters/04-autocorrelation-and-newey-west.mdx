<Callout type="note" title="What you need from Chapter 3">
[Chapter 3](../03-regression-and-standard-errors/) builds these ideas in full. Here is a short reminder of
what this chapter uses.

- **OLS** (ordinary least squares) fits $y_t = x_t'\beta + \Err{\varepsilon_t}$ by choosing $\hat\beta$ to make
  the sum of squared errors as small as possible. The answer is $\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$, where row $t$ of
  the matrix $X$ is $x_t'$.
- The **standard error** of $\hat\beta$ is how far the estimate would move, typically, if you drew a new sample.
  The **$t$-statistic** is $\hat\beta$ divided by its standard error.
- The **classical** standard error assumes that the errors are independent and all have the same variance.
  **White** standard errors drop the second assumption. Both keep the first.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Can a sliding window create information?

From February 1881 to June 2023, the shiller-monthly data hold 1,709 monthly returns of the US stock market. You
can build a 12-month return from every starting month: February 1881 to January 1882, then March 1881 to February
1882, and so on. That gives 1,698 twelve-month returns. Their average log return is 8.7% a year.

How sure can you be of that 8.7%? The standard error from a textbook regression is 0.44 percentage points, so
the $t$-statistic is 19.7. Now cut the same months into 142 separate 12-month blocks that do not overlap. Their average is
almost the same, 8.7%. But the standard error is 1.45 points, and the $t$-statistic is 6.0.

Both numbers come from the same 1,709 months. So here is the question. Did sliding the window one month at a
time really make the estimate three times more precise? If not, which standard error should you believe, and
how do you fix the other one? This chapter answers both questions.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Neighbours share almost everything

Take two 12-month returns that start one month apart. The first covers months 1 to 12. The second covers months
2 to 13. They share 11 months. Only one month leaves and one month enters. So if the first window had a great
year, the second one almost surely had a great year too.

In the widget, window A is fixed on months 1 to 12. Window B starts $j$ months later. The shaded band marks
the months they share. The lower chart shows every 12-month return of the simulation, one for each starting
month.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> start at a gap of one month. A and B share 11 of 12 months, and
the lower chart is a smooth line, because each point is almost the same sum as its neighbour. Now drag the gap to

The monthly returns in this simulation are independent by construction. Yet the 12-month returns built from them
are strongly correlated with their neighbours. At a gap of one month, the shared months alone give a
correlation of $11/12 = 0.92$. The simulation's sample estimate is 0.91.

This is the whole problem in one picture. Each 12-month return is one row of a regression. The error of
that row is mostly the luck of the months inside the window. Rows that share months share luck. So the errors are
**autocorrelated**: an error is correlated with the errors of the rows near it in time.

The classical standard error assumes the 1,698 rows are 1,698 independent pieces of evidence. They are not. Two
rows one month apart carry nearly the same news, counted twice. A rough count of the truly separate evidence is
the number of non-overlapping years, about 142. A formula that believes it has 1,698 independent rows will be
far too confident.

<Callout type="key">Overlapping returns share months, so their errors move together. The classical standard
error counts the same news many times, and so it is too small, and the $t$-statistic too large.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> The variance of $\hat\beta$ with autocorrelated errors

### Where the classical formula goes wrong

Start from the regression $y_t = x_t'\beta + \Err{\varepsilon_t}$, for $t = 1, \dots, T$. Here $x_t$ is the
vector of regressors, including the constant 1. Stack the rows into $y$ and $X$, and substitute $y =
X\beta + \Err{\varepsilon}$ into the OLS formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat\beta &= (X'X)^{-1}X'(X\beta + \Err{\varepsilon}) \\
&= \beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'\Err{\varepsilon} \\
&= \beta + (X'X)^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T} x_t \Err{\varepsilon_t}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last step writes $X'\Err{\varepsilon}$ row by row. Now treat $X$ as fixed. This is a simplification that
keeps the algebra short. Large-sample theory gives the same formula without it. Then the only random part of
$\hat\beta$ is the sum. Write each term of the sum as $z_t = x_t \Err{\varepsilon_t}$. Then

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta) = (X'X)^{-1}\operatorname{Var}\big(\textstyle\sum_t z_t\big)(X'X)^{-1}.
$$

The errors have mean zero, so each $z_t$ has mean zero. The variance of a sum is the sum of all the covariances
of its terms, one for each pair $(t, s)$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_t z_t\Big) = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}[z_t z_s'].
$$

Group the pairs by their distance in time, $j = t - s$. Define the average covariance at distance $j$ as
$\Gamma_j = \tfrac1T \sum_t \mathbb{E}[z_t z_{t-j}']$. The pairs at distance $-j$ are the same pairs in the
other order, so $\Gamma_{-j} = \Gamma_j'$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_t z_t\Big) &= T \sum_{j=-(T-1)}^{T-1} \Gamma_j = T\,S, \\
S &= \Gamma_0 + \sum_{j \ge 1} \big(\Gamma_j + \Gamma_j'\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Put the pieces together. This is the formula that holds whatever the errors do:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta) = (X'X)^{-1}\,T S\,(X'X)^{-1}.
$$

The classical and White formulas both assume $\Gamma_j = 0$ for every $j \ne 0$. They keep $\Gamma_0$ only.
When neighbouring errors are positively correlated, the dropped terms are positive, and the variance is
understated.

### Overlapping returns give an MA($k-1$) error

Now make the problem concrete. Let monthly log returns $\Ret{r_t}$ be independent, with mean $\mu$ and variance
$\sigma^2$. Regress the $k$-month return on a constant, so $x_t = 1$ and $\hat\beta$ is the sample mean of
$\Ret{r_{t \to t+k}}$. The error is the sum of $k$ monthly surprises:

$$
\Err{\varepsilon_t} = \Ret{r_{t \to t+k}} - k\mu = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (\Ret{r_{t+i}} - \mu).
$$

Two errors $j$ months apart, with $0 \le j < k$, share $k - j$ months. Months that are not shared are
independent, so only the shared ones add to the covariance. Each adds $\sigma^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(\Err{\varepsilon_t}, \Err{\varepsilon_{t-j}}) &= (k - j)\,\sigma^2, \\
\operatorname{corr}(\Err{\varepsilon_t}, \Err{\varepsilon_{t-j}}) &= 1 - \frac{j}{k}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For $j \ge k$ the windows share nothing, and the covariance is zero. A series built from $k$ consecutive
independent shocks, whose autocorrelation stops after lag $k - 1$, is called a **moving average of order
$k - 1$**, or MA($k-1$). With $k = 12$ the correlation falls in a straight line, from $11/12$ at lag 1 to $1/12$ at
lag 11, and then is zero.

How wrong is the classical standard error here? With $x_t = 1$, we have $X'X = T$, so
$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta) = T S / T^2 = S/T$. Add up the covariances:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S &= k\sigma^2 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} (k - j)\sigma^2 \\
&= k\sigma^2 + 2\sigma^2\,\frac{k(k-1)}{2} = k^2\sigma^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The middle step uses the sum $1 + 2 + \dots + (k-1)$, which equals $k(k-1)/2$. The classical formula keeps only $\Gamma_0 =
k\sigma^2$. So it understates the variance by a factor of $k$, and the standard error by a factor of
$\sqrt{k}$. For $k = 12$, that is $\sqrt{12} = 3.46$. There is a second way to read $S/T = k\sigma^2/(T/k)$. It
is the variance of one 12-month return, divided by $T/k$. That is what $T/k$ *independent* 12-month returns
would give you. The overlap does not add information. It only makes the rows look more numerous.

### The Newey–West estimator

In real data we do not know $S$. We estimate each $\Gamma_j$ with the residuals,

$$
\hat\Gamma_j = \frac1T \sum_{t=j+1}^{T} \Err{\hat\varepsilon_t}\Err{\hat\varepsilon_{t-j}}\,x_t x_{t-j}',
$$

and then add them up to a **lag length** $L$, with weights that fall in a straight line:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat S &= \hat\Gamma_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{L} w_j\,\big(\hat\Gamma_j + \hat\Gamma_j'\big), \\
w_j &= 1 - \frac{j}{L+1}, \\
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta) &= (X'X)^{-1}\,T\hat S\,(X'X)^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the **Newey–West** estimator, also called a HAC estimator (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation
consistent). The weights $w_j = 1 - j/(L+1)$ are the **Bartlett weights**. With $L = 0$, the estimator is
White's.

Why not use weight 1 up to lag $L$? Flat weights are the Hansen–Hodrick choice that Chapter 9 also reports. A
sum of estimated covariances with flat weights can come out negative, and a negative variance has no standard
error. The Bartlett weights rule this out.

<details>
<summary>Why the Bartlett weights never give a negative variance</summary>

1. Take one regressor, so each $z_t$ is a number. Treat the values before $t = 1$ and after $t = T$ as zero.
2. Define the moving sum of $L + 1$ neighbours, $m_t = z_t + z_{t-1} + \dots + z_{t-L}$.
3. Square it: $m_t^2 = \sum_{a=0}^{L}\sum_{b=0}^{L} z_{t-a}z_{t-b}$.
4. Add over $t$. For each pair $(a, b)$, the sum $\sum_t z_{t-a}z_{t-b}$ is $\sum_t z_t z_{t-j}$ with $j = |a - b|$.
5. Count the pairs with $|a - b| = j$. There are $L + 1$ pairs with $j = 0$, and $2(L + 1 - j)$ pairs for each
   $j$ from 1 to $L$.
6. So $\frac{1}{T(L+1)}\sum_t m_t^2 = \hat\Gamma_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{L} \frac{L+1-j}{L+1}\,2\hat\Gamma_j$, which is $\hat S$.
7. The left side is a sum of squares, so $\hat S \ge 0$. With several regressors, apply the same steps to
   $c'z_t$ for any vector $c$. Then $c'\hat S c \ge 0$ for every $c$.

</details>

Step 6 also shows what the estimator does. It measures the variance of sums of $L + 1$ neighbouring terms. That
is the overlap idea used in reverse: if the errors move together, sums of neighbours swing more.

### How to choose $L$

The lag length is a choice, and it has a cost in each direction.

- **Too short.** For $k$-month overlapping returns, the true autocorrelation runs to lag $k - 1$. So $L$ must be
  at least $k - 1$. Even then, the Bartlett weights shrink the lags they keep. With $L = 11$, the weight at lag 6
  is only $1 - 6/12 = 0.5$. So in practice choose $L$ well above $k - 1$.
- **Too long.** Every extra lag adds one more estimated covariance, and each estimate is noisy. With $L$ close
  to $T$, the estimate of $S$ is mostly noise.

There is no single right $L$. A good habit is to show the standard error for a range of $L$ and check that the
conclusion does not depend on the choice. That is what the Playground does.

<Callout type="warning">Newey–West fixes the formula, not the data. With $T/k$ non-overlapping periods, you have
only about $T/k$ pieces of independent evidence, and no estimator can create more.</Callout>

Look at what the real data say. The classical standard error, 0.44 points, is the one from the Hook. Newey–West
with $L = 24$ gives 1.38 points, about 3.1 times larger. The theory of the Formalisation said $\sqrt{12} = 3.46$
for independent months. The 142 non-overlapping years gave 1.45 points. So the corrected standard error and
the honest count of years agree. The classical one does not.

Now switch the horizon to "1 month". The same months, used one at a time, give a classical standard error of
1.19 points a year, not 0.44. These monthly returns are not overlapping, but their lag-1 autocorrelation is 0.26.
Newey–West does not need to know why. At $L = 24$ it gives 1.49 points, close to the 12-month answer. Used
correctly, the two ways of slicing the data tell the same story.

What can this evidence show? The average log return of the S&P composite from 1881 to 2023 is clearly
positive: even the corrected $t$-statistic is above 6. What it cannot show is the mean to the nearest point.
A standard error of about 1.4 points means a range of about plus or minus 2.8 points around 8.7%. The simulation
makes the same point more sharply. With 50 years of data, one draw can miss a true 6% mean by more than six
points.

Three limits remain. First, Newey–West tends to understate the standard error in samples of this size. Across the
simulation's seeds 1 to 200, its average at $L = 24$ is 2.00 points, below the true 2.22. Second, its $t$-statistic only follows the normal distribution in large samples. Third, the
choice of $L$ is a judgment call, so report it.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- Overlapping $k$-period returns share months, so their regression errors follow an MA($k-1$): the
  correlation at lag $j$ is $1 - j/k$ when months are independent.
- With autocorrelated errors, $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta) = (X'X)^{-1}\,T S\,(X'X)^{-1}$, and $S$ includes
  the covariances at every lag. The classical formula drops them.
- For the mean of overlapping 12-month returns, the classical standard error is too small by about
  $\sqrt{12} = 3.46$. On shiller-monthly data, 1881–2023, it is 0.44 points against 1.38 for Newey–West with $L = 24$.
- Newey–West adds the estimated covariances up to lag $L$ with Bartlett weights $1 - j/(L+1)$, which keep the
  variance positive. Choose $L$ above $k - 1$ and show that the result holds for a range of $L$.
- No estimator creates information. The effective sample is about $T/k$ separate periods.
