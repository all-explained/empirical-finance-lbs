## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> What return can you expect today?

[Chapter 9](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/09-time-varying-expected-returns/) measured expected returns with a regression on a century of data. The callout above gives the catch:
a regression fitted on the past can fail in real time. So here is a question you may have. Is
there a way to read the expected return of the stock market off today's prices, with no regression at all?

Martin (2017) says yes, with a condition. He builds a lower bound on the expected market return from the prices
of options on the S&P 500 index. He uses daily option prices from January 1996 to January 2012 (p. 382). His
monthly bound averaged only 1.86% a year in 2004–2006. In November 2008, at the height of the financial crisis,
it peaked at 55.0% a year (p. 385). The same method, applied to the same kind of prices, gave answers that differ
by a factor of almost thirty.

This site has no real option data yet. So every option price in this chapter is **simulated**, from a seeded
model, and the numbers from Martin (2017) are quoted with their page.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Insurance prices measure fear

An **option** is a contract on the index. A **put** with strike $K$ pays $K - P_{t+1}$ if the index ends below
$K$, and nothing otherwise. A **call** pays $P_{t+1} - K$ if the index ends above $K$. In this chapter $K$ is a
strike price, not a number of factors. A put is insurance against a fall. A call is a bet on a rise.

An option that would pay nothing if the index stayed where it is today is **out of the money**. These options pay
only if the index moves far. So their prices measure how far the market thinks the index can move. When people
fear large moves, they pay more for that insurance, and all these prices rise together.

Add up the prices of all the out-of-the-money options: puts at strikes below today's forward price, calls at
strikes above it. That sum is the shaded area in the bottom panel below. The top panel shows the **smile**: the
volatility that makes each option price match the Black–Scholes formula. It is higher at low strikes, because
crash insurance is expensive. The widget turns the area into a number: a floor under the expected return.

excess return.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> From $P = \mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}X]$ to the option strip

Take one period to be the life of the options, from $t$ to $t+1$. In the widget it is one year. $\Ret{R_{t+1}}$
is the return of the index over that period, and $\Rf{R^f_t}$ is the risk-free rate over the same period. Both
are net rates, as in the rest of this course.

### Step 1: risk-neutral probabilities

With a finite list of states $s$, Chapter 8's pricing equation reads
$P_t = \sum_s \Prob{\pi(s)}\,\SDF{M(s)}\,X(s)$. Define new weights

$$
\Prob{\pi^*(s)} = \Prob{\pi(s)}\,\SDF{M(s)}\,(1 + \Rf{R^f_t}).
$$

Each weight is positive, because $\SDF{M}$ and $\Prob{\pi}$ are. They add up to one, because
$\sum_s \Prob{\pi(s)}\SDF{M(s)} = \mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M}] = 1/(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})$. So they are probabilities: the
**risk-neutral probabilities**, and $\mathbb{E}^*_t$ is an expectation with them. Put them into the pricing
equation:

$$
P_t = \frac{1}{1 + \Rf{R^f_t}} \sum_s \Prob{\pi^*(s)}\,X(s) = \frac{\mathbb{E}^*_t[X_{t+1}]}{1 + \Rf{R^f_t}}.
$$

A price is the risk-neutral expected payoff, discounted at the risk-free rate. The same identity, written the other
way round, translates between the two measures: $\mathbb{E}^*_t[X] = (1 + \Rf{R^f_t})\,\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M}X]$.
Risk-neutral odds are real odds tilted towards the states where $\SDF{M}$ is high. In Chapter 8's two-state
example, the bad state has a real probability of 0.5 and a risk-neutral probability of 0.579.

### Step 2: the risk-neutral mean and variance of the return

Use $X = 1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}}$. Since $\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R})] = 1$, the risk-neutral mean is
$\mathbb{E}^*_t[1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}}] = 1 + \Rf{R^f_t}$. Under these odds, every asset earns the risk-free rate.
Use $X = (1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}})^2$ for the second moment. The **risk-neutral variance** is then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}^*_t(\Ret{R_{t+1}}) &= \mathbb{E}^*_t[(1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}})^2] - (1 + \Rf{R^f_t})^2 \\
&= (1 + \Rf{R^f_t})\,\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}})^2] \\
&\quad - (1 + \Rf{R^f_t})^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding 1 to a return does not change its variance, so we write $\operatorname{Var}^*_t(\Ret{R})$.

### Step 3: an identity for the risk premium

Start from the expected excess return and add and subtract $\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R})^2]$. We drop the
dates inside the expectations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_t[\Ret{R}] - \Rf{R^f_t}
&= \Big\{\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R})^2] - (1 + \Rf{R^f_t})\Big\} \\
&\quad - \Big\{\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R})^2] - \mathbb{E}_t[1 + \Ret{R}]\Big\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide the variance of Step 2 by $1 + \Rf{R^f_t}$. You get exactly the first brace. In the second brace, use
$\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R})] = 1$ to write $\mathbb{E}_t[1 + \Ret{R}]$ as
$\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R})]\,\mathbb{E}_t[1 + \Ret{R}]$. Then the brace is a covariance,
$\mathbb{E}[AB] - \mathbb{E}[A]\mathbb{E}[B]$ with $A = \SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R})$ and $B = 1 + \Ret{R}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_t[\Ret{R_{t+1}}] - \Rf{R^f_t}
&= \frac{\operatorname{Var}^*_t(\Ret{R_{t+1}})}{1 + \Rf{R^f_t}} \\
&\quad - \operatorname{Cov}_t\!\big(\SDF{M_{t+1}}(1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}}),\ \Ret{R_{t+1}}\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

This holds for any asset. The first term can be read from prices, as Step 5 shows. The second term cannot.

### Step 4: the condition, and what it rules out

Martin (2017, p. 371) calls the following the **negative correlation condition**:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}_t\!\big(\SDF{M_{t+1}}(1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}}),\ \Ret{R_{t+1}}\big) \le 0.
$$

If it holds, the second term of Step 3 is at least zero, and dropping it gives the **lower bound**

$$
\mathbb{E}_t[\Ret{R_{t+1}}] - \Rf{R^f_t} \;\ge\; \frac{\operatorname{Var}^*_t(\Ret{R_{t+1}})}{1 + \Rf{R^f_t}}.
$$

The condition is an assumption about the world, not a theorem. It rules out three kinds of case.

- **Hedge assets.** Take Chapter 8's insurance, which pays most in the bad state. Its true premium is −3.8%, but
  the bound would claim at least 5.4%. Martin (2017, p. 373) names gold and, in recent years, Treasury bonds as
  assets of this kind.
- **A risk-neutral world.** If $\SDF{M}$ is a constant, the covariance is $\SDF{M}\operatorname{Var}(\Ret{R})$,
  which is positive (p. 373). The bound needs an SDF that is volatile and falls when the market rises.
- **A risky asset with a low Sharpe ratio.** Chapter 8's stock fails too. Its premium is 4.1%, but the bound would
  say 6.3%. Its SDF is too calm for its volatility.

Why trust the condition for the market? Martin (2017, p. 374) shows that, when $\SDF{M}$ and the return are
lognormal, it is the same as "the Sharpe ratio is larger than the volatility". He puts the market's Sharpe ratio
near 50% and its volatility near 16%. He also notes that he knows no model that fits the data in which the
condition fails for the market (p. 373).

### Step 5: the variance is an area of option prices

Assume the index pays no dividends, so $1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}} = P_{t+1}/P_t$. This is a simplification: the real index
pays dividends. Martin (2017, p. 379) allows dividends that are known in advance, and a known dividend does not
change the variance of the return. Then
$\operatorname{Var}^*_t(\Ret{R}) = \mathbb{E}^*_t[P_{t+1}^2]/P_t^2 - (1 + \Rf{R^f_t})^2$. We need
$\mathbb{E}^*_t[P_{t+1}^2]$.

Here is the trick. For any $x \ge 0$, the payoff $\max(0, x - K)$ equals $x - K$ for $K$ below $x$ and zero above.
So

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\int_0^\infty \max(0, x - K)\,dK &= 2\int_0^x (x - K)\,dK \\
&= 2\Big(x^2 - \tfrac12 x^2\Big) = x^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

A square is a portfolio of calls, one at every strike. Set $x = P_{t+1}$, take $\mathbb{E}^*_t$ of both sides, and
divide by $1 + \Rf{R^f_t}$. By Step 1, $\mathbb{E}^*_t[\max(0, P_{t+1} - K)]/(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})$ is the price of the
call, $\mathrm{call}_t(K)$:

$$
\frac{\mathbb{E}^*_t[P_{t+1}^2]}{1 + \Rf{R^f_t}} = 2\int_0^\infty \mathrm{call}_t(K)\,dK.
$$

Calls with low strikes are deep in the money and trade little. **Put–call parity** swaps them for puts. A call
minus a put with the same strike pays $P_{t+1} - K$ in every state. So its price is $P_t - K/(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})$.
Write $F_t = P_t(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})$ for the **forward price** of the index. Then

$$
\mathrm{call}_t(K) = \mathrm{put}_t(K) + \frac{F_t - K}{1 + \Rf{R^f_t}}.
$$

Split the integral at $F_t$ and use parity below it. The extra term integrates to
$\int_0^{F_t}(F_t - K)\,dK = F_t^2/2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathbb{E}^*_t[P_{t+1}^2]}{1 + \Rf{R^f_t}}
&= 2\Big[\int_0^{F_t}\mathrm{put}_t(K)\,dK + \int_{F_t}^\infty \mathrm{call}_t(K)\,dK\Big] \\
&\quad + \frac{F_t^2}{1 + \Rf{R^f_t}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now divide by $P_t^2$ and subtract $1 + \Rf{R^f_t}$. Since $F_t/P_t = 1 + \Rf{R^f_t}$, the last term divided by
$P_t^2$ is exactly $1 + \Rf{R^f_t}$, and it cancels:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\operatorname{Var}^*_t(\Ret{R_{t+1}})}{1 + \Rf{R^f_t}} \\
&\quad = \frac{2}{P_t^2}\,\Prob{\Big[\int_0^{F_t}\mathrm{put}_t(K)\,dK + \int_{F_t}^\infty \mathrm{call}_t(K)\,dK\Big]}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The bracket is the shaded area in the widget. With this, the bound of Step 4 reads straight off one day's
option prices. Martin (2017, p. 382) defines an index from the same area, the **SVIX**, scaled to a year:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{SVIX}^2_t &= \frac{1}{\tau}\operatorname{Var}^*_t\!\left(\frac{1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}}}{1 + \Rf{R^f_t}}\right) \\
&= \frac{2}{\tau\,(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})\,P_t^2} \\
&\quad \times \Prob{\Big[\int_0^{F_t}\mathrm{put}_t(K)\,dK + \int_{F_t}^\infty \mathrm{call}_t(K)\,dK\Big]},
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\tau$ is the life of the options in years. The bound, per year, is $(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})\,\mathrm{SVIX}^2_t$. In the
widget, $\tau = 1$, $\mathrm{SVIX}^2 = 0.0409$ and $\Rf{R^f} = 3\%$, so the bound is $1.03 \times 0.0409$, or 4.21%.

### Step 6: variance or entropy?

The famous fear index is the **VIX**, not the SVIX. It uses the same options but weights each price by $1/K^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{VIX}^2_t &= \frac{2(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})}{\tau} \\
&\quad \times \Big[\int_0^{F_t}\frac{\mathrm{put}_t(K)}{K^2}\,dK + \int_{F_t}^\infty \frac{\mathrm{call}_t(K)}{K^2}\,dK\Big].
\end{aligned}
$$

Low strikes get the largest weights, so the VIX leans on crash insurance. What does it measure? The **entropy**
of a positive random variable $X$ is $L(X) = \log\mathbb{E}[X] - \mathbb{E}[\log X]$. The log is concave, so by
Jensen's inequality $L(X) \ge 0$, with zero only when $X$ is a constant. Like a variance, it measures spread.
Write $L_t$ with real probabilities and $L^*_t$ with risk-neutral ones. Martin (2017, p. 401) shows that
$\mathrm{VIX}^2_t = \tfrac{2}{\tau}L^*_t\big((1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}})/(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})\big)$.

<details>
<summary>Every step of the VIX–entropy link</summary>

1. For a smooth $f$, Taylor's theorem with an integral remainder, expanded at $F$, gives
   $f(x) = f(F) + f'(F)(x - F) + \int_0^F f''(K)\max(0, K - x)\,dK + \int_F^\infty f''(K)\max(0, x - K)\,dK$.
   Check it: for $x$ above $F$ only the last integral is nonzero, and it is $\int_F^x f''(K)(x - K)\,dK$, the
   usual remainder.
2. Take $f = \log$, so $f'(F) = 1/F$ and $f''(K) = -1/K^2$. Put $x = P_{t+1}$ and $F = F_t$.
3. Take $\mathbb{E}^*_t$. The linear term drops out, because $\mathbb{E}^*_t[P_{t+1}] = P_t(1 + \Rf{R^f_t}) = F_t$.
   By Step 1, $\mathbb{E}^*_t[\max(0, K - P_{t+1})] = (1 + \Rf{R^f_t})\,\mathrm{put}_t(K)$, and the same for calls.
   So $\mathbb{E}^*_t[\log P_{t+1}] = \log F_t - (1 + \Rf{R^f_t})\big[\int_0^{F_t}\mathrm{put}_t(K)/K^2\,dK + \int_{F_t}^\infty\mathrm{call}_t(K)/K^2\,dK\big]$.
4. Let $X = (1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}})/(1 + \Rf{R^f_t}) = P_{t+1}/F_t$. Then $\mathbb{E}^*_t[X] = 1$, so $\log\mathbb{E}^*_t[X] = 0$
   and $L^*_t(X) = -\mathbb{E}^*_t[\log X] = \log F_t - \mathbb{E}^*_t[\log P_{t+1}]$.
5. By step 3 this is $(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})$ times the weighted bracket. Multiply by $2/\tau$: that is $\mathrm{VIX}^2_t$.

</details>

So SVIX measures risk-neutral variance and VIX measures risk-neutral entropy. If the return is lognormal, the two
are almost the same: $\mathrm{VIX}^2 = \sigma^2$ and $\mathrm{SVIX}^2 = (e^{\sigma^2\tau} - 1)/\tau$, a little more
(Martin 2017, p. 402). For a lognormal $X$, $\log\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[\log X] + \tfrac12\operatorname{Var}(\log X)$,
so $L(X)$ is half the variance of $\log X$. When $\SDF{M}$ and the return are jointly lognormal, $\log X$ has the
same variance under both sets of odds (Martin 2017, p. 374), so $L_t$ and $L^*_t$ agree too. With a skew, they
part. The entropy weights the left tail more, so a fat left tail raises VIX above SVIX. In the widget's default smile, the VIX is 21.8% and
the SVIX is 20.2%. Martin (2017, p. 402) finds the VIX above the SVIX on every day of his sample: the market is
not lognormal, even over one month.

Which one gives the bound? The variance. Step 3 is an identity in variance, and the condition of Step 4 closes
it. The entropy gives a different identity. With real odds,
$\log\mathbb{E}_t[X] = \mathbb{E}_t[\log X] + L_t(X)$. With risk-neutral odds, $0 = \mathbb{E}^*_t[\log X] + L^*_t(X)$.
So VIX pins down the risk-neutral mean of the log return. It does not, by itself, bound the real expected return.
If you put the VIX in place of the SVIX, you get $1.03 \times 0.0477$, or 4.91%: a larger number with no
theorem behind it.

<Callout type="key">$\mathbb{E}_t[\Ret{R}] - \Rf{R^f_t} \ge \operatorname{Var}^*_t(\Ret{R})/(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})$, and
the right side is $2/P_t^2$ times the area under the out-of-the-money option prices. It needs the negative
correlation condition. The VIX measures entropy and loads on the left tail; the bound uses the SVIX.</Callout>

### What a lower bound can and cannot tell you

A lower bound is a floor, not a forecast. It says the expected excess return is *at least* this much. The true
premium may be far higher. Martin (2017, p. 382) finds that his monthly bound averaged 5.00% a year, close to
common estimates of the equity premium. He then tests whether the bound holds with equality, with predictive
regressions, and does not reject it (p. 367). A failure to reject is not proof.

The bound rests on the negative correlation condition. Option prices cannot test it, because the covariance
uses real-world odds. If you doubt the condition, you must doubt the bound.

Real strikes stop somewhere. The widget drops every option outside the strikes 30 to 300. Dropped options can
only shrink the area, so a truncated strip still gives a valid floor, just a lower one (Martin 2017, p. 381).

Above all, the widget's numbers are not estimates. They come from a toy smile that we chose, so they carry no
standard error and no sample period. They show how the machine works. Martin's numbers, with their pages, are
the evidence. Unlike the regressions of Chapter 9, his bound needs no long sample, because it reads the
forward-looking information in one day's prices. It pays for that with an assumption about the SDF.

<Callout type="warning">The bound is only as good as the negative correlation condition. It fails for hedge
assets, and it says nothing about how far above the floor the true premium lies.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- Risk-neutral odds are real odds tilted by $\SDF{M}$: $\Prob{\pi^*(s)} = \Prob{\pi(s)}\SDF{M(s)}(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})$.
  Every price is a risk-neutral expected payoff, discounted at the risk-free rate.
- An identity: $\mathbb{E}_t[\Ret{R}] - \Rf{R^f_t} = \operatorname{Var}^*_t(\Ret{R})/(1 + \Rf{R^f_t}) -
  \operatorname{Cov}_t(\SDF{M}(1 + \Ret{R}), \Ret{R})$. The negative correlation condition turns it into a lower
  bound. It fails for hedge assets.
- The risk-neutral variance is $2/P_t^2$ times the area under out-of-the-money put and call prices. The SVIX
  scales it to a year, and the annual bound is $(1 + \Rf{R^f_t})\mathrm{SVIX}^2_t$.
- The VIX weights the same options by $1/K^2$ and measures risk-neutral entropy. It leans on the left tail and
  gives no bound by itself.
- The bound grows with the square of volatility, so it spikes in crises: Martin's monthly bound went from an
  average of 1.86% in 2004–2006 to 55.0% in November 2008 (p. 385).
