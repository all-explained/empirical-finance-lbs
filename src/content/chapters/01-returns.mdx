## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> What happened to one dollar since 1926?

Suppose someone put \$1 into the US stock market in July 1926 and never touched it. They kept every
dividend, the cash a company pays to its owners, and bought more shares with it. What would that dollar be
worth in August 2026?

The answer, from the monthly data in this course: about \$19,700. The same dollar in Treasury bills, short loans
to the US government that are about as safe as an investment gets, grew to about \$25.50. Neither number
adjusts for inflation, so both overstate what the money can buy. But the gap between them is huge.

Here is a second fact that looks like a mistake. Over those 1,202 months, the stock market's average monthly
return was 0.97%. If you grow \$1 by 0.97% every month for 1,202 months, you get about \$104,000. That is
more than five times the \$19,700 the dollar really became. The average is correct, and the final value is
correct. So where did the missing \$84,000 go?

This chapter answers that question. On the way, you will learn the three kinds of return that every later
chapter uses: the simple return, the log return and the excess return.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Growth multiplies

A **return** is how much an investment gains or loses over a period, as a fraction of what you put in. If
you pay \$100 for a share, and a month later it is worth \$103, your return for the month is 3%. Returns
let you compare a \$5 share with a \$5,000 share, because they measure gains per dollar invested.

The key fact is that returns **multiply** over time. They do not add. A 10% gain on \$100 gives \$110. A
second 10% gain is 10% of \$110, not of \$100, so you end at \$121, not \$120. The extra dollar is a gain on
the earlier gain. This snowball effect is called **compounding**.

Compounding also works against you. Suppose you gain 50% in one month and lose 50% the next. The two
returns average to zero. But \$100 becomes \$150, and then half of \$150 is \$75. You lost a quarter of your
money while your average return was zero. A loss hurts more than an equal-sized gain helps, because the loss
hits a bigger pile.

That is the whole secret of the missing \$84,000. Stock returns swing up and down a lot from month to month.
Each swing is like the +50% and −50% pair, only smaller. The ups and downs cancel in the *average*, but
they do not cancel in the *final value*. So the average simple return overstates how fast your money
really grows.

labelled "T-bills", is \$1 in T-bills. The dashed line grows \$1 at the *average* monthly return, every month.

Two more things to notice. First, switch to the linear scale. On a linear scale, the stock line looks flat for
50 years and then shoots up. That is misleading: a 1% move in 1930 is a few cents, and a 1% move in 2020 is
hundreds of dollars. On a **log scale**, each step up the axis multiplies the value by 10. So equal vertical
distances mean equal *percentage* changes, and a steady growth rate looks like a straight line. For growth
over long periods, the log scale is almost always the honest picture.

Second, the T-bill line is smooth and the stock line is rough. From August 1929 to June 1932, the stock line fell by about 84%. The
reward for living with that roughness is the big gap at the right edge.

<Callout type="key">Returns multiply over time. Ups and downs that cancel in the average still cost you
money in the final value. So the average simple return is higher than the growth rate you actually get.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Three kinds of return

Now we write the pictures down. Time runs in periods $t = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, and in this course a period is
usually a month. $P_t$ is the price of an asset at the end of period $t$, and $D_{t+1}$ is the dividend it
pays during the next period.

### The simple return

The **simple return** (or net return) from $t$ to $t+1$ is what you get back, divided by what you paid,
minus one:

$$
\Ret{R_{t+1}} = \frac{P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}}{P_t} - 1 = \underbrace{\frac{P_{t+1} - P_t}{P_t}}_{\text{price change}} + \underbrace{\frac{D_{t+1}}{P_t}}_{\text{dividend yield}}.
$$

Example: you buy at $P_t = 100$, the price rises to $P_{t+1} = 108$, and you receive a dividend of
$D_{t+1} = 2$. Then $\Ret{R_{t+1}} = 110/100 - 1 = 0.10$, a 10% return: 8% from the price and 2% from the
dividend. We always write returns as decimals, so "10%" means $R = 0.10$. Return data without dividends is
called a *price-only* return. It misses the second term, and over a century it misses a lot.

The **gross return** is $1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}}$. It is the number your money is multiplied by. In the example, it is
1.10.

### Compounding over many periods

Over $k$ periods, you multiply the gross returns. Write $\Ret{R_{t \to t+k}}$ for the return over the $k$
periods after $t$. Then

$$
1 + \Ret{R_{t \to t+k}} = (1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}})(1 + \Ret{R_{t+2}}) \cdots (1 + \Ret{R_{t+k}}) = \prod_{j=1}^{k} (1 + \Ret{R_{t+j}}).
$$

The symbol $\prod$ means "multiply all of these together", as $\sum$ means "add all of these". This
product is exactly the solid lines in the widget: each month, the value of your \$1 is multiplied by that
month's gross return.

### The log return

Products are awkward. A long product of numbers close to one is hard to think about, and the statistics
you will learn in Chapter 2 work best with sums. The **log return** turns the product into a sum:

$$
\Ret{r_{t+1}} = \log(1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}}).
$$

Here $\log$ is always the natural logarithm. In the example, $\Ret{r_{t+1}} = \log 1.10 = 0.0953$, a log
return of 9.53%. For small returns the two are close: a simple return of 1% is a log return of 0.995%.
For big returns they differ a lot: a simple return of $-100\%$, a total loss, is a log return of minus
infinity.

The log of a product is the sum of the logs, $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$. Take logs of the product above:

$$
\Ret{r_{t \to t+k}} = \log(1 + \Ret{R_{t \to t+k}}) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \log(1 + \Ret{R_{t+j}}) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \Ret{r_{t+j}}.
$$

So **log returns add over time**. To get the log return over a year, add the 12 monthly log returns. To get
back to dollars, undo the log with the exponential: $1 + \Ret{R_{t \to t+k}} = e^{\Ret{r_{t \to t+k}}}$. The
"+50%, then −50%" example becomes easy. The log returns are $\log 1.5 = 0.405$ and $\log 0.5 = -0.693$. They
add to $-0.288$, and $e^{-0.288} = 0.75$. You keep 75 cents of each dollar.

### Across assets, simple returns add

Log returns win over time, but simple returns win across assets. A **portfolio** is a mix of assets. Put a
fraction $w_i$ of your money in asset $i$, with weights that add to one. Each dollar in asset $i$ becomes
$1 + \Ret{R_{i,t+1}}$ dollars. So the whole portfolio becomes $\sum_i w_i (1 + \Ret{R_{i,t+1}})$, and since
$\sum_i w_i = 1$, the portfolio's simple return is

$$
\Ret{R_{p,t+1}} = \sum_i w_i \Ret{R_{i,t+1}}.
$$

The portfolio's log return has no such formula: the log of a sum is not the sum of the logs. Take half in an
asset that gains 50% and half in one that loses 50%. The portfolio's simple return is
$0.5(0.5) + 0.5(-0.5) = 0$, and its log return is $\log 1 = 0$. But the weighted average of the two log returns
is $0.5(0.405) + 0.5(-0.693) = -0.144$, which is wrong.

<Callout type="key">Log returns add over time. Simple returns add across assets. Use log returns for long
horizons and simple returns for portfolios.</Callout>

### The excess return

An investment is only good relative to what you could get without risk. The **risk-free rate**
$\Rf{R^f_t}$ is the return on a safe asset from $t$ to $t+1$. In our data it is the one-month T-bill. It carries
the date $t$, not $t+1$, because you know it when you buy: the bill's price today fixes what it pays next month.
The **excess return** is the return above it:

$$
\Ret{R^e_{t+1}} = \Ret{R_{t+1}} - \Rf{R^f_t}, \qquad \Ret{r^e_{t+1}} = \Ret{r_{t+1}} - \Rf{r^f_t}, \quad \Rf{r^f_t} = \log(1 + \Rf{R^f_t}).
$$

The excess return is the reward for taking risk. For the US market, the average monthly excess return from July
1926 to August 2026 is 0.70%, about 8.3% a year, with a standard error of 0.15% a month (data `ff3-monthly`).
The standard error measures how far the average from one sample could be from the true mean.
Chapter 2 explains it from zero. For now, note that this average is about four and a half
standard errors above zero.

### Why the average log return is lower

Now the missing \$84,000. Compounding the average *log* return gets the final value exactly right, because log
returns add: $T$ months of $\bar r$ sum to the true total $\sum_t r_t$. Compounding the average *simple* return
does not. How far apart are the two averages?

Write $\mu = \mathbb{E}[\Ret{R}]$ for the mean of the simple return and $\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(\Ret{R})$ for
its variance, the average squared distance from the mean. For a return near zero, the log is close to a
parabola:

$$
\log(1 + R) \approx R - \tfrac12 R^2.
$$

Take the expectation, the long-run average, of both sides:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\Ret{r}] \approx \mathbb{E}[\Ret{R}] - \tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R}^2] = \mu - \tfrac12(\sigma^2 + \mu^2) \approx \mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2.
$$

<details>
<summary>Every step of the algebra</summary>

1. The Taylor series of the log around zero is $\log(1 + R) = R - \tfrac12 R^2 + \tfrac13 R^3 - \dots$.
   We keep the first two terms. The rest are small when $R$ is a few percent.
2. The expectation of a sum is the sum of the expectations, so $\mathbb{E}[R - \tfrac12 R^2] = \mathbb{E}[R] - \tfrac12 \mathbb{E}[R^2]$.
3. The variance is $\sigma^2 = \mathbb{E}[(R - \mu)^2] = \mathbb{E}[R^2] - 2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[R] + \mu^2 = \mathbb{E}[R^2] - \mu^2$.
   So $\mathbb{E}[R^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2$.
4. Substitute: $\mathbb{E}[r] \approx \mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2 - \tfrac12\mu^2$.
5. For monthly stock returns, $\mu \approx 0.01$, so $\tfrac12\mu^2 \approx 0.00005$, while
   $\tfrac12\sigma^2 \approx 0.0014$. We drop the $\mu^2$ term, which is about 30 times smaller.

</details>

So the average log return is below the average simple return by about half the variance. This is a
simplification, and we say so: it drops the cubic and higher terms, and it is only good when returns are
small. Check it on the data. From July 1926 to August 2026, the market's average simple monthly return is
$\bar R = 0.97\%$ and its standard deviation is $\hat\sigma = 5.29\%$. The formula gives
$0.97\% - \tfrac12(5.29\%)^2 = 0.97\% - 0.14\% = 0.83\%$. The actual average log return is $\bar r = 0.82\%$.
The widget's readout shows the same three numbers for any start year.

This gap of 0.14% a month looks small. But over 1,202 months it adds up. The exact gap between the two final
values is a total log return of 1.66, and $e^{1.66}$ is about 5.3. That is the "more than five times" from the Hook. The missing \$84,000 was never there:
it was the cost of volatility, hidden by the average.

<Callout type="warning">Do not grow money at the average simple return to predict long-run wealth. Volatility
makes the true growth rate lower, by about $\tfrac12\sigma^2$ per period.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What you now know

- The simple return is $\Ret{R_{t+1}} = (P_{t+1} + D_{t+1})/P_t - 1$. It includes the dividend, and the gross
  return $1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}}$ is what your money is multiplied by.
- Returns compound: over many periods you multiply gross returns. The log return
  $\Ret{r_{t+1}} = \log(1 + \Ret{R_{t+1}})$ turns that product into a sum, so log returns add over time.
- Simple returns add across assets, with the portfolio weights. Log returns do not.
- The excess return $\Ret{R^e_{t+1}} = \Ret{R_{t+1}} - \Rf{R^f_t}$ is the reward for risk. For the US market
  since 1926, it has averaged about 0.70% a month.
- The average log return is lower than the average simple return by about $\tfrac12\sigma^2$. That is why
  \$1 in stocks became about \$19,700, not \$104,000.

**Next up:** the 0.70% a month is an estimate from one century of data. Chapter 2 asks how precise it is,
and why the average return is so much harder to pin down than the volatility.
