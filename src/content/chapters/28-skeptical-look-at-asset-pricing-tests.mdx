## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> A model made of noise fits almost as well

Take the 25 portfolios sorted on size and book-to-market, monthly, from 1963-07 to 2026-08: 758 months of Kenneth
French's data. Run the two-pass regression of
[Chapter 17](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/17-cross-sectional-regressions-and-fama-macbeth/) with an intercept.
The Fama–French three-factor model (FF3) gets a cross-sectional $R^2$ of 0.663. The CAPM gets 0.078. That gap is
the usual case for FF3.

Now build a fake model. Each of its two factors is a random mix of SMB and HML, the size and value factors of
[Chapter 19](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/19-size-and-value/), plus noise. The noise is three quarters of the
factor's variance, so the factor is mostly noise. Draw 200 such models with a fixed seed and run the same two-pass
regression on each. The median $R^2$ is 0.613. In 87.5% of the draws, the fake model explains more than half of the
spread in average returns.

None of these factors was built to explain anything. So, **if a model made of noise fits the 25 portfolios almost as
well as FF3, what does a high $R^2$ on them prove?**

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Few directions, so every factor lines up

[Chapter 6](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/06-principal-components/) showed that the 25 portfolios move in very few
directions. The widget below opens on their scree plot from July 1963. The first principal component carries 83.2%
of the variance, the second 6.3% and the third 3.5%. Three directions carry almost everything, and they are close
to the market, size and value.

<PCAExplorer client:visible initialPreset="ff25-1963" initialView="scree" />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> switch to "loadings". Component 1 loads with the same sign on every
portfolio, like the market. Components 2 and 3 tilt small against big and growth against value, like SMB and
HML.</p>

Now think about a new factor and its betas. A beta is a covariance with the factor, scaled. The portfolios differ
from each other almost only through the three common directions. So any factor that moves with those directions,
even weakly, gives the 25 portfolios betas that follow the same pattern as their betas on SMB and HML. The average
returns follow that pattern too: that is why FF3 fits. So the new betas line up with the average returns, and the
$R^2$ is high.

The fit does not need the factor to be strong. A weak link with SMB and HML makes the betas small, but the second
pass simply uses a larger premium $\lambda$ to stretch them. The $R^2$ only asks whether the points line up, not how
large the slope is.

Lewellen, Nagel & Shanken (2010), LNS below, make this argument. They write that a factor needs only a weak
correlation with SMB or HML, and none with the small, idiosyncratic residuals of the portfolios (p. 2). Their page
numbers are those of the 2006 NBER working paper.

gets betas that line up with the average returns, so a high cross-sectional $R^2$ on these portfolios is a low
hurdle.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Why the fit is easy, and four fixes

### Expected returns are linear in almost any betas

Let a true model with $K$ factors $\Factor{f_t}$ price the $N$ assets. Write the returns as

$$
\Ret{R^e_t} = \mathbb{E}[R^e] + B(\Factor{f_t} - \mathbb{E}f) + \Err{\varepsilon_t},
\qquad \mathbb{E}[R^e] = B\lambda ,
$$

where row $i$ of the $N \times K$ matrix $B$ is $\beta_i'$, and $\varepsilon_t$ is uncorrelated with $f_t$. A
researcher proposes $K$ other factors $\Factor{f^P_t}$ ($P$ for "proposed"). Assume they are correlated with the
returns only through $f_t$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t, f^{P\prime}_t) = 0$ (LNS, Observation 1, p. 5).

1. The covariance of the returns with the new factors is
   $\operatorname{Cov}(R^e, f^{P\prime}) = B\operatorname{Cov}(f, f^{P\prime}) + \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon, f^{P\prime}) = B\operatorname{Cov}(f, f^{P\prime})$.
2. The betas on the new factors are that covariance times $\operatorname{Var}(f^P)^{-1}$:
   $B_P = B\,Q$, with $Q = \operatorname{Cov}(f, f^{P\prime})\operatorname{Var}(f^P)^{-1}$, a $K \times K$ matrix.
3. If the two sets of factors are correlated at all, $Q$ is invertible. Then
   $\mathbb{E}[R^e] = B\lambda = B\,Q\,Q^{-1}\lambda = B_P\lambda_P$, with $\lambda_P = Q^{-1}\lambda$.

So expected returns are exactly linear in the new betas, and the population $R^2$ is 1. A weak correlation only
makes $Q$ small and the premium $\lambda_P$ large. On the 25 portfolios, the market betas are all close to 1, so
SMB and HML carry the cross-section, and two proposed factors are enough (LNS, fn. 1, p. 5). That is why the fake
models of the Hook have two factors.

### The OLS cross-sectional $R^2$

The second pass regresses the $N$ average excess returns on a constant and the estimated betas,
$\bar R^e_i = \lambda_0 + \hat\beta_i'\lambda + \alpha_i$. Its $R^2$ compares the squared pricing errors with the
spread of the average returns around their cross-sectional mean $\bar{\bar R}^e = \tfrac1N\sum_i \bar R^e_i$:

$$
R^2_{\text{OLS}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_i \Err{\hat\alpha_i}^2}{\sum_i \big(\Data{\bar R^e_i} - \bar{\bar R}^e\big)^2} .
$$

It counts every asset equally. A noisy portfolio and a quiet one get the same weight, and the $R^2$ does not ask
whether the premia make economic sense.

### The GLS $R^2$ and the mean–variance frontier

GLS, as in Chapter 17, weights the pricing errors by $\Sigma^{-1}$, the inverse covariance matrix of the returns
(here the sample one, with divisor $T$). It chooses $\lambda_0$ and $\lambda$ to minimise
$\alpha'\Sigma^{-1}\alpha$. Its $R^2$ compares that minimum with what a constant alone leaves:

$$
R^2_{\text{GLS}} = 1 - \frac{\Err{\hat\alpha}'\Sigma^{-1}\Err{\hat\alpha}}
{(\bar R^e - \mu_{\text{gmv}}\iota)'\Sigma^{-1}(\bar R^e - \mu_{\text{gmv}}\iota)} ,
$$

where $\iota$ is the $N$-vector of ones and $\mu_{\text{gmv}} = \iota'\Sigma^{-1}\bar R^e / \iota'\Sigma^{-1}\iota$
is the mean excess return of the global minimum-variance portfolio. This $R^2$ has a clean reading in mean–variance
terms (LNS, pp. 18–19, building on Kandel and Stambaugh). Measure Sharpe ratios against the zero-beta rate:
$SR^2(\lambda_0) = (\mathbb{E}[R^e] - \lambda_0)^2/\sigma^2$, for a portfolio whose weights sum to one.

1. For a portfolio $w$ with $\iota'w = 1$, write $x = \bar R^e - \lambda_0\iota$. Then
   $SR^2(\lambda_0) = (w'x)^2 / w'\Sigma w$.
2. The highest value over all portfolios is $SR^2_{\text{tan}}(\lambda_0) = x'\Sigma^{-1}x$, reached at
   $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}x$, the tangency portfolio (proof in the box below).
3. Fix $\lambda_0$ and choose $\lambda$. The GLS minimum of $(x - \hat\beta\lambda)'\Sigma^{-1}(x - \hat\beta\lambda)$ is
   $x'\Sigma^{-1}x - x'\Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta(\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}x$.
4. The second term is $SR^2_f(\lambda_0)$, the highest squared Sharpe ratio among the factors' mimicking
   portfolios: the portfolios $w = \Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta a$, whose covariances with the assets, $\Sigma w = \hat\beta a$,
   are combinations of the betas.
5. So at the GLS zero-beta rate $\hat\lambda_0$, the numerator is
   $\hat\alpha'\Sigma^{-1}\hat\alpha = SR^2_{\text{tan}}(\hat\lambda_0) - SR^2_f(\hat\lambda_0)$: the squared Sharpe
   ratio the model leaves on the table.
6. With no factors, step 3 has no second term, and the best $\lambda_0$ minimises $x'\Sigma^{-1}x$. Setting its
   derivative $-2\iota'\Sigma^{-1}(\bar R^e - \lambda_0\iota)$ to zero gives $\lambda_0 = \mu_{\text{gmv}}$. That is
   the denominator.

<details>
<summary>The algebra of steps 2 to 4</summary>

Step 2. Put $v = \Sigma^{1/2}w$. Then $(w'x)^2 = \big(v'\Sigma^{-1/2}x\big)^2 \le (v'v)(x'\Sigma^{-1}x)$ by the
Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and $v'v = w'\Sigma w$. So $(w'x)^2/w'\Sigma w \le x'\Sigma^{-1}x$, with equality when
$v \propto \Sigma^{-1/2}x$, that is $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}x$. The ratio does not change when $w$ is rescaled, so
the bound holds for weights that sum to one.

Step 3. The GLS slope is $\hat\lambda = (\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}x$. Expand the
quadratic at $\hat\lambda$: the cross term is $-2\hat\lambda'\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}x$ and the square term is
$\hat\lambda'\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta\hat\lambda = \hat\lambda'\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}x$. Together they give
$-x'\Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta(\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}x$.

Step 4. For $w = \Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta a$, $w'x = a'\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}x$ and
$w'\Sigma w = a'\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta a$. Apply step 2 to the vector $a$, with
$\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta$ in place of $\Sigma$ and $\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}x$ in place of $x$. The highest
value is the second term of step 3.

</details>

So $R^2_{\text{GLS}}$ measures how close the model's mimicking portfolios come to the mean–variance frontier. It is
1 only if some mix of them sits on the frontier, and 0 if they do no better than a constant. The OLS $R^2$ has no
such link. A random mix of SMB,
HML and noise can line its betas up with the average returns and still have mimicking portfolios far from the
frontier. LNS find population GLS $R^2$s far below the OLS ones for artificial factors (Figure 4, pp. 19–20).

### Traded factors must price themselves

When a factor $f_{k,t}$ is itself an excess return, like the market, SMB or HML, the model must also price it. In a
regression on all $K$ factors, the beta of $f_k$ is 1 on itself and 0 on the others. Put the factor into
$\mathbb{E}[R^e_i] = \lambda_0 + \beta_i'\lambda$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\Factor{f_k}] = \lambda_0 + \lambda_k .
$$

The model also says that $\lambda_0 = 0$, because these are excess returns. So $\lambda = \mathbb{E}[f]$: each premium
must equal the factor's mean excess return. A free second pass ignores this, and that is what lets the proposed
factors fit. Take one true factor and one proposed factor $f^P_t$ that is a traded excess return (LNS, p. 6):

1. From step 2 above, $Q = \operatorname{Cov}(f, f^P)/\operatorname{Var}(f^P)$, so the free premium is
   $\lambda_P = \lambda/Q = \lambda\operatorname{Var}(f^P)/\operatorname{Cov}(f, f^P)$.
2. The true model prices $f^P$ too: $\mathbb{E}[f^P] = \lambda\operatorname{Cov}(f^P, f)/\operatorname{Var}(f)$.
3. Divide step 1 by step 2:
   $\lambda_P/\mathbb{E}[f^P] = \operatorname{Var}(f)\operatorname{Var}(f^P)/\operatorname{Cov}(f, f^P)^2 = 1/\operatorname{corr}(f, f^P)^2$.

So the free premium is larger than the factor's mean unless the correlation is 1. The restriction
$\lambda_P = \mathbb{E}[f^P]$ would reject every proposed factor that is not the true one. Following LNS
(Prescription 4, p. 21), we add the factors to the test assets and test $\hat\lambda - \bar f = 0$ and
$\hat\lambda_0 = 0$.

The standard errors come from the monthly slopes, as in Fama–MacBeth. Under the restriction, the month-$t$ slope
minus the factor's own return, $\hat\lambda_t - f_t$, is a fixed mix of that month's residuals $\varepsilon_t$: the
factor shock cancels. The betas are estimated, so we scale the standard errors by $\sqrt{1 + \zeta}$, the Shanken
correction of [Chapter 18](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/18-errors-in-variables-and-shanken/), with
$\zeta = \bar f'\Sigma_f^{-1}\bar f$.

### A confidence interval for $R^2$

A sample $R^2$ is one draw from a wide sampling distribution. To see its spread, we bootstrap months: draw $T$
months with replacement, keep each month's returns and factors together, rerun both passes and record the $R^2$.
Repeat 100 times with a fixed seed. The 95% interval runs from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of the 100 values.

This interval is centred on the sample $R^2$, which is biased up (LNS, p. 21). So read it as the sampling spread,
not as a range for the true $R^2$. LNS go one step further. They simulate the sample $R^2$ for each candidate true
$R^2$ and keep the true values that could have produced the one they see (pp. 21–22).

Plainly, the four fixes say this.

1. **More assets.** With the industries, FF3's $R^2$ falls from 0.663 to 0.221, and its interval reaches down to
   0.035.
2. **Traded factors.** On the 25 portfolios, the CAPM's premium is $-3.9\%$ a year, against a market mean of 7.2%:
   a gap of $-11.1\%$ with $t = -2.54$. FF3's premia are close to the factor means, with gaps of $-0.9\%$, $-0.2\%$
   and $0.4\%$ a year. The market gap is small but precise ($t = -2.72$); the others are not significant ($t$ of
   $-0.65$ and $1.55$). On the 55 assets, the gaps grow to $-3.3\%$, $-0.8\%$ and $-1.7\%$ ($t$ of $-3.60$, $-2.13$
   and $-3.59$). Every $\hat\lambda_0$ in the table is more than two standard errors from zero.
3. **GLS.** FF3's $R^2_{\text{GLS}}$ is 0.204 on the 25 portfolios and 0.076 on the 55: its mimicking portfolios stay
   far from the frontier.
4. **Intervals.** The CAPM's interval on the 25 portfolios runs from 0.000 to 0.536, and FF3's from 0.367 to 0.838.
   One sample $R^2$ says little.

FF3 beats the CAPM on every line. But no line says that FF3 prices these assets: its premia and zero-beta rate fail
on the 55 assets, and most of its high $R^2$ is the factor structure of the 25 portfolios.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- The 25 size–value portfolios move in few directions. Any factor that touches them, even weakly, has betas that
  line up with the average returns, so a high $R^2$ on them is a low hurdle.
- On our data, two-factor models of SMB, HML and 75% noise have a median OLS $R^2$ of 0.613, against FF3's 0.663.
- Four fixes make the test harder: add other assets, ask traded factors to price themselves ($\lambda = \mathbb{E}[f]$,
  $\lambda_0 = 0$), report $R^2_{\text{GLS}}$, which measures distance to the frontier, and give $R^2$ an interval.
- With these fixes, FF3 is better than the CAPM but still rejected: its premia drift from the factor means on 55
  assets, and its zero-beta rates are not zero.

**Next up:** Chapter 29, *Multiple testing*, asks what happens when hundreds of researchers try hundreds of
factors, and how high a $t$-statistic must be before you believe one. Chapter 30, *Shrinking the cross-section*,
then estimates many factor premia at once.
