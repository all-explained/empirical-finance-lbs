## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> A signal too faint to see

Take the 96 years of real consumption per person in fred-consumption-annual, 1930 to 2025, the sample of
Chapter 35. Growth averaged 1.83% a year, with a standard deviation of 2.17%. It has a little memory: its
first-order autocorrelation is 0.36. But a good part of that comes from the way the national accounts average
consumption over the year. [Chapter 36](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/36-measuring-consumption/) showed that
averaging alone pushes this autocorrelation toward 1/4.

Now suppose that expected growth is not constant. Suppose it drifts, a little above normal for a few years, then a
little below. In the calibration of Bansal, Kiku & Yaron (2012), this slow part is only 2.8% of the variance of
monthly growth. Next month's growth then has a correlation of 0.028 with this month's. No test on a century of data
could reliably find that. Yet in the same model it earns 2.05 of the 6.42 percentage points of the equity premium.

So here is the question. Can a part of consumption growth that you can hardly see in the data be what makes stocks
risky? And if it is that hard to see, how would you ever test it?

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Small, slow, and priced

Write monthly consumption growth as a mean, plus a slow part $\Signal{x_t}$, plus noise. The slow part is an AR(1),
as in Chapter 5. In the calibration of Bansal, Kiku & Yaron, which Beeler & Campbell (2012) call BKY, its persistence
is $\phi_x = 0.975$ a month, a half-life of 27.4 months. The older calibration of Bansal and Yaron (2004), called BY,
has $\phi_x = 0.979$, a half-life of 32.7 months (Beeler & Campbell, working paper, pdf p. 34).

The widget draws 100 seeded years of log consumption, less its trend. The solid line includes $\Signal{x_t}$. The
dashed line uses the very same shocks without it.

<LongRunRisk client:visible />

Month by month, the two lines move together: the noise is the same, and $\Signal{x_t}$ adds a tiny drift. But the
drift lasts for years, so it adds up. In seed 1, the two lines drift up to 0.29 log points apart.
A small, persistent drift is a large change in the level of consumption, decades out.

The lower panel measures this with the **variance ratio**: the variance of growth over $k$ years, divided by $k$ times
the variance of one year's growth. White noise gives 1 at every horizon. Small positive autocorrelations that last
make the ratio climb. For the model, over a 10,000-year path, it reaches 2.63 at 10 years. For our data it is 1.61.

Why would an investor care about a drift they hardly see? Because in this model the investor *does* see it. The
agent knows $\Signal{x_t}$; only we, with our short sample, do not. And with the right preferences, news about growth
far in the future is feared in itself. A stock whose dividends fall when long-run growth falls pays badly exactly in
those states. That is long-run risk.

<Callout type="key">A persistent part of expected growth is small month by month but large over decades. With
Epstein–Zin preferences, its shocks carry their own price of risk.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Preferences, dynamics, solution

### Two parameters for two attitudes

Power utility ties two things together. Risk aversion $\gamma$ says how much you dislike uncertainty across states.
The elasticity of intertemporal substitution $\psi$ says how willingly you shift consumption across time when the
interest rate moves. Under power utility $\psi = 1/\gamma$, so a $\gamma$ of 10 forces $\psi = 0.1$, and a saver who
barely substitutes demands a high rate to save. That is the risk-free rate puzzle of Chapter 35.

Epstein–Zin preferences, used by Bansal, Kiku & Yaron (2012, working paper, pdf p. 6) after Epstein and Zin (1989),
cut that tie. Your value $V_t$ mixes today's consumption with a certainty equivalent of tomorrow's value:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_t^{1-1/\psi} &= (1 - \delta)\,C_t^{1-1/\psi} + \delta\,\mathit{CE}_t^{1-1/\psi}, \\
\mathit{CE}_t &= \left(\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[V_{t+1}^{1-\gamma}\right]\right)^{1/(1-\gamma)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The certainty equivalent bends risk with $\gamma$. The first line bends time with $\psi$. Set $\gamma = 1/\psi$ and
the two curvatures agree: that is power utility.

### The log SDF

Write $\theta_{\text{EZ}} = (1 - \gamma)/(1 - 1/\psi)$ and $\Ret{r_{c,t+1}}$ for the log return on the claim to all
future consumption, which is aggregate wealth. The log SDF is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\SDF{m_{t+1}} = {} & \theta_{\text{EZ}}\log\delta - \frac{\theta_{\text{EZ}}}{\psi}\Delta c_{t+1} \\
& + (\theta_{\text{EZ}} - 1)\Ret{r_{c,t+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Under power utility, $\theta_{\text{EZ}} = 1$ and the return drops out: $\SDF{m_{t+1}} = \log\delta - \gamma\Delta c_{t+1}$,
the SDF of Chapter 35. With $\gamma = 10$ and $\psi = 1.5$, as in both calibrations, $\theta_{\text{EZ}} = -27$. Then the
return on wealth enters with weight $-28$: a fall in wealth is a bad state in itself.

<details>
<summary>Derivation of the log SDF, step by step</summary>

1. The SDF is the ratio of marginal values, $\SDF{M_{t+1}} = (\partial V_t/\partial C_{t+1})/(\partial V_t/\partial C_t)$,
   per unit of probability $\Prob{\pi(s)}$. Differentiate the first line: $\partial V_t/\partial C_t = (1-\delta)V_t^{1/\psi}C_t^{-1/\psi}$
   and $\partial V_t/\partial\mathit{CE}_t = \delta V_t^{1/\psi}\mathit{CE}_t^{-1/\psi}$.
2. Differentiate the certainty equivalent: $\partial\mathit{CE}_t/\partial V_{t+1}(s) = \Prob{\pi(s)}\,\mathit{CE}_t^{\gamma}V_{t+1}(s)^{-\gamma}$.
   Then $\partial V_{t+1}/\partial C_{t+1} = (1-\delta)V_{t+1}^{1/\psi}C_{t+1}^{-1/\psi}$. Chain the three:
   $\SDF{M_{t+1}} = \delta (C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-1/\psi}(V_{t+1}/\mathit{CE}_t)^{1/\psi - \gamma}$.
3. $V_t$ is homogeneous of degree one in the whole consumption stream, so $V_t$ equals the sum of each future
   consumption times its marginal value. Divide by $\partial V_t/\partial C_t$: the price of the claim to all
   consumption after today, $P_{c,t}$, satisfies $P_{c,t} + C_t = V_t^{1-1/\psi}C_t^{1/\psi}/(1-\delta)$.
4. Put this in the first line of the preferences: $\delta\,\mathit{CE}_t^{1-1/\psi} = (1-\delta)P_{c,t}C_t^{-1/\psi}$.
   The return on the claim, $(P_{c,t+1} + C_{t+1})/P_{c,t}$, is then
   $e^{\Ret{r_{c,t+1}}} = \delta^{-1}(V_{t+1}/\mathit{CE}_t)^{1-1/\psi}(C_{t+1}/C_t)^{1/\psi}$.
5. Solve step 4 for $(V_{t+1}/\mathit{CE}_t)^{1-1/\psi}$ and raise it to the power
   $(1/\psi - \gamma)/(1 - 1/\psi) = \theta_{\text{EZ}} - 1$. Put that into step 2 and take logs.

</details>

### The dynamics

The model runs in months, with four independent standard normal shocks:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta c_{t+1} &= \mu_c + \Signal{x_t} + \sigma_t\eta_{t+1}, \\
\Signal{x_{t+1}} &= \phi_x\Signal{x_t} + \varphi_e\sigma_t e_{t+1}, \\
\sigma^2_{t+1} &= \bar\sigma^2 + \phi_\sigma(\sigma^2_t - \bar\sigma^2) + \sigma_w w_{t+1}, \\
\Delta d_{t+1} &= \mu_d + \beta_{d,x}\Signal{x_t} + \beta_{d,\eta}\sigma_t\eta_{t+1} \\
&\quad + \varphi_d\sigma_t u_{t+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\bar\sigma^2$ is a parameter, the long-run mean of $\sigma^2_t$, not a sample mean. Volatility itself moves
slowly, with $\phi_\sigma = 0.999$ in BKY. Dividends load on $\Signal{x_t}$ with leverage $\beta_{d,x} = 2.5$ (BKY) or
3 (BY), so the stock market feels long-run growth more than consumption does.

### The log-linear solution

Linearise the return on wealth as in [Chapter 10](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/10-campbell-shiller-decomposition/),
with the log price–consumption ratio $\Signal{pc_t}$ in place of $\Signal{pd_t}$:
$\Ret{r_{c,t+1}} \approx \kappa_c + \rho_c\Signal{pc_{t+1}} + \Delta c_{t+1} - \Signal{pc_t}$. Guess that the ratio
is linear in the two states, $\Signal{pc_t} = A_0 + A_1\Signal{x_t} + A_2\sigma^2_t$.

Price wealth with its own return: $\mathbb{E}_t[e^{\SDF{m_{t+1}} + \Ret{r_{c,t+1}}}] = 1$. The exponent is

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \SDF{m_{t+1}} + \Ret{r_{c,t+1}} \\
& \quad = \theta_{\text{EZ}}\big[\log\delta + \kappa_c + (1 - 1/\psi)\Delta c_{t+1} \\
& \qquad + \rho_c\Signal{pc_{t+1}} - \Signal{pc_t}\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

Given the states, it is normal, so its mean plus half its variance must be zero, whatever $\Signal{x_t}$ is. Only
the mean depends on $\Signal{x_t}$. Collect its $\Signal{x_t}$ terms: $(1 - 1/\psi)$ from growth,
$\rho_c A_1\phi_x$ from the next ratio and $-A_1$ from today's. They must cancel:

$$
A_1 = \frac{1 - 1/\psi}{1 - \rho_c\phi_x}.
$$

The $\sigma^2_t$ terms give $A_2$, and the constants give $A_0$, in the same way (Bansal, Kiku & Yaron, pdf p. 8).
Each claim's $\rho_c$ and $\kappa_c$ come from its own mean ratio, so the library solves for them together. In BKY,
$\rho_c = 0.9987$ and $A_1 = 12.70$.

The sign of $A_1$ is the sign of $1 - 1/\psi$. Good news about growth does two things. It raises future cash flows,
which raises prices. It also raises interest rates, here by $1/\psi = 0.67$ times $\Signal{x_t}$, which lowers them.
With $\psi > 1$ the first effect wins, so prices rise with $\Signal{x_t}$ (Bansal, Kiku & Yaron, pdf p. 8). At
$\psi = 1$ the two cancel (Beeler & Campbell, pdf p. 3). Below 1, good news lowers the price of wealth.

### Three prices of risk

The surprise in $\SDF{m_{t+1}}$ comes from the surprises in $\Delta c_{t+1}$ and $\Ret{r_{c,t+1}}$, and so from the three
shocks that move them. Collect them:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\SDF{m_{t+1}} - \mathbb{E}_t\SDF{m_{t+1}} = {} & -\lambda_\eta\sigma_t\eta_{t+1} \\
& - \lambda_e\sigma_t e_{t+1} \\
& - \lambda_w\sigma_w w_{t+1},
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\lambda_\eta = \gamma$, $\lambda_e = (1 - \theta_{\text{EZ}})\rho_c A_1\varphi_e$ and
$\lambda_w = (1 - \theta_{\text{EZ}})\rho_c A_2$ (Bansal, Kiku & Yaron, pdf p. 9).

<details>
<summary>Where the three prices come from</summary>

The surprise in $\Ret{r_{c,t+1}}$ is $\sigma_t\eta_{t+1} + \rho_c A_1\varphi_e\sigma_t e_{t+1} + \rho_c A_2\sigma_w w_{t+1}$:
growth, plus the change in the next ratio. In $\SDF{m_{t+1}}$, $\eta_{t+1}$ enters through growth with weight
$-\theta_{\text{EZ}}/\psi$ and through the return with weight $\theta_{\text{EZ}} - 1$. The sum is
$\theta_{\text{EZ}}(1 - 1/\psi) - 1 = (1 - \gamma) - 1 = -\gamma$. The other two shocks enter only through the
return, with weight $\theta_{\text{EZ}} - 1$.

</details>

Under power utility $\theta_{\text{EZ}} = 1$, so $\lambda_e = \lambda_w = 0$: only this month's consumption shock is
priced. Since $(1 - \theta_{\text{EZ}})(1 - 1/\psi) = \gamma - 1/\psi$, the price of long-run risk $\lambda_e$ is
positive only when $\gamma > 1/\psi$. In BKY, $\lambda_\eta = 10$, $\lambda_e = 13.5$ and $\lambda_w$ is about
$-57{,}000$. That last number multiplies the tiny $\sigma_w = 2.8 \times 10^{-6}$; its sign says that a rise in
volatility is a bad state.

### The equity premium

The market's log return has surprises $\beta_{m,\eta}\sigma_t\eta_{t+1} + \beta_{m,e}\sigma_t e_{t+1} +
\beta_{m,w}\sigma_w w_{t+1}$ plus its own dividend shock. Write the pricing rule for the market and for the
risk-free asset in logs, as in Chapter 35, and subtract:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathbb{E}_t[\Ret{r_{m,t+1}} - \Rf{r^f_t}] + \tfrac12\operatorname{Var}_t(\Ret{r_{m,t+1}}) \\
& \quad = \beta_{m,\eta}\lambda_\eta\sigma^2_t + \beta_{m,e}\lambda_e\sigma^2_t \\
& \qquad + \beta_{m,w}\lambda_w\sigma^2_w.
\end{aligned}
$$

The market's beta on long-run news is $\beta_{m,e} = \rho_m A_{1,m}\varphi_e$, with
$A_{1,m} = (\beta_{d,x} - 1/\psi)/(1 - \rho_m\phi_x)$: leverage makes it large (Bansal, Kiku & Yaron, pdf p. 23).

At $\sigma^2_t = \bar\sigma^2$, BKY gives a premium of 6.42% a year and a risk-free rate of 1.28%. The three terms are
1.62, 2.05 and 2.76 points: 25%, 32% and 43% of the premium, the split Bansal, Kiku & Yaron report (pdf p. 12). BY
gives 5.54% and 2.58%. Now keep BY's $\gamma = 10$ and set $\psi = 1/\gamma$, power utility. The premium falls to
0.00% and the risk-free rate jumps to 16.75%. Both puzzles of Chapter 35 come back.

In the language of [Chapter 11](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/11-cash-flow-and-discount-rate-news/), $\Signal{x_t}$ moves
cash-flow news and $\sigma^2_t$ moves discount-rate news. BKY's slower volatility gives discount rates a larger role
than BY (Beeler & Campbell, pdf pp. 4–5).

<Callout type="note" title="The symbols">
The curriculum writes $\mu$, $\theta$, $\rho$ and $\varphi_e$; the papers also write $\kappa_1$. Here the mean is
$\mu_c$, as in Chapter 35. $\theta_{\text{EZ}}$ keeps its subscript because $\theta$ is the GMM parameter vector, the
persistence is $\phi_x$ because $\rho$ is a Campbell–Shiller constant, and $\kappa_1$ is $\rho_c$, the Chapter 10
constant of the consumption claim. $\varphi_e$ stays, always with its subscript, so it never reads as the filter
weight $\varphi$ of Chapter 36. All rates are
log real rates; annual figures are 12 times the monthly ones. The premium includes the half variance, as in
Bansal, Kiku & Yaron's eq. (10), pdf p. 9. Both papers are 2012 *Critical Finance Review* articles; pages are those
of their working papers.
</Callout>

### Can the data see it?

Two tests. First, the variance ratio. If $\Signal{x_t}$ is there, long-horizon growth should vary more than short
horizons suggest. Our data give $\mathit{VR}(10) = 1.61$; the model's 10,000-year path gives 2.63 for BKY and 3.26 for
BY. But one century is short. In 1,000 seeded samples of 96 years, 21.3% of BKY samples and 8.3% of BY samples have
a $\mathit{VR}(10)$ at or below 1.61. The ratio cannot tell BKY from the data.

Second, the test of Beeler & Campbell (pdf pp. 12–16). If prices rise with $\Signal{x_t}$, the price–dividend ratio
should forecast consumption growth. Regress growth over the next $k$ years on $\Signal{pd_t}$. On our data, 1930 to
2025, the one-year slope is 0.006 ($t = 0.80$, Newey–West with $k$ lags) and the $R^2$ is 0.018. At five years the
slope is $-0.025$ ($t = -1.61$) and the $R^2$ 0.068, with the wrong sign. In the model's long path, the one-year
$R^2$ is 0.387 for BY and 0.043 for BKY.

Beeler & Campbell report population $R^2$ of 0.390 at one year and 0.373 at five for BY, against 0.068 and 0.000
in their data, 1930–2006 (theirs, Table VI, pdf p. 41). Over their years, our data give 0.072 at one year. In our
1,000 seeded samples, no BY sample has a one-year $R^2$ as low as our 0.018. For BKY, 18.7% do.

Bansal, Kiku & Yaron answer that the price–dividend ratio mixes two states, so it is a weak proxy for
$\Signal{x_t}$. With the risk-free rate added in a VAR, they find a consumption $R^2$ of 26% at one year in their data
(theirs, pdf pp. 12–13).

<Callout type="warning" title="What the evidence can and cannot show">
The data reject BY's large, visible $\Signal{x_t}$. They do not reject BKY, but only because BKY makes $\Signal{x_t}$
small and gives volatility much of the work. The tests have little power: a 96-year sample is short next to a
signal with a half-life of two years and a share of 2.8% of the variance. The estimates are point values with
Newey–West standard errors from one sample, and the 1,000 seeded samples assume the model is true.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- Epstein–Zin preferences give risk aversion $\gamma$ and the elasticity $\psi$ separate jobs, and add the return on
  wealth to the SDF with weight $\theta_{\text{EZ}} - 1$.
- A small, persistent $\Signal{x_t}$ is priced at $\lambda_e = (1 - \theta_{\text{EZ}})\rho_c A_1\varphi_e$, which is
  positive only when $\gamma > 1/\psi$.
- With $\psi > 1$, prices rise with expected growth, and leverage makes the market's beta on long-run news large.
- BKY gives a premium of 6.42% and a risk-free rate of 1.28%; power utility gives almost no premium.
- Our data reject BY's visible $\Signal{x_t}$ but cannot reject BKY's faint one: the evidence is weak either way.
