## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Is a characteristic a risk or a free lunch?

Small stocks and cheap stocks have earned more, on average, than large and expensive ones. Chapter 17 showed one
way to measure this: regress each month's returns on the stocks' characteristics, and average the slopes. That
measures *how much* a characteristic predicts. It does not say *why*.

There are two stories. In the first, a characteristic is a sign of risk. A cheap stock moves strongly with some
economy-wide factor, so it is risky, and it must pay more. In the second, the characteristic predicts returns for
some other reason, such as a pricing mistake that investors are slow to correct. Then the extra return is an alpha, a
reward without a risk behind it.

The title of this chapter is the first story in four words. Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) built a method, instrumented
principal components analysis (IPCA), that lets the data choose between the two stories. In their data, 36
characteristics of 12,813 US firms from July 1962 to May 2014 (p. 23), four IPCA factors leave little for the
alpha story. With the alphas held at zero, the four factors explain 19.4% of the variation in monthly stock
returns, and 1.8% of it through expected returns. On the same stocks the five factors of Fama and French explain
21.9% and only 0.3% (pp. 5–6). Those are the paper's numbers, from licensed firm data that this site does not
have. This chapter builds the method on a simulated panel, where we know the truth.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Betas that follow the characteristics

Think about one firm over ten years. It starts small and cheap. It grows, and its price rises faster than its book
value. If a firm's risk comes from what kind of firm it is, then its beta should change as it changes. A beta
estimated from its past returns would always lag behind.

IPCA makes one assumption that removes this problem. A firm's betas are a fixed linear function of its
characteristics. The same map turns any firm's characteristics into its betas, in every month. So the model does not
estimate a beta for each firm. It estimates one small table: one row for each characteristic and one column for
each factor. Each entry says how much one unit of that characteristic adds to the beta on that factor. When a firm's
characteristics change, its betas change with them, at once.

The factors themselves are latent. Nobody tells the model that one of them is "value". The model finds the factors
and the table together, so that the betas fit the returns as well as possible. You can think of an ordinary
principal components analysis (Chapter 6) with one extra rule: each stock's loadings must come from its
characteristics.

Next, the alpha. The model can also give each firm an alpha that is a linear function of its characteristics. If
the characteristics predict returns only through the betas, that second map is zero. If they predict returns in
some other way, the alpha map must pick up what the betas miss. The test of this chapter asks whether the alpha map
is zero.

There is one more idea. A characteristic gives a simple way to build a portfolio: each month, hold each stock in
proportion to its characteristic. With size, you hold more of the large stocks and you go short the small ones. These
**characteristic-managed portfolios** are the managed portfolios of Chapter 22, with a characteristic in the place of
the state variable. The whole fit uses only their returns, with one small matrix for each month. It never needs the
return of a single firm.

<Callout type="key">IPCA lets betas move with characteristics through one fixed map, and asks whether
characteristics predict returns beyond their effect on betas.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> The model, its fit and its test

### The model

Let $z_{it}$ be the $\dim z$ characteristics of firm $i$ known at $t$, with a constant 1 first. The
paper writes $L$ for $\dim z$; here $L$ counts Newey–West lags. The excess return of firm $i$ from $t$ to $t+1$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Ret{R^e_{i,t+1}} &= \alpha_{it} + \beta_{it}'\Factor{f_{t+1}} + \Err{\varepsilon_{i,t+1}}, \\
\beta_{it} &= \Gamma_\beta' z_{it}, \qquad \alpha_{it} = z_{it}'\Gamma_\alpha.
\end{aligned}
$$

$\Gamma_\beta$ is the $\dim z \times K$ table of the Intuition, and $\Gamma_\alpha$ is a $\dim z$-vector (p. 9).
Stack the $N$ firms of month $t$ in the $N \times \dim z$ matrix $Z_t$, whose row $i$ is $z_{it}'$:

$$
\Ret{R^e_{t+1}} = Z_t\Gamma_\alpha + Z_t\Gamma_\beta\Factor{f_{t+1}} + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}.
$$

The **restricted model** sets $\Gamma_\alpha = 0$. Then, if the factor means are $\lambda$, a firm's expected excess
return is $z_{it}'\Gamma_\beta\lambda$. Characteristics move expected returns only through betas.

### Why ranks

Raw characteristics have awkward units and long tails. One firm with a huge book-to-market ratio could steer the
whole fit. So each month, each characteristic except the constant is replaced by its rank across firms, divided by
the number of firms with data, less 0.5 (p. 23). The values then lie in $[-0.5, 0.5]$ in every month. The paper does this to
limit the effect of outliers (p. 46). It also keeps the scale of the characteristics the same over time, so one map
$\Gamma_\beta$ can serve every month.

### The fit needs only managed portfolios

IPCA chooses $\Gamma_\beta$ and the factors to minimise the sum of squared errors over all firms and months
(p. 12). For the restricted model,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\Gamma_\beta,\,f}\ \sum_{t}\ &\big(\Ret{R^e_{t+1}} - Z_t\Gamma_\beta\Factor{f_{t+1}}\big)' \\
&\times \big(\Ret{R^e_{t+1}} - Z_t\Gamma_\beta\Factor{f_{t+1}}\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Fix $\Gamma_\beta$. Then month $t$ is an OLS regression of the $N$ returns on the $N \times K$ betas
$Z_t\Gamma_\beta$. The OLS formula, with $Z_t\Gamma_\beta$ as the regressors, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Factor{f_{t+1}} &= \big(\Gamma_\beta' Z_t'Z_t\Gamma_\beta\big)^{-1}\Gamma_\beta'\,\Ret{R^Z_{t+1}}, \\
\Ret{R^Z_{t+1}} &= Z_t'\Ret{R^e_{t+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$R^Z_{t+1}$ holds the returns of the $\dim z$ **managed portfolios** (p. 14). Entry $l$ weights each firm's return
by its $l$-th characteristic. The paper writes $x_{t+1}$ for it and $W_t$ for $Z_t'Z_t$. Here $x_t$ is a predictor
and $W$ is a weighting matrix, so we keep the long names.

Now fix the factors. The fitted values are linear in $\Gamma_\beta$, so this step is one pooled OLS regression too.
Its solution again uses only $R^Z_{t+1}$ and $Z_t'Z_t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_\beta) = {}&\Big(\sum_t \Factor{f_{t+1}}\Factor{f_{t+1}}' \otimes Z_t'Z_t\Big)^{-1} \\
&\times \sum_t \Factor{f_{t+1}} \otimes \Ret{R^Z_{t+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>The algebra: the pooled regression for $\Gamma_\beta$</summary>

$\operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_\beta)$ stacks the $K$ columns of $\Gamma_\beta$ into one long vector. The $N$ fitted values
$Z_t\Gamma_\beta f_{t+1}$ add up column $k$ of $\Gamma_\beta$, times $Z_t$, times factor $k$. So they are linear in
$\operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_\beta)$:

$$
Z_t\Gamma_\beta f_{t+1} = (f_{t+1}' \otimes Z_t)\operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_\beta).
$$

So month $t$ adds the $N \times K\dim z$ block of regressors $f_{t+1}' \otimes Z_t$, and pooled OLS gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_\beta) \\
&\ = \Big(\sum_t (f_{t+1}' \otimes Z_t)'(f_{t+1}' \otimes Z_t)\Big)^{-1} \\
&\quad \times \sum_t (f_{t+1}' \otimes Z_t)'R^e_{t+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The transpose of a Kronecker product is the product of the transposes, and Kronecker products multiply part by
part: the left parts together and the right parts together. So the first sum is small:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(f_{t+1}' \otimes Z_t)'(f_{t+1}' \otimes Z_t) \\
&\ = (f_{t+1} \otimes Z_t')(f_{t+1}' \otimes Z_t) \\
&\ = f_{t+1}f_{t+1}' \otimes Z_t'Z_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

For the second sum, write $R^e_{t+1} = 1 \otimes R^e_{t+1}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(f_{t+1} \otimes Z_t')(1 \otimes R^e_{t+1}) \\
&\ = f_{t+1} \otimes Z_t'R^e_{t+1} = f_{t+1} \otimes R^Z_{t+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</details>

So the fit needs only the $\dim z$ managed-portfolio returns and the $\dim z \times \dim z$ matrix $Z_t'Z_t$ of
each month. In the simulated panel of the Playground, that is 4 returns a month instead of 200, and the fit works
with 120 small blocks instead of 24,000 firm-months. In the paper's data, 37 portfolios (36 characteristics and the constant) stand in for 12,813 firms.

### Alternating least squares

The two steps give **alternating least squares** (ALS): solve for the factors given $\Gamma_\beta$, then for
$\Gamma_\beta$ given the factors, and repeat. Each step is an exact least-squares solution given the other, so the
objective can never rise. The start value is the first $K$ eigenvectors of $\sum_t R^Z_{t+1}R^{Z\prime}_{t+1}$, the
principal components of the managed portfolios. The paper stops when no element of $\Gamma_\beta$ or of the factors
moves by $10^{-6}$ or more, and notes that this start is usually within 10 iterations of the end (p. 52).

### The normalisation removes the rotation

The fit depends on $\Gamma_\beta$ and $f$ only through the product $\Gamma_\beta f_{t+1}$. So for any invertible
$K \times K$ matrix $H$,

$$
Z_t\Gamma_\beta\Factor{f_{t+1}} = Z_t\,(\Gamma_\beta H^{-1})(H\Factor{f_{t+1}}).
$$

This is the rotation of Chapter 31. IPCA picks one rotation (p. 13). The columns of $\Gamma_\beta$ are orthonormal,
$\Gamma_\beta'\Gamma_\beta = I_K$. The factors are uncorrelated in the sample, with the largest variance first. Each
factor has a non-negative mean. The first rule leaves only rotations and reflections. The second leaves only a
change of order and sign, the third fixes the order, and the last fixes the signs, as long as no two factors have
the same variance. So each cell of the heat map in the Playground has a definite sign.

### With as many factors as characteristics: Fama–MacBeth

Take $K = \dim z$. Then $\Gamma_\beta$ is square and invertible, and the factor step becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma_\beta\Factor{f_{t+1}} &= \Gamma_\beta\Gamma_\beta^{-1}(Z_t'Z_t)^{-1}(\Gamma_\beta')^{-1} \\
&\quad \times \Gamma_\beta'Z_t'\Ret{R^e_{t+1}} \\
&= (Z_t'Z_t)^{-1}Z_t'\Ret{R^e_{t+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

That is the month-$t$ Fama–MacBeth regression of returns on characteristics from Chapter 17 (p. 15). Every $\Gamma_\beta$ gives the same fit, so ALS stops after one iteration. On the seeded panel with $K = 4$, the fitted
$\Gamma_\beta f_{t+1}$ matches the Fama–MacBeth slopes of every month to within $10^{-15}$. With $K < \dim z$, IPCA
is a Fama–MacBeth regression forced through $K$ combinations of the characteristics.

### Two $R^2$

The paper scores a fit in two ways (p. 24), over all firms and months:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R^2_{\text{total}} = 1 - {}&\textstyle\sum_{i,t}\big(R^e_{i,t+1} \\
&\quad - z_{it}'(\hat\Gamma_\alpha + \hat\Gamma_\beta\hat f_{t+1})\big)^2 \\
&\big/ \textstyle\sum_{i,t}(R^e_{i,t+1})^2, \\[0.5em]
R^2_{\text{pred}} = 1 - {}&\textstyle\sum_{i,t}\big(R^e_{i,t+1} \\
&\quad - z_{it}'(\hat\Gamma_\alpha + \hat\Gamma_\beta\hat\lambda)\big)^2 \\
&\big/ \textstyle\sum_{i,t}(R^e_{i,t+1})^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

$\hat\lambda$ is the mean of the estimated factors, and $\hat\Gamma_\alpha = 0$ in the restricted fit. The total $R^2$
uses each month's factor values, so it measures how well the model explains risk, the co-movement of returns. The
predictive $R^2$ uses only what is known at $t$, so it measures how well the model explains expected returns. Both
divide by the raw sum of squares, not by the sum around a mean.

### The test of $\Gamma_\alpha = 0$

The unrestricted model adds $\Gamma_\alpha$, and the fit is the same with $Z_t(\Gamma_\alpha + \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1})$
in place of $Z_t\Gamma_\beta f_{t+1}$. A part of $\Gamma_\alpha$ that lies along the columns of $\Gamma_\beta$ can
move into the factor means. Add any combination of the columns of $\Gamma_\beta$ to $\Gamma_\alpha$, subtract the
same constants from the factors, and the fit is the same. So the library
keeps $\hat\Gamma_\alpha$ orthogonal to the columns of $\hat\Gamma_\beta$. The paper does not state this rule, but
its simulations draw $\Gamma_\alpha$ that way (p. 53). The statistic is the squared length of the alpha map (p. 18):

$$
W_\alpha = \hat\Gamma_\alpha'\hat\Gamma_\alpha.
$$

Its distribution has no simple form, so the paper uses a bootstrap (p. 19). Let
$\Err{\hat\varepsilon^Z_{t+1}} = R^Z_{t+1} - Z_t'Z_t(\hat\Gamma_\alpha + \hat\Gamma_\beta\hat f_{t+1})$ be the residuals of the
managed portfolios. Build each new sample as

$$
\tilde R^Z_{t+1} = Z_t'Z_t\hat\Gamma_\beta\hat f_{t+1} + \Err{\tilde\varepsilon^Z_{t+1}}.
$$

Each $\tilde\varepsilon^Z_{t+1}$ is the residual vector of a month drawn at random, times a Student $t$ draw with 5
degrees of freedom, scaled to variance 1. The paper writes $\hat d$ for the residuals; here $d_t$ is a log dividend.
The
$\hat\Gamma_\alpha$ term is left out, so $\Gamma_\alpha = 0$ is true in every sample. Refit each sample and record
its $\tilde W_\alpha$. The $p$-value is the share of the $\tilde W_\alpha$ that are larger than $W_\alpha$. The paper
uses 1,000 samples. The widget uses 200, so that it answers in seconds.

<Callout type="warning" title="A test given K">A test of $\Gamma_\alpha = 0$ is a test given $K$. Too few factors can push a missing
factor's premium into the alphas. Too many can let a spare factor absorb a true alpha. The Playground shows both.</Callout>

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> start at $K = 2$ with no alpha. ALS stops after 12 iterations. The
gap to the final objective is $3.3 \times 10^{-5}$ after the first iteration and $2.4 \times 10^{-14}$ after the
eleventh. From the second iteration on, it shrinks by a factor of 6 to 8 at each step. Every cell of
$\hat\Gamma_\beta$ differs from the truth by at most 0.13. The total $R^2$ is 8.25% and the predictive $R^2$ is 0.60%. The test does not reject:
$p = 0.685$.</p>

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this too:</strong> set $K = 1$. There is still no alpha in the panel, but now
$p = 0.020$, and the test rejects at 5%. Then go back to $K = 2$ and move the alpha share to 0.4%. The test rejects
with $p = 0.005$. Now set $K = 3$ with an alpha share of 1.0%. The test no longer rejects: $p = 0.395$.</p>

Each of these results has a reason. With $K = 1$, the second factor is missing. Its premium is still in the returns,
and it lines up with the characteristics through the second column of $\Gamma_\beta$. The only place left for it is
$\Gamma_\alpha$. The paper finds the same pattern in real stocks: one factor is rejected with a $p$-value below
0.01%, and two factors are not, with $p = 15.4\%$ (pp. 25–26).

With a true alpha and $K = 2$, the restricted fit cannot explain the expected returns, and it shows. At an alpha
share of 1.0%, the restricted predictive $R^2$ falls to 0.01%, while the unrestricted fit reaches 0.29%. The
alpha map holds almost all the predictive content of the characteristics.

With $K = 3$, a third factor is free. Its loadings can line up with $\Gamma_\alpha$, and its mean then plays the
part of the alpha. The fit cannot tell a constant reward without risk from a factor that has a mean. Here the extra
factor absorbs the alpha. So choose $K$ before you trust the test. The paper reports the test for each $K$ from 1
to 6, next to the two $R^2$ (p. 25).

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- IPCA lets each firm's betas move with its characteristics, $\beta_{it} = \Gamma_\beta'z_{it}$, and lets the data
  find the latent factors. Characteristics are ranked each month, so one map serves every month.
- The fit needs only the managed portfolios $R^Z_{t+1} = Z_t'R^e_{t+1}$ and $Z_t'Z_t$. Alternating least squares
  starts from their principal components and never raises the objective.
- The normalisation $\Gamma_\beta'\Gamma_\beta = I$, with uncorrelated factors that have non-negative means, removes
  the rotation. With $K = \dim z$, IPCA gives the Fama–MacBeth slopes.
- The total $R^2$ measures risk, the predictive $R^2$ measures expected returns. The bootstrap test of
  $\Gamma_\alpha = 0$ asks whether characteristics predict returns beyond their betas, and its answer depends on $K$.
