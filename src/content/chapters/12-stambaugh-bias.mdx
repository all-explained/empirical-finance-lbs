## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> A slope from a world with nothing to find

In [Chapter 9](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/09-time-varying-expected-returns/), the log dividend–price ratio
at the end of each year forecast the next year's log excess return. On the goyal-welch-annual data, with
dividend–price ratios from 1926 to 2024 and returns from 1927 to 2025, the slope is 0.042. That uses 99 pairs of
years. The classical $t$-statistic is 1.07.

Now imagine a world where returns cannot be forecast at all. The true slope is exactly zero. Give that world the
same kind of predictor as ours: just as slow, with shocks of the same size and the same link to returns. Draw 99
years of data from it, and run the same regression. Do this a thousand times.

You would expect the slopes to scatter around zero. They do not. Their average is 0.033, and more than a third
of them are larger than the 0.042 we found in the data. So here is the question you should have: **how much of
the slope we measure is real, and how much is produced by the regression itself?** This chapter shows where the
extra slope comes from, how big it is, and how to test honestly when it is there.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Two estimates that make the same mistake

Start with the two facts that cause the problem.

**Fact one: the dividend–price ratio moves slowly.** In
[Chapter 5](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/05-persistence-ar1-and-var/) you met the AR(1), a series that
remembers its last value: $\Signal{x_{t+1}} = \mu_x + \phi\,\Signal{x_t} + v_{t+1}$. On our data the persistence
of the dividend–price ratio is $\phi = 0.92$ a year. A high ratio today means a high ratio for many years.

In a short sample, the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimate $\hat\phi$ of a persistent series tends to come out
**too low**. Here is the reason. OLS measures deviations from the sample mean, not from the true mean. A slow
series spends long stretches on one side of its true mean, so the sample mean is pulled towards the path that
happened. Measured from that mean, the deviations look shorter-lived than they are. So the series looks less
persistent than it is.

**Fact two: the return and the ratio are hit by the same news.** When good news lifts stock prices, the return
this year is high. The same price rise makes the dividend–price ratio fall, because the price is in its
denominator. So the shock to the return, $u_{t+1}$, and the shock to the ratio, $v_{t+1}$, move in opposite
directions. Their correlation, $\operatorname{corr}(u, v)$, is negative. On our data it is $-0.86$.

Now put the two facts together. In a sample where the ratio's shocks happen to make $\hat\phi$ come out too
low, the return's shocks are tied to those same shocks. The regression of the return on the ratio sees that
pattern as a link between today's ratio and next year's return. So in exactly those samples, the slope $\hat b$
comes out too high. One mistake drags the other with it. On average, $\hat\phi$ is too low, and so $\hat b$ is too
high. This is the **Stambaugh bias**.

The widget below shows it. It draws 1,000 seeded samples from a world where the true slope is zero. The top chart
is the histogram of the 1,000 slopes $\hat b$. The bottom chart is the histogram of their $t$-statistics. The
shock sizes are those of the data, so $\hat b$ is in the same units as the slope of Chapter 9.

<Callout type="key">With a persistent predictor whose shocks move against the return's shocks, OLS finds
predictability where there is none. The slope is biased up, and the $t$-statistic rejects "no predictability" too
often.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Where the bias comes from, and how big it is

### The predictive system

Write the model as two equations. The return equation is the predictive regression of Chapter 9 at a one-period
horizon. The predictor equation is an AR(1):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Ret{r^e_{t+1}} &= a + b\,\Signal{x_t} + \Err{u_{t+1}}, \\
\Signal{x_{t+1}} &= \mu_x + \phi\,\Signal{x_t} + \Err{v_{t+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\Ret{r^e_{t+1}}$ is the log excess return and $\Signal{x_t}$ is the predictor, for us the log dividend–price
ratio $\Signal{dp_t}$. The shocks $\Err{u}$ and $\Err{v}$ cannot be forecast from anything known at $t$, and they
are independent over time. But shocks in the same period can be correlated. Write their covariance and the
variance of the predictor's shock as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{uv} &= \operatorname{Cov}(\Err{u_{t+1}}, \Err{v_{t+1}}), \\
\sigma_v^2 &= \operatorname{Var}(\Err{v_{t+1}}).
\end{aligned}
$$

The sample has $T$ periods: pairs $(\Signal{x_t}, \Ret{r^e_{t+1}})$ for $t = 0, \dots, T - 1$. Both equations use the
same regressor $\Signal{x_t}$, so both have the same sample mean $\bar x = \tfrac1T \sum_t \Signal{x_t}$.

### Step 1: write each estimation error as a ratio

The OLS slope is $\hat b = \sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)\,\Ret{r^e_{t+1}} \big/ \sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)^2$.
Substitute the return equation for $\Ret{r^e_{t+1}}$. The constant $a$ drops out, because the deviations
$\Signal{x_t} - \bar x$ add up to zero. The term $b\,\Signal{x_t}$ gives back $b$, because
$\sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)\Signal{x_t} = \sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)^2$. What is left is the error:

$$
\hat b - b = \frac{\sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)\,\Err{u_{t+1}}}{\sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)^2}.
$$

The AR(1) is also a regression on $\Signal{x_t}$, with $\Signal{x_{t+1}}$ on the left. The same steps give

$$
\hat\phi - \phi = \frac{\sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)\,\Err{v_{t+1}}}{\sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)^2}.
$$

The two errors have the same denominator. They differ only in which shock is in the numerator.

### Step 2: split the return shock

Regress the return shock on the predictor's shock in the population. The slope of that regression is
$c = \sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2$, so

$$
\Err{u_{t+1}} = c\,\Err{v_{t+1}} + e_{t+1}, \qquad c = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}.
$$

The rest, $e_{t+1}$, is uncorrelated with $\Err{v_{t+1}}$ by construction. With normal shocks, uncorrelated means
independent, so $e$ is independent of every $\Err{v}$ at every date.

### Step 3: substitute

Write $S = \sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)^2$ for the common denominator. Put the split into the numerator of
$\hat b - b$, and use Step 1 for the part with $\Err{v}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat b - b &= c\,\frac{\sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)\,\Err{v_{t+1}}}{S} \\
&\quad + \frac{\sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)\,e_{t+1}}{S} \\
&= c\,(\hat\phi - \phi) + \frac{\sum_t (\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)\,e_{t+1}}{S}.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 4: take expectations

The path of the predictor is built from its starting value and the shocks $\Err{v}$ only. So $e$ is independent
of every $\Signal{x_t}$. Fix the path of $\Signal{x}$. Then the last fraction is a weighted sum of the $e$'s, and
each $e$ has mean zero. So the last term has mean zero for every path, and therefore on average too. That leaves

$$
\mathbb{E}[\hat b - b] = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\;\mathbb{E}[\hat\phi - \phi].
$$

This is exact under the model. It says that the slope inherits the bias of $\hat\phi$, scaled by $c$. If the shocks
are uncorrelated, $c = 0$ and there is no bias, however persistent the predictor is.

### Step 5: the bias of $\hat\phi$

For an AR(1) with an estimated mean, a classic approximation for the bias of $\hat\phi$ is

$$
\mathbb{E}[\hat\phi - \phi] \approx -\frac{1 + 3\phi}{T}.
$$

We state this one without proof: its derivation is a long expansion of a ratio of random sums. You can check it
in the widget instead. The readout shows the formula next to the average of $\hat\phi - \phi$ over the simulated
samples. Put the two results together:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\hat b - b] \approx -\frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\cdot\frac{1 + 3\phi}{T}.
$$

Read it term by term. The bias shrinks like $1/T$, so it is a small-sample problem. It grows with $\phi$. And its
sign is the opposite of the sign of $\sigma_{uv}$: a negative covariance, as for the dividend–price ratio, means an
**upward** bias.

### On our data

On goyal-welch-annual, the estimates are $\hat\phi = 0.92$, $\operatorname{corr}(u, v) = -0.86$ and
$c = \sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2 = -0.78$, with $T = 99$. The approximation gives $\mathbb{E}[\hat\phi - \phi] \approx
-0.038$. Multiply by $c$: the slope's bias is about 0.030. That is about 70% of the estimated slope of 0.042.

The simplest correction subtracts the bias: $0.042 - 0.030 = 0.013$ (the difference of the unrounded numbers).
Be careful with this number. It plugs in $\hat\phi$, which is itself biased down. The true $\phi$ is probably a bit
higher, the true bias a bit larger, and so this correction does too little. It is also a first-order approximation.

### The $t$-statistic is biased too

The bias moves the whole distribution of $\hat b$ to the right, so it moves the $t$-statistic too. A one-sided
test of $b = 0$ against $b > 0$ usually rejects when $t > 1.645$. That cut-off promises a 5% chance of a false
alarm only when $t$ has a standard normal distribution. Here it does not.

The fix is to **simulate the test**. Build the world where $b = 0$ with the estimated $\phi$, $\sigma_v$, $\sigma_u$ and
$\operatorname{corr}(u, v)$. Draw many samples of length $T$, and compute $t$ in each. The 95th percentile of
those $t$'s is the cut-off that really gives a 5% chance of a false alarm. The share of simulated $t$'s above the
data's $t$ is the simulated $p$-value.

In the world calibrated to our data, 15.9% of the samples have $t > 1.645$. The test that promises 5% has a true
**size**, its rate of false alarms, more than three times as large. The simulated 5% cut-off is 2.17. Our
$t$-statistic of 1.07 is below both cut-offs. In 31.6% of the samples, $t$ is larger than 1.07. So the
one-year regression, taken alone, gives no evidence against "returns cannot be forecast".

<Callout type="warning">The simulated test is only as good as the world you simulate. It uses estimates of
$\phi$ and $\operatorname{corr}(u, v)$, normal shocks and a constant slope. A higher true $\phi$ makes the bias
and the size problem worse, as the Playground shows.</Callout>

### How to read the evidence

What can the data show, then? With the slope of the one-year regression alone, not much. The estimate is small,
most of it could be bias, and its $t$-statistic is far from the simulated cut-off. You cannot reject "no
predictability" with this test on these 99 years.

What the data cannot show is that returns are *not* predictable. A test that fails to reject is not proof of the
null. The same bias also affects the long-horizon regressions of Chapter 9, which use the same persistent
predictor.

Cochrane (2008, p. 2) explains the source of the problem the same way: a change in prices moves both the return
and the dividend yield, so their shocks are negatively correlated, the slope is biased up and the $t$-statistic
leans towards rejection. In his simulation of a world with no return predictability, the return slope is as large
as his estimate about 22% of the time (p. 11), and the bias is about half of his estimated slope (p. 23). His
sample and his null world differ from ours, so his numbers differ too. His point is that the return regression
alone is weak evidence. He then finds much stronger evidence in a second regression, of dividend growth on the
ratio. Chapter 11 builds that test.

<Callout type="key">Never compare the $t$-statistic of a regression on a persistent predictor with 1.645 or 1.96.
Simulate the world where the slope is zero, and compare with its cut-off.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- In the system $\Ret{r^e_{t+1}} = a + b\,\Signal{x_t} + \Err{u_{t+1}}$, $\Signal{x_{t+1}} = \mu_x + \phi\,\Signal{x_t} +
  \Err{v_{t+1}}$, OLS gives $\mathbb{E}[\hat b - b] = (\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2)\,\mathbb{E}[\hat\phi - \phi]$.
- $\hat\phi$ is biased down, by about $(1 + 3\phi)/T$. With $\sigma_{uv} < 0$, as for the dividend–price ratio, $\hat b$
  is biased up.
- On goyal-welch-annual (99 years), the bias is about 0.030, against an estimated slope of 0.042.
- The $t$-statistic is biased too. A world calibrated to our data rejects at 1.645 in 15.9% of samples, and its 5%
  cut-off is 2.17. Use a simulated cut-off, and say which $\phi$ the simulation assumes.
- A test that fails to reject is not proof that returns cannot be forecast.
