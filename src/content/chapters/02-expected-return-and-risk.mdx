## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> How sure are we about the reward for risk?

[Chapter 1](../01-returns/) found that, from July 1926 to August 2026, the US stock market beat one-month
Treasury bills by 0.70% a month on average. That is 8.3% a year. It is one of the most famous numbers in finance: the
**equity premium**, the extra return that investors have earned for holding stocks instead of a safe asset.

So here is the question you probably have. If you plan your savings around that number, how much should you
trust it? A century sounds like a lot of data. It is 1,202 monthly returns. Surely that pins the average down?

It does not, at least not well. With the tools of this chapter, the honest answer is "about 8.3% a year, give
or take 1.8%". A range of two of those errors on each side runs from 4.7% to 12.0% a year. At 4.7%, stocks are
a modest bet. At 12.0%, they are an excellent one. The same 1,202 months tell you the market's **volatility**,
its typical swing, much more precisely: 18.4% a year, give or take less than one percentage point.

Why can a century measure the size of the swings so well and the average so badly? This chapter answers that
question. On the way you will meet the standard error, the Sharpe ratio, and the fat tails of real returns.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> One century is one sample

Imagine a world where we *know* the truth. Each month, the market's excess return is drawn at random, with a
true mean of 0.5% and a volatility of 4.5%. That is 6.0% a year on average, with swings of about 15.6% a year,
close to the real market. Now let 1,000 investors each live through their own sample of $T$ months from this
world, and let each one compute the average.

They do not get the same answer. Each sample has its own luck: one has a crash early on, another a long boom.
The widget draws all 1,000 samples. The grey bars show where their averages land. The dashed line is the truth
that every investor is trying to find.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> the widget opens at 600 months, 50 years. The averages spread

Look at the readout line "spread as a share of the truth". At 600 months, the averages miss the true mean by
37% of its size, in a typical sample. The sample volatilities miss the true volatility by only 3%. The same
data, the same months, and one estimate is more than ten times as precise as the other.

The blue dot is the real market over its last $T$ months, with a bar of two standard errors on each side. Its
bar is about as wide as the cloud of simulated averages. That is the point: the real century is just one draw
from a world like this, and we never see the other 999.

Why is the mean so hard? Think of a boat that drifts slowly while waves toss it up and down. Watch it for one
minute and you can measure the height of the waves well, because every wave tells you something about wave
height. But the drift is tiny next to the waves. To see it, you must watch for a long time and compare where
the boat started with where it ended. Monthly returns are the same: the waves (4.5% a month) are much bigger
than the drift (0.5% a month).

<Callout type="key">Each month says a lot about the size of the swings and very little about the average. So a
sample measures volatility well and the mean return badly.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Means, standard errors and Sharpe ratios

Write $\Ret{R^e_t}$ for the excess return in month $t$, for $t = 1, \dots, T$. We assume for now that the months
are independent draws with the same mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_t}]$ and volatility
$\sigma$. Chapter 4 relaxes the independence.

### The sample mean and its standard error

The **sample mean** is the plain average:

$$
\bar R^e = \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Ret{R^e_t}.
$$

It is a random number, because the sample is random. Its variance tells us how far it typically lands from
$\mu$. The variance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of their variances, and a constant factor
$\tfrac1T$ comes out squared:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar R^e) = \frac{1}{T^2} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \operatorname{Var}(\Ret{R^e_t})
= \frac{1}{T^2} \cdot T\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{T}.
$$

The **standard error** is the square root of this variance, the typical size of the estimation error:

$$
\mathrm{se}(\bar R^e) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T}}.
$$

We do not know $\sigma$, so we use the sample volatility $\hat\sigma$ in its place. This is the formula that the
widget's "average standard error" uses. Two features matter. First, the error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{T}$: to halve
it you need four times as much data. Second, the error is large when $\sigma$ is large compared with $\mu$. On
the data, $\bar R^e = 0.70\%$ a month and $\hat\sigma = 5.30\%$ a month, so

$$
\mathrm{se}(\bar R^e) = \frac{5.30\%}{\sqrt{1202}} = 0.15\% \text{ a month}.
$$

The ratio $\bar R^e / \mathrm{se}(\bar R^e) = 4.5$ is the $t$-statistic of the hypothesis "the true mean is zero".
The mean is 4.5 standard errors above zero, so a century is enough to say that the premium is positive. It is
not enough to say how big it is. A range of two standard errors on each side covers the true mean in about 95%
of samples, if the sample mean is close to normal.

### Why more frequent data does not help the mean

To compare with yearly figures, multiply the monthly mean by 12: $\mu_{\text{ann}} = 12\mu$. A year is 12
independent months, so its variance is $12\sigma^2$ and its volatility is $\sigma_{\text{ann}} = \sqrt{12}\,\sigma$.
Now suppose the sample covers $T/12$ years. The standard error of the annual mean is

$$
\mathrm{se}(12\,\bar R^e) = \frac{12\,\sigma}{\sqrt{T}} = \frac{\sqrt{12}\,\sigma \cdot \sqrt{12}}{\sqrt{T}}
= \frac{\sigma_{\text{ann}}}{\sqrt{T/12}}.
$$

The number of months has disappeared. Only the number of *years* is left. Daily data over the same years would
give the same answer, because the mean depends on the drift, and only a longer span shows more drift. On our
data, $\sigma_{\text{ann}} = 18.4\%$ and there are 100 years, so the standard error is 1.8% a year. To bring it
down to 1% a year, you would need $(18.4/1)^2$, about 340 years of data.

### Why the volatility is easy

For the variance, every month counts. Suppose for a moment that we knew $\mu$. Then the variance estimate is an
average of the squared deviations $(\Ret{R^e_t} - \mu)^2$, and the same rule as above gives its variance:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{T}\operatorname{Var}\big((\Ret{R^e_t} - \mu)^2\big)
= \frac{\mathbb{E}[(\Ret{R^e_t} - \mu)^4] - \sigma^4}{T}.
$$

<details>
<summary>Every step to the standard error of $\hat\sigma$</summary>

1. For any random variable $Y$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}[Y^2] - (\mathbb{E}[Y])^2$. With
   $Y = (\Ret{R^e_t} - \mu)^2$, $\mathbb{E}[Y^2] = \mathbb{E}[(\Ret{R^e_t} - \mu)^4]$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y] = \sigma^2$.
   That is the last step above.
2. The fourth moment, divided by $\sigma^4$, is the kurtosis. Write it as $3 + \text{excess kurtosis}$, because
   a normal distribution has kurtosis 3. Then
   $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\sigma^2) = (2 + \text{excess kurtosis})\,\sigma^4/T$.
3. A small change in $\sigma^2$ changes $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$ by that change divided by $2\sigma$, the
   derivative of the square root. So $\mathrm{se}(\hat\sigma) \approx \mathrm{se}(\hat\sigma^2)/(2\sigma)$.
4. Substitute: $\mathrm{se}(\hat\sigma) \approx \sigma^2\sqrt{(2 + \text{excess kurtosis})/T}\,/\,(2\sigma) =
   \sigma\sqrt{(2 + \text{excess kurtosis})/(4T)}$.
5. Using $\bar R^e$ in place of the unknown $\mu$ changes this only by terms of order $1/T$, which are small when
   $T$ is large.

</details>

For normal returns the excess kurtosis is 0, and the result is simple:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{se}(\hat\sigma)}{\sigma} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2T}}.
$$

This shrinks with the number of *observations*, not the number of years. With 1,202 months it is 2.0%. Compare
the mean: its standard error is 22% of its size. That is the whole puzzle of the Hook in two formulas. Real
returns have fat tails, which raise the error of $\hat\sigma$; we come back to this in the Playground.

### The Sharpe ratio

The **Sharpe ratio** measures the reward per unit of risk:

$$
SR = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e}]}{\sigma(\Ret{R^e})}.
$$

On the monthly data, $SR = 0.70\% / 5.30\% = 0.13$. To annualise, put the annual mean over the annual
volatility:

$$
SR_{\text{ann}} = \frac{12\mu}{\sqrt{12}\,\sigma} = \sqrt{12}\,\frac{\mu}{\sigma} = \sqrt{12}\,SR.
$$

So the market's annual Sharpe ratio is $\sqrt{12} \times 0.13 = 0.45$. The $\sqrt{12}$ comes from the
volatility, and it assumes independent months. If returns were correlated from month to month, $\sqrt{12}$ would
be the wrong factor.

How precise is 0.45? Volatility is measured well, so almost all the error comes from the mean. Treat
$\sigma_{\text{ann}}$ as known. Then the error of $SR_{\text{ann}}$ is the error of the annual mean divided by
$\sigma_{\text{ann}}$, which is $1/\sqrt{T/12}$. With 100 years, that is 0.10. So a century says that the Sharpe
ratio is about 0.45, give or take 0.10.

### Skewness and fat tails

Mean and volatility do not describe the whole shape of returns. Two more numbers help. **Skewness** measures
whether the distribution leans to one side:

$$
\text{skewness} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[(\Ret{R^e} - \mu)^3]}{\sigma^3}.
$$

Cubing keeps the sign, so big losses push skewness down and big gains push it up. **Excess kurtosis** measures
the weight of the tails, the very large moves in either direction:

$$
\text{excess kurtosis} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[(\Ret{R^e} - \mu)^4]}{\sigma^4} - 3.
$$

The fourth power makes big moves count enormously. A normal distribution has skewness 0 and excess kurtosis 0.
A positive excess kurtosis means **fat tails**: more extreme months than a normal with the same volatility
predicts. Our library estimates both by replacing each expectation with a sample average.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> look at the whole distribution first. The bars rise higher and

mean, a normal expects 1.6 months. The data have 11. The worst, September 1931, had an excess return of −28.7%, which is 5.6

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this too:</strong> switch the sample between "before 1947" and "1947 on". Watch
the sign of the skewness and the size of the excess kurtosis.</p>

On the full sample, the skewness is 0.15 and the excess kurtosis is 7.4. A tall, narrow middle and fat tails go
together: with the volatility held fixed, the extra weight in the tails comes from the shoulders of the
distribution. The best month, April 1933, had an excess return of +38.8%, or 7.2 standard deviations above the mean. Of the 1,202 months, 39% had a
negative excess return.

The two subsamples tell different stories. Before 1947, the skewness is +0.49: the 1930s had huge rebounds as
well as huge crashes. From 1947 on, it is −0.49, with an excess kurtosis of only 1.7. So the sign of the
skewness depends on the sample, and with few extreme months it is itself estimated with a large error. Do not
read too much into a single skewness number.

There is a deeper reason for fat tails. Volatility was 28.1% a year before 1947 and 14.9% a year from 1947 on.
A mix of calm and wild periods looks fat-tailed even if each period alone were normal: the wild months supply
the extremes, the calm ones the tall middle. Later chapters treat volatility that changes over time.

Fat tails also hurt the variance estimate. Plug the excess kurtosis of 7.4 into the formula of the Formalisation:
the standard error of $\hat\sigma$ is 4.4% of its size, not 2.0%. That is still five times better than the mean.

<Callout type="warning">A normal curve fitted to monthly returns understates crashes. A "five-standard-deviation
month" sounds impossible under the normal, yet the US market has had several. Report skewness and excess
kurtosis next to the volatility.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- The sample mean has standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{T}$. For the US market from 1926 to 2026, the excess return
  is 8.3% a year with a standard error of 1.8% a year.
- The error of the annual mean depends on the number of years, not on how often you sample. The volatility's
  error shrinks with the number of observations, so the mean is hard to estimate and the volatility is easy.
- Annualise with $\mu_{\text{ann}} = 12\mu$, $\sigma_{\text{ann}} = \sqrt{12}\,\sigma$ and
  $SR_{\text{ann}} = \sqrt{12}\,SR$. The market's annual Sharpe ratio is about 0.45, give or take 0.10.
- Monthly returns have fat tails: an excess kurtosis of 7.4, and 11 months more than three standard deviations
  below the mean where a normal expects 1.6. The sign of the skewness depends on the sample.
