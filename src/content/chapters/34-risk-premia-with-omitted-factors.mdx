## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Three answers for one premium

Take the 25 portfolios sorted on size and book-to-market, in Kenneth French's monthly data from 1963-07 to
2026-08: 758 months. You want the risk premium of the value factor, HML. HML is itself a portfolio, so you already
have one answer: its mean excess return is 0.294% a month.

Now run the two-pass regression of
[Chapter 17](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/17-cross-sectional-regressions-and-fama-macbeth/) with HML as the only
factor. The first pass gives each portfolio's beta on HML. The second pass regresses the 25 mean excess returns on
those betas, with no intercept. The slope, the estimated premium, is −0.140% a month. Value now looks like a hedge
that you pay for. Run the same test with SMB alone, and its premium is 0.640% a month, more than four times its mean
of 0.147%.

Put Mkt-RF, SMB and HML into the regression together, and the premia become 0.560%, 0.144% and 0.353% a month,
close to the three means. So the premium of HML depends on which *other* factors you happen to include.

That is a problem far beyond value. Suppose you care about a factor that is not a portfolio, such as a liquidity
shock or consumption growth. You do not have its mean as a check, and nobody can give you the full list of the other
priced factors. **Can you estimate one factor's premium without knowing all the others?**

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Price the whole space, then project

Why did HML alone go wrong? The second pass fits mean returns with one beta. But the means of the 25 portfolios
also reward other risks, such as the market and size. When the loadings on those omitted risks line up, across the
portfolios, with the loadings on HML, the slope on HML takes their reward as its own. When they line up against
HML's loadings, the slope is pushed down. It is the omitted-variable bias of any regression, here in the second pass.

The widget shows the same thing in an economy that we build, so that we know the truth. It has two priced factors,
each with a premium of 6% a year. You observe only the first one, $\Factor{\chi_t}$. The second one is omitted. Its
strength $s$ scales its loadings, and $c$ sets how they correlate, across the 25 assets, with the loadings on the
first factor. Each panel shows one estimator over 100 seeded samples of 600 months.

<OmittedFactorBias client:visible initialStrength={1} initialCorrelation={0.5} />

At $s = 1$ and $c = 0.5$, the two-pass estimates sit to the right of the truth. Their mean bias is +2.77% a year,
close to the +3.00% that the algebra below predicts, and their root mean squared error is 3.65%. The three-pass
estimates centre on the truth. Their mean bias is −0.27% a year and their root mean squared error is 1.88%.

The three-pass estimator of Giglio & Xiu (2021) does not try to name the omitted factor. It works in three moves:

1. Find the space in which all the returns move, with principal components (PCA,
   [Chapter 31](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/31-latent-and-observable-factors/)). The components span the
   omitted factor too, even though nobody named it.
2. Price that whole space with a cross-sectional regression on the components' loadings. No priced factor is left
   out, so this regression has no omitted variable.
3. Ask how much of the space your factor is, with a time-series regression of $\Factor{\chi_t}$ on the components,
   and add up the premia in those amounts.

Chapter 31 showed that the components are fixed only up to a rotation. Step 3 makes that harmless: whatever arrows
PCA draws on the "sheet" of factors, the factor's position on the sheet stays the same, and so does its premium.

<Callout type="key">The two-pass estimate of one factor's premium absorbs the premia of omitted factors whose
loadings correlate with its own. The three-pass estimator prices the full latent factor space and then projects the
factor on it, so no priced factor is left out.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> The model, the bias and the three passes

### Returns on latent factors, and the observed factor on them

Write the $N$ excess returns at month $t$ as a vector $\Ret{R^e_t}$, driven by $K$ latent factors $\Factor{f_t}$
with mean zero, so they are the factors' innovations:

$$
\Ret{R^e_t} = \beta\lambda + \beta\Factor{f_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_t} .
$$

Here $\beta$ is the $N \times K$ matrix of loadings, $\lambda$ the $K$ premia, and the residuals $\varepsilon_t$ have
mean zero and are uncorrelated with $f_t$. So $\mathbb{E}[R^e] = \beta\lambda$. The observed factor is one series,
related to the latent ones by a time-series regression:

$$
\Factor{\chi_t} = a + \beta_\chi'\Factor{f_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_{\chi,t}} .
$$

The $K$-vector $\beta_\chi$ holds its loadings, and $\varepsilon_{\chi,t}$ is measurement error, uncorrelated with
$f_t$. These are equations 1 and 2 of Giglio & Xiu (2021, p. 1956). The paper writes $v_t$, $u_t$, $\gamma$ for our
$f_t$, $\varepsilon_t$, $\lambda$, and $g_t = \xi + \eta v_t + z_t$ for our second equation. The style guide's
Part VIII table says why we change each letter.

The **risk premium of $\chi_t$**, $\lambda_\chi$, is the mean excess return of a portfolio with beta 1 on $\chi_t$
and beta 0 on everything else (p. 1956). Here is why it equals $\beta_\chi'\lambda$.

1. Take portfolio weights $w$ with $w'\beta = \beta_\chi'$. With many assets and $K$ factors, such weights exist.
2. Its excess return is $w'R^e_t = \beta_\chi'\lambda + \beta_\chi'f_t + w'\varepsilon_t$.
3. Its factor part, $\beta_\chi'f_t$, is the part of $\chi_t$ that the factors explain. So the portfolio moves one
   for one with $\chi_t$ and has no other factor risk. In a well-diversified portfolio $w'\varepsilon_t$ is small.
4. Its mean is $\beta_\chi'\lambda + w'\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t] = \beta_\chi'\lambda$. So

$$
\lambda_\chi = \beta_\chi'\lambda .
$$

For a traded factor such as HML, the factor is such a portfolio, so $\lambda_\chi$ should equal its mean excess
return. That is our check in the Hook.

### The omitted-variable bias of the two-pass estimate

Take the economy of the widget: $K = 2$ independent factors, the observed factor is the first one with no error,
$\chi_t = f_{1,t}$, and $\lambda_\chi = \lambda_1$. Asset $i$ has loadings $\beta_{i,1}$ and $\beta_{i,2}$.

1. First pass: regress each $R^e_{i,t}$ on $\chi_t$ alone. Because $f_{2,t}$ is uncorrelated with $f_{1,t}$, the
   slope estimates $\beta_{i,1}$ without bias. If the two factors were correlated in time, this step would be biased
   too (p. 1954).
2. Second pass: regress the means $\Data{\bar R^e_i}$ on $\beta_{i,1}$, with no intercept:
   $\hat\lambda_\chi = \sum_i \beta_{i,1}\bar R^e_i \big/ \sum_i \beta_{i,1}^2$.
3. For large $T$, $\bar R^e_i \approx \beta_{i,1}\lambda_1 + \beta_{i,2}\lambda_2$. Substitute:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat\lambda_\chi &\approx \lambda_1 + \lambda_2\,\frac{\sum_i \beta_{i,1}\beta_{i,2}}{\sum_i \beta_{i,1}^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

4. The fraction is the cross-sectional slope of the omitted loadings on the included ones. In the widget,
   $\beta_{i,1}$ is a standard normal draw, and $\beta_{i,2}$ is $s$ times another standard normal draw whose
   correlation with $\beta_{i,1}$, across assets, is $c$. For large $N$ the slope tends to $s\,c$, so the bias is
   $s\,c\,\lambda_2$.
5. At $s = 1$, $c = 0.5$ and $\lambda_2 = 6\%$ a year, the bias is 3.00% a year.

The bias needs both a priced omitted factor ($s\lambda_2 \neq 0$) and correlated loadings ($c \neq 0$). With
$c = 0$ the omitted factor only adds noise, through pricing errors that the second pass does not see.

<details>
<summary>Measurement error adds a second bias</summary>

Now let $\chi_t = f_{1,t} + \varepsilon_{\chi,t}$, and first take $c = 0$.

1. The first-pass slope tends to $\operatorname{Cov}(R^e_{i,t}, \chi_t)/\operatorname{Var}(\chi_t)$. The covariance
   is $\beta_{i,1}\operatorname{Var}(f_{1,t})$, and the variance is
   $\operatorname{Var}(f_{1,t}) + \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{\chi,t})$.
2. So every beta shrinks by the same factor, $\operatorname{Var}(f_{1,t})/\operatorname{Var}(\chi_t)$. This is
   attenuation bias (p. 1955).
3. The second pass divides the means by betas that are too small, so the slope grows by the inverse factor:
   $\hat\lambda_\chi \approx \lambda_1\operatorname{Var}(\chi_t)/\operatorname{Var}(f_{1,t})$.
4. With correlated omitted loadings as well, the two effects multiply:
   $\hat\lambda_\chi \approx (\lambda_1 + s\,c\,\lambda_2)\operatorname{Var}(\chi_t)/\operatorname{Var}(f_{1,t})$.

[Chapter 18](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/18-errors-in-variables-and-shanken/) dealt with a different error: the
first-pass betas are estimated, so the usual standard errors of the second pass are too small. Here the factor
itself is measured with error, and the estimate is biased.

</details>

### Rotation invariance

PCA recovers the latent factors only up to an invertible $K \times K$ matrix $H$, as Chapter 31 showed. Rewrite the
model with $Hf_t$:

1. Returns: $R^e_t = \beta H^{-1}(H\lambda) + \beta H^{-1}(Hf_t) + \varepsilon_t$. The new loadings are
   $\beta H^{-1}$ and the new premia are $H\lambda$.
2. The observed factor: $\chi_t = a + \beta_\chi'H^{-1}(Hf_t) + \varepsilon_{\chi,t}$. Its new loadings are
   $\beta_\chi'H^{-1}$.
3. The premium is the product, and the $H$ cancels:

$$
(\beta_\chi'H^{-1})(H\lambda) = \beta_\chi'\lambda = \lambda_\chi .
$$

The loadings $\beta_\chi$ and the premia $\lambda$ each change with $H$, so neither can be learnt without knowing the
true factors. Their product cannot change, so it can be learnt from any rotation of them (p. 1957). This is the
idea the whole estimator rests on.

### The three passes

With $K$ principal components of the excess returns (p. 1959):

1. **PCA.** Take the first $K$ components $\Factor{\hat f_t}$ of the demeaned returns, scaled so that
   $\tfrac1T\sum_t \hat f_t\hat f_t' = I_K$, and their loadings $\hat\beta$. By the argument above, they estimate
   $Hf_t$ and $\beta H^{-1}$ for some $H$.
2. **Cross-section.** Regress the means on the loadings, by OLS with no intercept:
   $\hat\lambda = (\hat\beta'\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'\Data{\bar R^e}$, the premia of the components.
3. **Time series.** Regress $\chi_t$ on the components, with an intercept, to get $\hat\beta_\chi$. The $R^2$ of this
   regression says how much of $\chi_t$ the components span.

Then $\hat\lambda_\chi = \hat\beta_\chi'\hat\lambda$. In one line, with the demeaned series
$\tilde\chi_t = \chi_t - \bar\chi$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat\lambda_\chi = {} &\Big(\tfrac1T\textstyle\sum_t \tilde\chi_t\hat f_t'\Big) \\
&\times (\hat\beta'\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'\bar R^e ,
\end{aligned}
$$

because $\tfrac1T\sum_t \hat f_t\hat f_t' = I_K$ turns the time-series slope into a plain average. Our library also gives a standard
error from the paper's large-$N$, large-$T$ theory (Theorem 1, p. 1964).

### Why extra components do no harm

PCA makes the $K$ components uncorrelated and their loading columns orthogonal, so $\hat\beta'\hat\beta$ is
diagonal. Then the three passes split into one product per component.

1. Step 2 becomes $K$ separate slopes: $\hat\lambda_k = \hat\beta_k'\bar R^e / \hat\beta_k'\hat\beta_k$, with
   $\hat\beta_k$ the $k$-th column.
2. Step 3 becomes $K$ separate averages: $\hat\beta_{\chi,k} = \tfrac1T\sum_t \tilde\chi_t\hat f_{k,t}$.
3. So $\hat\lambda_\chi = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat\beta_{\chi,k}\hat\lambda_k$. The components are nested, so going from
   $K$ to $K + 1$ components adds exactly one term, $\hat\beta_{\chi,K+1}\hat\lambda_{K+1}$.
4. If component $K + 1$ is not a true factor, it is a mix of idiosyncratic noise, uncorrelated with $\chi_t$. Its
   slope $\hat\beta_{\chi,K+1}$ tends to zero as $T$ grows, and so does the added term.
5. If instead $K$ is below the true number, a missing component is a true factor that $\chi_t$ may load on. Its term
   is then not zero, and leaving it out biases the estimate.

So it is safer to take too many components than too few. Giglio & Xiu report that their estimator stays consistent
as long as the number of components is at least the true number (p. 1972).

### What our data show

Apply both estimators to Mkt-RF, SMB and HML, one factor at a time, on the 25 portfolios from 1963-07 to 2026-08. We
take $K = 5$ components, more than the three that Chapter 31 found to span the factors, because extra components do
no harm. Step 3 gives an $R^2$ of 0.98, 0.99 and 0.95: the components span all three factors almost fully.

| Factor | Mean | Two-pass, alone | Three-pass, $K = 5$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mkt-RF | 0.602 | 0.687 | 0.538 (0.160) |
| SMB | 0.147 | 0.640 | 0.186 (0.109) |
| HML | 0.294 | −0.140 | 0.320 (0.106) |

All numbers are in % a month, with the three-pass standard error in parentheses. Each three-pass premium is within
half a standard error of the factor's mean, as it should be for a traded factor. With $K = 3$ they are 0.549%,
0.153% and 0.340%, about the same. With too few components, the bias of the last step above shows: HML's premium is −0.030% with one
component, whose $R^2$ for HML is 0.01, and 0.174% with two.

More components are not free in a sample of 25 portfolios, though. Going from six to seven moves Mkt-RF's premium
from 0.536% to 0.611%, because the seventh term is 0.075%: still under half a standard error.

Now add the 30 industry portfolios of `ff30-industry-monthly`, for 55 portfolios. HML alone in the two-pass gets
−0.512% a month. The three-pass estimates with $K = 5$ are 0.582%, 0.138% and 0.106%. Mkt-RF and SMB stay close to
their means, but HML's premium is 1.9 standard errors below its mean of 0.294%, although the components span 84% of
its variance. The industries bring strong comovement that is not value, and their mean returns pull the second step
away from HML's. So, plainly: on our data the three-pass estimator fixes the wild two-pass numbers, but with these
few portfolios it does not always land on a traded factor's mean.

These are whole-sample estimates: the components come from the same returns that they then price, the limit that
Chapter 31 pointed out. Giglio & Xiu use a far
larger set of 647 portfolios of stocks, bonds and currencies, with seven components (pp. 1972–1973). In their data,
the two-pass premium of momentum with no other factors is −201 basis points a month, against a mean of 69, and 71
with the three Fama–French factors as controls. Their three-pass estimate is 49 (p. 1973). Those are their numbers,
not ours. For the traded factors they study, the three-pass estimates are mostly close to the means (p. 1975).

A last link: a factor whose step-3 $R^2$ is near zero has $\hat\beta_\chi \approx 0$, so its three-pass premium is near
zero too. The useless factors of
[Chapter 27](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/27-weak-and-useless-factors/), which look priced in a two-pass test, would get
a premium near zero here, in large samples. Giglio & Xiu make this point for weak and noisy factors (p. 1974).

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- Returns follow $R^e_t = \beta\lambda + \beta f_t + \varepsilon_t$, and the observed factor
  $\chi_t = a + \beta_\chi'f_t + \varepsilon_{\chi,t}$. Its premium is $\lambda_\chi = \beta_\chi'\lambda$.
- A two-pass estimate with $\chi_t$ alone absorbs the premia of omitted factors whose loadings correlate with its
  own: $s\,c\,\lambda_2$ in the two-factor economy. Measurement error in $\chi_t$ inflates it further.
- No rotation $H$ of the latent factors changes $\lambda_\chi$, so PCA's arbitrary rotation does not matter.
- The three passes: PCA, a cross-sectional regression of the means on the loadings, and a time-series regression of
  $\chi_t$ on the components. Extra components add terms that vanish; too few leave a bias.
- On the 25 portfolios from 1963-07, the three-pass premia of Mkt-RF, SMB and HML are within half a standard error of
  their means, while the two-pass with HML alone gives −0.140% a month.
