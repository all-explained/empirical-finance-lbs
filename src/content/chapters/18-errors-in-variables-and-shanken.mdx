## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> The betas are estimates too

A two-pass test prices a factor model in two steps. The **first pass** runs one time-series regression for each
test asset and gives its betas $\hat\beta_i$. The **second pass** regresses the assets' mean excess returns
across assets on those betas. Its slopes are the risk premia $\hat\lambda$: the extra mean return per unit of
beta. Chapter 17, "Cross-sectional regressions and Fama–MacBeth", builds this method in full.

Here is the snag. The second pass treats the betas as if we knew them. We do not. Each $\hat\beta_i$ comes from a
regression with its own sampling error. So the usual standard error of $\hat\lambda$ leaves out one source of
noise. It should be too small.

How much too small? Take the Fama–French three factors (FF3) and the 25 size–book-to-market portfolios on
ff3-monthly and ff25-size-bm-monthly, from 1963-07 to 2026-08. That is $T = 758$ months. The second pass gives a
market premium of 6.72% a year. Its standard error is 1.970% a year if you treat the betas as known. With the
correction of this chapter it is 1.971%. The noise in the betas changes almost nothing.

Now take a made-up world with one factor whose Sharpe ratio is 5 a year. Run the same two passes on 200 seeded
samples of 600 months. The usual 95% interval for the premium contains the true value in only 83.5% of the
samples. So here is the question you should have: **when do estimated betas make the standard errors wrong, and
by how much?** The answer is one number, $c$, and it turns out to be a squared Sharpe ratio.

Why does the Sharpe ratio matter? Think about one asset. Its mean excess return is noisy, because its residual
$\varepsilon_{i,t}$ does not average out to exactly zero in a finite sample. The naive standard error counts that
noise.

Its beta is noisy too, and for the same reason. The beta is the slope of the asset's return on the factor. The
residuals push that slope up or down, and they push it more when the factor moves only a little. A factor with low
volatility is like a short ruler: small errors in the reading give large errors in the slope.

Now follow the beta error into the second pass. The second pass says that the asset's mean return should be
$\hat\beta_i\lambda$. An error of size $d$ in the beta looks like an error of $\lambda d$ in the mean return. So the
beta error matters more when the premium $\lambda$ is large and when the factor's volatility $\sigma_f$ is small.
Both point to one ratio, $\lambda/\sigma_f$. For a factor that is itself a return, that ratio is its Sharpe ratio.

The two noises are the same size up to that ratio. The beta error adds noise with variance $(\lambda/\sigma_f)^2$
times the noise of the mean return. That squared ratio is $c$. The corrected standard error simply scales the
residual part of the variance by $1 + c$.

<Callout type="key">An error in an estimated beta looks like an error in the asset's mean return, scaled by the
premium. The corrected variance multiplies the residual part by $1 + c$, where $c$ is the factor's squared Sharpe
ratio.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Deriving the corrected variance

### The model and its assumptions

Write $\Ret{R^e_t}$ for the $N$-vector of test-asset excess returns in month $t$, and $\Factor{f_t}$ for the
$K$-vector of factors. Stack the betas into the $N \times K$ matrix $\beta$, whose row $i$ is $\beta_i'$. The
time-series model is

$$
\Ret{R^e_t} = a + \beta\,\Factor{f_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_t}.
$$

We assume three things. First, the factors are independent over time, with mean $\mathbb{E}f$ and covariance
$\Sigma_f$. Second, the residuals $\Err{\varepsilon_t}$ are independent over time with mean zero. Third, their
covariance is $\Sigma_\varepsilon$ in every month, whatever the factors do. That third assumption is called
**conditional homoskedasticity**. We use all three below, and we return to them at the end.

The factor model says $\mathbb{E}[R^e] = \beta\lambda$. Take the mean of the time-series model:
$\mathbb{E}[R^e] = a + \beta\,\mathbb{E}f$. So $a = \beta(\lambda - \mathbb{E}f)$, and

$$
\Ret{R^e_t} = \beta\lambda + \beta(\Factor{f_t} - \mathbb{E}f) + \Err{\varepsilon_t}.
$$

Average over the $T$ months. With $\bar f$ and $\bar\varepsilon$ the sample means,

$$
\Data{\bar R^e} = \beta\lambda + \beta(\bar f - \mathbb{E}f) + \Err{\bar\varepsilon}.
$$

The second pass is an OLS regression of $\bar R^e$ on the betas, without an intercept. With known betas it is
$\hat\lambda = A\,\bar R^e$, where $A = (\beta'\beta)^{-1}\beta'$. Note that $A\beta = I$.

### Step 1: the error with known betas

Apply $A$ to $\bar R^e$. Since $A\beta = I$,

$$
\hat\lambda - \lambda = (\bar f - \mathbb{E}f) + A\,\Err{\bar\varepsilon}.
$$

The two terms are independent, because the residuals are independent of the factors. The variance of a mean of
$T$ independent draws is the variance of one draw over $T$. So $\operatorname{Var}(\bar f) = \Sigma_f/T$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(\bar\varepsilon) = \Sigma_\varepsilon/T$, and

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\lambda) = \frac1T\big[A\Sigma_\varepsilon A' + \Sigma_f\big].
$$

This is the **naive** variance. The first term is the residual part. The second is the factor part: even with
perfect betas, the premium is a mean over $T$ months and has the sampling error of $\bar f$.

The Fama–MacBeth standard error, which averages month-by-month slopes, gives the same thing. With fixed betas it
equals $\tfrac1T A\Sigma A'$, where $\Sigma = \beta\Sigma_f\beta' + \Sigma_\varepsilon$ is the covariance of returns.
Expand: $A\Sigma A' = A\beta\,\Sigma_f\,\beta'A' + A\Sigma_\varepsilon A' = \Sigma_f + A\Sigma_\varepsilon A'$, because $A\beta = I$.

### Step 2: the error in each first-pass beta

The first pass regresses $R^e_{i,t}$ on a constant and $\Factor{f_t}$. Write $\hat\Sigma_f = \tfrac1T\sum_t (f_t - \bar f)(f_t
- \bar f)'$. The OLS slope is

$$
\hat\beta_i = (T\hat\Sigma_f)^{-1}\sum_t (\Factor{f_t} - \bar f)\,R^e_{i,t}.
$$

Since the deviations $f_t - \bar f$ add up to zero, we can subtract $\bar R^e_i$ from $R^e_{i,t}$ without changing the
sum. The model gives $R^e_{i,t} - \bar R^e_i = (f_t - \bar f)'\beta_i + \varepsilon_{i,t} - \bar\varepsilon_i$.
Substitute. The term in $\beta_i$ gives back $\beta_i$, and the term in $\bar\varepsilon_i$ drops out, again because the
deviations add up to zero. What is left is the error:

$$
\hat\beta_i - \beta_i = (T\hat\Sigma_f)^{-1}\sum_t (\Factor{f_t} - \bar f)\,\Err{\varepsilon_{i,t}}.
$$

Stack the rows $(\hat\beta_i - \beta_i)'$ into a matrix, and multiply on the right by $\lambda$:

$$
(\hat\beta - \beta)\lambda = \frac1T\sum_t \Err{\varepsilon_t}\,w_t,
\qquad w_t = (\Factor{f_t} - \bar f)'\,\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\lambda.
$$

Each $w_t$ is one number per month. It is large when the factor is far from its mean, and it is scaled by
$\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\lambda$: the premium measured in units of factor variance.

### Step 3: the error with estimated betas

In practice the second pass uses $\hat\beta$, so $\hat\lambda = \hat A\,\bar R^e$ with $\hat A = (\hat\beta'\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'$, and
$\hat A\hat\beta = I$. In the equation for $\bar R^e$, write $\beta = \hat\beta - (\hat\beta - \beta)$ in the first term only:

$$
\Data{\bar R^e} = \hat\beta\lambda - (\hat\beta - \beta)\lambda + \beta(\bar f - \mathbb{E}f) + \Err{\bar\varepsilon}.
$$

Apply $\hat A$. The first term gives $\lambda$, so

$$
\hat\lambda - \lambda = \hat A\beta\,(\bar f - \mathbb{E}f) + \hat A\,\Err{\bar\varepsilon} - \hat A(\hat\beta - \beta)\lambda.
$$

This is exact. Now we simplify it to first order. Each of the three terms is small, of order $1/\sqrt T$. The
matrices in front of them differ from their known-beta values by a term of order $1/\sqrt T$: $\hat A = A$ plus a
small error, and $\hat A\beta = I$ plus a small error. Replacing them changes each term by an amount of order $1/T$,
which is smaller than the terms we keep. So, in large samples,

$$
\hat\lambda - \lambda \approx \underbrace{(\bar f - \mathbb{E}f)}_{u_1}
+ \underbrace{A\,\Err{\bar\varepsilon}}_{u_2}
- \underbrace{\frac1T\sum_t A\,\Err{\varepsilon_t}\,w_t}_{u_3}.
$$

The first two pieces are those of Step 1. The third is new: it is the beta error of Step 2, passed through $A$.

### Step 4: the three pieces are uncorrelated

Fix the path of the factors. Then $u_1$ and every $w_t$ are fixed numbers, and $u_2$ and $u_3$ are sums of
residuals with mean zero. A fixed number has no covariance with a mean-zero noise, so $u_1$ is uncorrelated with
$u_2$ and $u_3$.

For $u_2$ and $u_3$, write $u_2 = \tfrac1T\sum_s A\varepsilon_s$. The residuals of different months are
independent, so only the terms with $s = t$ survive, and each gives $A\Sigma_\varepsilon A'$:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(u_2, u_3) = \frac1{T^2}\sum_t A\Sigma_\varepsilon A'\,w_t
= \frac1{T^2}A\Sigma_\varepsilon A' \sum_t w_t = 0.
$$

The sum is zero because $\sum_t w_t = \big(\sum_t (f_t - \bar f)\big)'\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\lambda$ and the deviations add up to zero.

### Step 5: add up the variances

Uncorrelated pieces have variances that add. We already have $\operatorname{Var}(u_1) = \Sigma_f/T$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(u_2) = A\Sigma_\varepsilon A'/T$. For $u_3$, the same argument as in Step 4 gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(u_3) = \frac1{T^2}\sum_t w_t^2\,A\Sigma_\varepsilon A'.
$$

The sum of the $w_t^2$ is

$$
\sum_t w_t^2 = \lambda'\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\Big[\sum_t (f_t - \bar f)(f_t - \bar f)'\Big]\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\lambda
= \lambda'\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}(T\hat\Sigma_f)\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\lambda = T\,\lambda'\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\lambda.
$$

In large samples $\hat\Sigma_f$ is close to $\Sigma_f$. So $\operatorname{Var}(u_3) = c\,A\Sigma_\varepsilon A'/T$, with
$c = \lambda'\Sigma_f^{-1}\lambda$. Add the three variances:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\lambda) = \frac1T\Big[(\beta'\beta)^{-1}\beta'\Sigma_\varepsilon\beta(\beta'\beta)^{-1}\,(1 + c) + \Sigma_f\Big],
\qquad c = \lambda'\Sigma_f^{-1}\lambda.
$$

This is the **Shanken correction**. It keeps the factor part and multiplies the residual part by $1 + c$. To use
it, plug in the estimates $\hat\beta$, $\hat\Sigma_\varepsilon$, $\hat\Sigma_f$ and $\hat\lambda$. For a second pass weighted by
$\Sigma_\varepsilon^{-1}$ (GLS), the same steps give $(\beta'\Sigma_\varepsilon^{-1}\beta)^{-1}$ in place of the residual sandwich.

### Why $c$ is a squared Sharpe ratio

With one factor, $c = \lambda^2/\sigma_f^2 = (\lambda/\sigma_f)^2$. Suppose the factor is an excess return, like Mkt-RF.
Then the model must price the factor itself. Its beta on itself is 1, so its premium is its own expected excess
return: $\lambda = \mathbb{E}f$. So $c = (\mathbb{E}f/\sigma_f)^2$, the squared Sharpe ratio of the factor.

With $K$ traded factors, again $\lambda = \mathbb{E}f$, and $c = \mathbb{E}f'\,\Sigma_f^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}f$. In
[Chapter 7](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/07-mean-variance-and-the-capm/) you saw that $\mu^{e\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e$ is
the squared Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio. Here the assets are the factors, so $c$ is the highest squared
Sharpe ratio you can get from any mix of the factors.

For a factor that is not a return, such as consumption growth, $\lambda$ is not the factor's mean. The formula still
reads $(\lambda/\sigma_f)^2$ with one factor. So a factor with a small volatility and a large estimated premium has a
large $c$, and a large correction. That is a property of the formula. Which real factors have a large $c$ is a
question for the data.

One more point about units: $c$ is per period. With monthly data it is the monthly squared Sharpe ratio, which is
the annual one divided by 12. That is why $c$ is usually small with monthly data.

### When the correction matters

For one premium, the ratio of the corrected variance to the naive variance is $1 + c\,s$. Here $s$ is the share of
the residual part in the naive variance. So two things must be large for the correction to matter: $c$, and the
residual part.

On our FF3 data, neither is large. At the estimated premia, $c = 0.0386$. That is a monthly Sharpe ratio of 0.196,
or 0.68 a year. For the market premium, the residual part is only 2.8% of the naive variance. The rest is the
factor part: the market itself is so noisy that the sampling error of its mean dominates. Together, the correction
raises the standard error of the market premium by 0.05%, from 1.970% to 1.971% a year.

In the made-up world of the Intuition, the factor has a Sharpe ratio of 5 a year, so $c = 2.08$ per month. With
$T = 600$, the average naive standard error is 0.218% a year, and the average corrected one is 0.293%. Across the
200 samples, $\hat\lambda$ really spreads by 0.279% a year. The corrected standard error is close to the truth. The
naive one is about a fifth too small.

### What the correction does not fix

The correction is honest about its own limits, and so should you be.

- **It assumes independent months.** If the factors or the residuals are autocorrelated, the variances in Steps 1
  and 5 are wrong. A later Part on GMM builds standard errors that allow for this.
- **It assumes conditional homoskedasticity.** Step 4 used the same $\Sigma_\varepsilon$ in every month. If residuals
  are larger in months when the factor moves a lot, $\sum_t w_t^2\,\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t'$ is larger on average than
  the formula says, and so is the true variance.
- **It fixes the standard error, not the estimate.** Noise in $\hat\beta$ spreads the betas out, so on average
  $\hat\beta'\hat\beta$ is larger than $\beta'\beta$. That pulls $\hat\lambda$ towards zero. This is the classic
  **errors-in-variables** bias, also called **attenuation**. In the world with a Sharpe ratio of 5 and $T = 600$,
  the average $\hat\lambda$ is 5.85% a year against a true 6%. The correction widens the interval, but it does not
  move its centre.
- **It is a large-sample result.** Step 3 dropped terms of order $1/T$. In short samples they matter.

<Callout type="warning">The Shanken correction widens the interval around a biased estimate. It assumes
independent, conditionally homoskedastic months, and it holds to first order in $1/T$.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- The second pass uses estimated betas. Treating them as known makes the standard errors of $\hat\lambda$ too small.
- To first order, $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\lambda) = \tfrac1T\big[(\beta'\beta)^{-1}\beta'\Sigma_\varepsilon\beta(\beta'\beta)^{-1}(1 + c) + \Sigma_f\big]$:
  the residual part is scaled by $1 + c$.
- $c = \lambda'\Sigma_f^{-1}\lambda$ is a squared Sharpe ratio per period. For traded factors it is the highest squared
  Sharpe ratio of any mix of the factors.
- On FF3 monthly data, $c = 0.0386$, and the standard error of the market premium moves from 1.970% to 1.971% a
  year. A factor with a high Sharpe ratio, or a small volatility and a large premium, makes the correction large.
- The correction assumes independent, conditionally homoskedastic months, and it does not fix the attenuation
  bias of $\hat\lambda$.
