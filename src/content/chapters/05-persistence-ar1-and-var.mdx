## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> How long does an expensive market stay expensive?

In June 1932, the dividends of the US stock market were 13.8% of its price. That ratio, dividends over price,
is the **dividend–price ratio**. In August 2000, its lowest month, it was 1.1%. In June 2023, the last month of our data, it was
1.58%. Over the 1,710 months from 1881 to 2023, its typical level was 3.8%.

So the ratio wanders far from its typical level, and it stays away for a long time. That gives you a practical
question. Suppose the ratio is far below normal today. How many years should you expect to wait until it is
back halfway to normal? One year? Ten?

Chapter 9 will use the dividend–price ratio to forecast stock returns. Every result there depends on the answer
to this question. This chapter gives you the tool to answer it: the AR(1) model. Then it stacks two series in
one model, the VAR(1), and forecasts them together.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> A series with a memory

Think of a cup of hot coffee in a cold room. Each minute, the coffee loses a fixed share of its gap to room
temperature. A large share means the coffee cools fast. A small share means it stays hot for a long time. An
AR(1) series works the same way, plus a small random push every month.

The number that matters is $\phi$, the **persistence**. Each month, the series keeps the share $\phi$ of its
gap from the mean, and then gets a new random shock of typical size $\sigma$. The **half-life** is the number of
months until half of today's gap is expected to be gone.

<Callout type="key">Persistence is memory. With $\phi$ close to 1, a shock today is still visible many years
later, and the half-life measures how many.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> The AR(1) and the VAR(1)

### The model

Write $\Signal{x_t}$ for the series at month $t$. An **AR(1)**, an autoregression of order one, says that next
month's value depends only on this month's value and a new shock:

$$
\Signal{x_{t+1}} = \mu_x + \phi\,\Signal{x_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}.
$$

The shock $\Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}$ has mean zero and variance $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$. It is uncorrelated with
$\Signal{x_t}$ and with every earlier value, so nobody can forecast it at $t$. The intercept $\mu_x$ is not the mean
of $x$, as the next step shows. We assume $0 \le \phi < 1$.

### The mean

If the series is **stationary**, its mean and variance are the same at every date. Take the mean of both sides.
The shock has mean zero, so $\mathbb{E}[x_{t+1}] = \mu_x + \phi\,\mathbb{E}[x_t]$. Stationarity says the two means
are the same number $\bar x$:

$$
\bar x = \mu_x + \phi\,\bar x
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\bar x = \frac{\mu_x}{1 - \phi}.
$$

Now subtract $\bar x = \mu_x + \phi\,\bar x$ from the model. The intercept drops out, and you get the gap form:

$$
\Signal{x_{t+1}} - \bar x = \phi\,(\Signal{x_t} - \bar x) + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}.
$$

This is the coffee rule. Each month keeps the share $\phi$ of the gap, and adds a shock.

### The variance

Take the variance of both sides of the model. $\mu_x$ is a constant, and $\Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}}$ is uncorrelated
with $\Signal{x_t}$, so there is no covariance term:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(x_{t+1}) = \phi^2 \operatorname{Var}(x_t) + \sigma_\varepsilon^2.
$$

Stationarity sets $\operatorname{Var}(x_{t+1}) = \operatorname{Var}(x_t) = \operatorname{Var}(x)$. Move the
$\phi^2$ term to the left and divide:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(x) = \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1 - \phi^2}.
$$

As $\phi$ gets close to 1, the bottom gets close to 0, and the variance grows without limit. Small monthly shocks
add up to large swings, because the series never forgets them. For the dividend–price ratio, the formula with
$\hat\phi = 0.9966$ and $\hat\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.043$ gives a standard deviation of 0.52. The sample standard
deviation of the log ratio is 0.45. The two are close but not equal, because $1 - \hat\phi^2$ is a small number
that the data measure only roughly.

### Forecasts many steps ahead

Write the gap form for month $t+2$, and put in the gap form for month $t+1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Signal{x_{t+2}} - \bar x &= \phi\,(\Signal{x_{t+1}} - \bar x) + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+2}} \\
&= \phi^2\,(\Signal{x_t} - \bar x) + \phi\,\Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}} + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each step multiplies the old gap by $\phi$ once more and adds one new shock. After $k$ steps:

$$
\Signal{x_{t+k}} - \bar x = \phi^k\,(\Signal{x_t} - \bar x) + \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \phi^j\,\Err{\varepsilon_{t+k-j}}.
$$

Now take the expectation given what you know at $t$. Every shock in the sum comes after $t$, so each one has
expectation zero:

$$
\mathbb{E}_t[\Signal{x_{t+k}}] - \bar x = \phi^k\,(\Signal{x_t} - \bar x).
$$

This is the many-step forecast. The expected gap shrinks by the factor $\phi$ every month, so the forecast slides
back to the mean along a curve that gets flatter as it goes.

### Autocorrelation

Multiply the $k$-step equation by $(\Signal{x_t} - \bar x)$ and take the mean. The future shocks are uncorrelated
with $\Signal{x_t}$, so only the first term survives:
$\operatorname{Cov}(x_{t+k}, x_t) = \phi^k \operatorname{Var}(x)$. Divide by $\operatorname{Var}(x)$ to get the
correlation:

$$
\operatorname{corr}(x_{t+k}, x_t) = \Model{\phi^k}.
$$

That is the dashed curve in the widget. The solid curve is the **sample autocorrelation**. At lag $k$ it puts the
sample mean $\bar x$ in place of the true one:

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{corr}}_k = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T} (x_t - \bar x)(x_{t-k} - \bar x)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T} (x_t - \bar x)^2}.
$$

### The half-life

The half-life $h$ is the horizon at which the expected gap is half of today's gap. By the forecast formula, that
means $\phi^h = 1/2$. Take logs of both sides, $h \log\phi = \log(1/2)$, and divide by $\log\phi$:

$$
h = \frac{\log(1/2)}{\log\phi}.
$$

Both logs are negative, so $h$ is positive. At $\phi = 0.5$, $h = 1$ month. At $\phi = 0.98$, $h$ is 34 months.
Close to 1, the half-life is very sensitive to $\phi$. That fact is the main limit of this chapter.

### Estimating an AR(1), and what the data cannot tell you

The AR(1) is a regression of $\Signal{x_{t+1}}$ on $\Signal{x_t}$, so OLS estimates it. On the log
dividend–price ratio from shiller-monthly, 1881 to 2023, OLS gives $\hat\phi = 0.9966$ with a classical
standard error of 0.0023. The residual standard deviation is $\hat\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.043$.

Now look at the range of two standard errors on each side. At the low end, $\phi = 0.9921$ gives a half-life of
7.3 years. At the high end, $\phi$ is 1.0012, above 1. There the series never comes back, and the half-life is
infinite. So the data are consistent with a half-life of seven years and with no half-life at all. Near 1, even
the classical standard error is only a rough guide.

The estimate also depends on the sample. From 1881 to 1951, $\hat\phi = 0.979$ and the half-life is 2.7 years.
From 1952 to 2023, $\hat\phi = 0.995$ and the half-life is 12.7 years. Perhaps the true $\phi$ changed. Perhaps
the mean of the ratio shifted down after the 1990s, which an AR(1) can only read as more persistence. These 142
years cannot tell the two stories apart.

The sample autocorrelations fall faster than the model says. At a lag of 12 months, $\hat\phi^{12} = 0.960$, but
the sample autocorrelation is 0.892. At 120 months, the model says 0.666 and the data show 0.556. This is partly a
small-sample effect, not only a defect of the model. In 200 seeded simulations of 1,710 months with
$\phi = 0.9966$, the sample autocorrelation at 120 months was below $\phi^{120}$ in 188, and its average was
0.39.

<Callout type="warning">A half-life of 17 years is a point estimate, not a fact. Near $\phi = 1$, a small error
in $\hat\phi$ is a huge error in the half-life.</Callout>

### Stacking series: the VAR(1)

Often you want to forecast two or more series that move together. Stack them in a column vector $z_t$. Here we
use the log dividend–price ratio $\Signal{dp_t}$ and the log **earnings–price ratio** $\Signal{ep_t}$, the
earnings of the market over its price, in logs:

$$
z_t = \begin{pmatrix} \Signal{dp_t} \\ \Signal{ep_t} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

A **VAR(1)**, a vector autoregression of order one, lets each series depend on last month's value of every
series. Measure $z_t$ from its mean, as in the gap form. Then:

$$
z_{t+1} = A\,z_t + \Err{\varepsilon_{t+1}},
$$

where $A$ is a $2 \times 2$ matrix:

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entry $a_{12}$ says how much this month's $ep$ moves next month's $dp$. If $a_{12} = a_{21} = 0$, the VAR is
just two separate AR(1)s.

The forecast works exactly as before, with a matrix in place of the number $\phi$. One month ahead, the shock has
expectation zero, so $\mathbb{E}_t[z_{t+1}] = A\,z_t$. Two months ahead, apply the same rule to that forecast:
$\mathbb{E}_t[z_{t+2}] = A\,\mathbb{E}_t[z_{t+1}] = A^2 z_t$. Each step multiplies by $A$ once more:

$$
\mathbb{E}_t[z_{t+k}] = A^k z_t.
$$

$A^k$ is the matrix $A$ multiplied by itself $k$ times. To estimate a VAR, run one OLS regression per series. Each
one has an intercept and last month's value of every series on the right. With a vector of intercepts $\mu_z$, the
mean solves $\bar z = \mu_z + A\bar z$, so $\bar z = (I - A)^{-1} \mu_z$, the same step as for the AR(1).

On shiller-monthly data, 1881 to 2023, OLS gives

$$
\hat A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.989 & 0.012 \\ -0.007 & 0.997 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entry 0.012 has a classical standard error of 0.0038, a $t$-statistic of 3.2. So a high earnings–price ratio,
for a given dividend–price ratio, comes before a rising dividend–price ratio. The plain reading: when companies pay
out a small share of their earnings, dividends tend to catch up later, or prices fall. The entry −0.007 has a
standard error of 0.0040, a $t$-statistic of −1.7, so that link is weaker.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- An AR(1) keeps the share $\phi$ of its gap from the mean each period:
  $\mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+k}] - \bar x = \phi^k(x_t - \bar x)$.
- Its variance is $\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(1 - \phi^2)$, its autocorrelation at lag $k$ is $\phi^k$, and its
  half-life is $\log(1/2)/\log\phi$.
- On shiller-monthly data, 1881 to 2023, the log dividend–price ratio has $\hat\phi = 0.9966$ (standard error
  0.0023) and a half-life of 17.1 years. But two standard errors span seven years to forever, and the estimate
  changes with the sample.
- A VAR(1) stacks several series: $\mathbb{E}_t[z_{t+k}] = A^k z_t$. Cross terms in $A$ let one series forecast
  another, as the earnings–price ratio does for the dividend–price ratio.
