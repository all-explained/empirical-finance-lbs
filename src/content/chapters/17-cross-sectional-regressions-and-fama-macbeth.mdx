## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Is the falling line real?

[Chapter 7](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/07-mean-variance-and-the-capm/) drew a line through 25 dots. Each dot
was one of the size and book-to-market portfolios, with its CAPM beta across and its average excess return up.
From July 1963 to August 2026, the CAPM said the line should rise by 7.2% a year per unit of beta, the market's
average excess return. The fitted line went the other way. Its slope was −4.3% a year.

So here is the question. A line through 25 dots is an estimate, and every estimate has an error. Is that falling
slope a fact about the market, or is it noise? And if it is noise, how large is the noise? Chapter 7 could not
say, because it gave the line no standard error.

This chapter gives it one. The method is the workhorse of empirical asset pricing. Run the cross-sectional
regression once in every month, and treat the 758 monthly slopes as a sample. Their average is −4.3% a year, with
a standard error of 4.8. That is a $t$-statistic of −0.91, so the data cannot tell this slope from zero. Fama &
French (1992, p. 438) found the same kind of result for single stocks from 1963 to 1990: the average monthly
slope on beta alone was 0.15% a month, only 0.46 standard errors from zero. Beta, on its own, did not explain
average returns.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> A movie of 758 lines

Picture one month, say October 1987. The market fell 23.2% that month. Each of the 25 portfolios fell too, and
the ones with a high beta fell more. Plot the 25 returns of that month against the 25 betas and draw the best
line. Its slope is steep and negative: −27.7%. In a month when the market rises, the high-beta portfolios rise
more, and the slope is positive.

So one month's slope is mostly that month's market news. It jumps around. Across the 758 months, the CAPM slope
has a standard deviation of 10.9% a month, and it is negative in 52% of the months. No single month tells you much
about the price of beta. But the *average* of the slopes is an estimate of that price, and the spread of the slopes
tells you how precise the average is. That is the whole idea. It is the same idea as estimating the equity premium
from a sample of monthly returns: a noisy series, an average, and a standard error that shrinks with $\sqrt{T}$.

The movie below plays the months one by one. The top panel is the month's cross-section: dots for the returns,
the line for the month's fit, thin lines for the gaps. The bottom panel is the running mean of all the slopes so
far, with a 95% band. The dashed line is the market's average excess return, the slope the CAPM predicts.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Two passes, then one pass a month

### The model and the first pass

A factor model says that expected excess returns line up with betas:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\Ret{R^e_i}\big] = \beta_i'\lambda, \qquad i = 1, \dots, N.
$$

Here $\beta_i$ is the $K$-vector of asset $i$'s exposures to the factors, and $\lambda$ is the $K$-vector of risk
premia, the price of one unit of each exposure. We do not know the betas. So the **first pass** estimates them with
one time-series regression for each asset, as in
[Chapter 16](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/16-time-series-tests-and-grs/):

$$
\Ret{R^e_{i,t}} = a_i + \beta_i'\Factor{f_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_{i,t}}, \qquad t = 1, \dots, T.
$$

Chapter 16 called the intercept $\alpha_i$. Here we write $a_i$, because in this chapter $\alpha$ names the
pricing error of the cross-sectional regression. The CAPM has one factor, the market's excess return. The Fama–French three-factor model (FF3) adds SMB and HML.
From here on, $\hat\beta_i$ is the first-pass estimate, and $\hat\beta$ is the $N \times K$ matrix whose row $i$
is $\hat\beta_i'$. We treat $\hat\beta$ as if it were the true $\beta$ until Chapter 18.

### The second pass: one regression across assets

Average each asset's excess return over time, $\Data{\bar R^e_i} = \tfrac1T \sum_t \Ret{R^e_{i,t}}$, and stack the
$N$ averages in a vector $\bar R^e$. The model says that $\bar R^e$ is close to $\hat\beta\lambda$. The gap is
the pricing error:

$$
\Data{\bar R^e} = \hat\beta\lambda + \Err{\alpha}.
$$

This is a regression *across assets*. There are $N$ observations, the regressors are the betas, and the
coefficients are the premia. Ordinary least squares picks $\lambda$ to make the sum of squared pricing errors
$\alpha'\alpha$ as small as possible. Write it out and set the derivative with respect to $\lambda$ to zero:

$$
\alpha'\alpha = (\bar R^e - \hat\beta\lambda)'(\bar R^e - \hat\beta\lambda), \qquad
\frac{\partial\, \alpha'\alpha}{\partial \lambda} = -2\hat\beta'(\bar R^e - \hat\beta\lambda) = 0.
$$

Move the terms with $\lambda$ to one side, $\hat\beta'\hat\beta\,\lambda = \hat\beta'\bar R^e$, and multiply by the
inverse of $\hat\beta'\hat\beta$:

$$
\hat\lambda = (\hat\beta'\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'\,\Data{\bar R^e}.
$$

The fitted mean returns are $\Model{\hat\beta_i'\hat\lambda}$, and the pricing errors are
$\Err{\hat\alpha_i} = \Data{\bar R^e_i} - \Model{\hat\beta_i'\hat\lambda}$. Many studies also add a constant,
$\lambda_0$, so that $\bar R^e_i = \lambda_0 + \hat\beta_i'\lambda + \alpha_i$. The algebra is the same with the
design matrix $X = [\mathbf{1}\ \hat\beta]$ in place of $\hat\beta$ and the coefficient vector $(\lambda_0, \lambda')'$
in place of $\lambda$. The model says $\lambda_0 = 0$: an asset with zero betas should earn no premium over the
risk-free rate. So an estimate of $\lambda_0$ far from zero is evidence against the model.

### GLS: weight the assets by their noise

OLS treats every asset the same. But some portfolios are noisier than others, and their noise is correlated.
Generalised least squares (GLS) takes this into account. It minimises $\alpha'\Sigma^{-1}\alpha$ instead, where
$\Sigma$ is the $N \times N$ covariance matrix of the returns. The same steps give

$$
\frac{\partial\, \alpha'\Sigma^{-1}\alpha}{\partial \lambda} = -2\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}(\bar R^e - \hat\beta\lambda) = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\hat\lambda_{GLS} = (\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'\Sigma^{-1}\,\Data{\bar R^e}.
$$

The weight $\Sigma^{-1}$ counts a precise combination of assets more than a noisy one. In sample,
$\hat\Sigma = \hat\beta\hat\Sigma_f\hat\beta' + \hat\Sigma_\varepsilon$, where $\Sigma_f$ is the covariance of the
factors and $\Sigma_\varepsilon$ that of the residuals. The first term only adds directions along the betas, which
the regression fits anyway. So the weight $\hat\Sigma_\varepsilon^{-1}$ gives the same $\hat\lambda_{GLS}$. Our
numbers use it.

How far can these estimates be from the factors' own averages $\Factor{\bar f}$? The first pass fits each asset's
mean exactly, $\bar R^e_i = \hat a_i + \hat\beta_i'\Factor{\bar f}$, because an OLS line passes through the means.
Stack these as $\bar R^e = \hat a + \hat\beta\Factor{\bar f}$ and put them into either estimator, with a weight
matrix $W$ ($I$ for OLS, $\Sigma^{-1}$ for GLS):

$$
\hat\lambda = (\hat\beta'W\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'W(\hat a + \hat\beta\Factor{\bar f})
= \Factor{\bar f} + (\hat\beta'W\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'W\hat a.
$$

So the cross-sectional premium is the factor's average plus a regression of the time-series intercepts $\hat a_i$
on the betas. It is close to $\bar f$ when those intercepts are small or unrelated to the betas.

On our 25 portfolios, the two weightings differ. For the CAPM without a constant, OLS gives a market premium of
8.2% a year and GLS gives 7.5%. The market's average excess return is 7.2%. For FF3, OLS gives 6.7%, 1.7% and 4.2%
a year for the market, SMB and HML, and GLS gives 7.5%, 1.8% and 3.6%. The factors' own averages are 7.2%, 1.8% and
3.5%. For the market and HML, GLS lands closer to them.

### Fama–MacBeth: a cross-sectional regression every month

The second pass gives a number but no standard error. Here is a simple way to get one. Instead of one regression
on the averages, run one regression in each month $t$ on that month's excess returns, with the same betas:

$$
\Ret{R^e_{i,t}} = \lambda_{0t} + \hat\beta_i'\lambda_t + \Err{\alpha_{i,t}}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, N.
$$

Each month gives OLS estimates $\hat\lambda_{0t}$ and $\hat\lambda_t$. With $X = [\mathbf{1}\ \hat\beta]$ and the
$N$-vector $R^e_t$ of the month's excess returns, the coefficients are
$(\hat\lambda_{0t}, \hat\lambda_t')' = (X'X)^{-1}X'R^e_t$. The estimate is the time average, and its standard
error treats the $T$ monthly slopes as a sample:

$$
\hat\lambda = \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T} \hat\lambda_t, \qquad
\mathrm{se}(\hat\lambda_k) = \frac{\sigma(\hat\lambda_{k,t})}{\sqrt T}, \qquad
\sigma^2(\hat\lambda_{k,t}) = \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\hat\lambda_{k,t} - \hat\lambda_k\right)^2.
$$

This is the cross-sectional approach of Fama and MacBeth (1973), which Fama & French (1992, p. 430) use: regress
the cross-section each month, then test the time-series means of the slopes.

On July 1963 to August 2026 ($T = 758$), with a constant:

| Model | Market | SMB | HML | Constant $\hat\lambda_0$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CAPM | −4.3% ($t$ = −0.91) | | | 13.9% ($t$ = 3.13) |
| FF3 | −6.6% ($t$ = −1.80) | 1.4% ($t$ = 1.04) | 3.9% ($t$ = 2.95) | 13.9% ($t$ = 4.50) |

All numbers are % a year (monthly slopes ×12). Only HML has a premium more than two standard errors from zero, and
the constant is large in both models, when the theory says it should be zero.

### Why Fama–MacBeth equals the two-pass estimate

Write $A = (X'X)^{-1}X'$. It does not depend on $t$, because the betas are the same in every month. Then the
average of the monthly coefficients is

$$
\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T} A\,R^e_t = A\left(\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T} R^e_t\right) = A\,\bar R^e = (X'X)^{-1}X'\,\Data{\bar R^e}.
$$

The first step takes the constant matrix $A$ out of the sum. The last expression is the second-pass OLS estimate.
So with fixed betas, Fama–MacBeth gives *exactly* the two-pass OLS estimate. On our data the two differ by less
than $10^{-14}$, which is rounding error.

The standard error has the same kind of story. The monthly coefficients are $A R^e_t$, so their sample covariance
is $A\hat\Sigma A'$, with $\hat\Sigma$ the sample covariance of the returns. Divide by $T$:

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\lambda) = \frac1T\, A\hat\Sigma A' = \frac1T (X'X)^{-1}X'\hat\Sigma X(X'X)^{-1}.
$$

This is the right OLS formula when the errors of a regression are correlated across observations. The 25 portfolios
move together every month, so their errors are strongly correlated. Fama–MacBeth takes this into account without
estimating $\Sigma$ at all: the monthly slopes carry the correlation with them.

There is also a picture behind $A$. Since $AX = (X'X)^{-1}X'X = I$, each row of $A$ is a set of portfolio weights.
The row for a factor has weights that add up to zero and a beta of one on that factor, and zero on the others. So
$\hat\lambda_t$ is the return in month $t$ of a zero-cost portfolio with a beta of one. That is why the slope fell
with the market in October 1987.

Two assumptions are hidden in the standard error. First, it treats the monthly slopes as uncorrelated over time.
If they are autocorrelated, a Newey–West correction, as in
[Chapter 4](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/04-autocorrelation-and-newey-west/), is safer. Second, it treats the
betas as known. They are estimates, and their error adds to the error of $\hat\lambda$. Chapter 18 adds that error
back.

### What the cross-sectional R² shows

The **cross-sectional $R^2$** measures how much of the spread in average returns the fitted values explain:

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i \Err{\hat\alpha_i}^2}{\sum_i \left(\Data{\bar R^e_i} - m\right)^2}, \qquad m = \frac1N\sum_i \Data{\bar R^e_i}.
$$

With a constant and OLS, it is the usual $R^2$ of a regression. Without a constant, it can be negative: the model
then prices the 25 portfolios worse than one number for all of them would.

Read it with care. With a constant, FF3 has an $R^2$ of 0.66. That looks good, but it comes with a constant of
13.9% a year and a negative market premium, and the model says the constant is zero. Without a constant, the $R^2$
of FF3 falls to 0.48, and with GLS weights it is 0.36. The CAPM without a constant has an $R^2$ of −0.59. So the
number depends on the constant and on the weights. It also has no standard error in this chapter, and 25 points
with three or four slopes are easy to fit. A high $R^2$ says the fitted values line up with the means. It does not
say the premia are right, and it is not a test. The test of the pricing errors is the GRS test of Chapter 16, or a
test on the $\hat\alpha_i$ of this chapter with the right standard errors.

Cochrane (2011, p. 1061) makes a broader point. Sorting stocks into portfolios and running a cross-sectional
regression are two ways of doing the same thing: describing average returns as a function of something you can
measure about each asset. On the next page he goes one step further. Time-series forecasts, cross-sectional
regressions and portfolio means are all views of one panel regression (Cochrane, 2011, p. 1062).

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- The first pass estimates each asset's betas with a time-series regression. The second pass regresses mean excess
  returns on those betas across assets: $\hat\lambda = (\hat\beta'\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'\bar R^e$.
- GLS weights the assets by $\Sigma^{-1}$. Either way, a premium is the factor's average plus a regression of
  the time-series intercepts on the betas.
- Fama–MacBeth runs the cross-sectional regression every month and averages the slopes, with
  $\mathrm{se}(\hat\lambda) = \sigma(\hat\lambda_t)/\sqrt T$. With fixed betas, the average equals the two-pass
  estimate exactly.
- From 1963 to 2026, the CAPM's market slope on the 25 portfolios is −4.3% a year with a $t$-statistic of −0.91,
  and the constant is far from zero. Beta alone does not price these portfolios.
- A high cross-sectional $R^2$ is a description, not a test. It depends on the constant and the weights.

small because they ignore it. The Shanken correction puts the missing error back.
