<Callout type="note" title="What you need from Chapter 2">
Chapter 2 is not written yet, so here is the short version of the tools this chapter uses.

- The **variance** of a series is the average squared distance from its mean. Its square root is the
  **standard deviation**, or volatility.
- The **covariance** of two series is the average product of their distances from their means. It is positive
  when the two tend to be above their means at the same time.
- The **correlation** is the covariance divided by both standard deviations. It runs from −1 to 1.
- The **covariance matrix** $\Sigma$ of $N$ series is a square table with $N$ rows and $N$ columns. Entry $(i, j)$
  is the covariance of series $i$ and series $j$, so the variances sit on the diagonal.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> How many things really move?

The Kenneth French Data Library sorts US stocks into 25 portfolios by two traits: size (the market value of
the company) and book-to-market (its accounting value over its market value). Companies with a high
book-to-market are called **value** stocks, and those with a low one are called **growth** stocks. The library
reports the monthly return of each portfolio from July 1926 to August 2026, which is 1,202 months.

To describe how 25 series move together, you need their 25 variances and 300 separate covariances. That is a
lot of numbers. But look at the correlations. The average correlation between two of these portfolios is 0.84,
and even the lowest is 0.63. When one portfolio has a good month, the other 24 mostly have one too.

So here is the question you probably have. If 25 series move almost as one, how many separate things are really
moving? The answer in this chapter: one direction carries 83.8% of all their variance. And in a second data set,
monthly changes in 30 Treasury yields from December 1985 to September 2026, three directions carry 98.9%. The tool
that finds these directions is called **principal components analysis**, or PCA.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> The long axis of a cloud

Start with two portfolios, so that you can see the picture. Each dot below is one month. Across is the return of
the largest growth portfolio, and up is the return of the smallest value portfolio. Their correlation is 0.67, so
the cloud is a tilted oval, not a round ball.

<PCAExplorer client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> look at the solid arrow, axis 1. It points along the long side of the
cloud, at 65° above the horizontal. The readout says it carries 88.1% of the variance of the pair. Now move the
rotation slider to 30°. Axis 1 now carries only 69.0%, and the thin lines from the dots to the axis get longer. At
45° it carries exactly 50%.</p>

Think of the cloud as a flat, long stone. If you had to describe where each dot is with one number only, you
would measure its position along the long side of the stone. That one number tells you most of what you need. The
second number, the position across the stone, only adds a small correction.

PCA finds that long side. The **first principal component** is the direction in which the cloud is most spread
out. The **second** is the direction at a right angle to the first, with the most spread that is left. With two
series there is nothing more. With 25 series, there are 25 such directions, each at a right angle to all the
ones before.

Now look at the thin lines from each dot to axis 1. They show what you lose if you keep only the first number.
The axis that carries the most variance is the same axis that makes these lines as short as possible. That is
not luck. You will see why in the next section.

<Callout type="key">The first principal component is the direction in which many series move together the most.
Keeping only that direction loses as little as possible.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Directions, variances and loadings

Write $\Data{z_t}$ for the list of the $N$ values at month $t$: the 25 portfolio returns, or the 30 yield changes.
A list like this is a **vector**. Write $\bar z$ for the vector of sample means and $\Sigma$ for the $N \times N$
covariance matrix. Below, $\Sigma$ is the sample covariance matrix with divisor $T - 1$. We drop the hats to keep
the formulas short.

### The variance of a direction

A direction is a vector $w$ of $N$ weights with length 1, which means $w'w = w_1^2 + \dots + w_N^2 = 1$. The
position of month $t$ along that direction is the weighted sum $w'(\Data{z_t} - \bar z)$. Its variance is

$$
\operatorname{Var}(w'\Data{z_t}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} w_i w_j \operatorname{Cov}(z_i, z_j) = w'\Sigma w.
$$

<details>
<summary>Every step of the algebra</summary>

1. The weighted sum is $w'\Data{z_t} = w_1 z_{1,t} + \dots + w_N z_{N,t}$.
2. The variance of a sum is the sum of all the covariances of its terms: $\operatorname{Var}(a + b) =
   \operatorname{Var}(a) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(a, b) + \operatorname{Var}(b)$, and the same pattern holds for $N$ terms.
3. A weight comes out of a covariance as a factor: $\operatorname{Cov}(w_i z_i, w_j z_j) = w_i w_j \operatorname{Cov}(z_i, z_j)$.
4. So $\operatorname{Var}(w'\Data{z_t}) = \sum_i \sum_j w_i w_j \Sigma_{ij}$, and that double sum is what the
   matrix product $w'\Sigma w$ means.

</details>

### The eigen-decomposition

A fact of linear algebra, which we use without proof, is that every covariance matrix can be written as

$$
\Sigma = Q L Q' = \ell_1 q_1 q_1' + \ell_2 q_2 q_2' + \dots + \ell_N q_N q_N'.
$$

The columns $q_1, \dots, q_N$ of $Q$ have length 1 and are at right angles to each other: $q_j'q_k = 1$ if $j = k$
and 0 otherwise. They are the **eigenvectors** of $\Sigma$. $L$ is a diagonal matrix with the **eigenvalues**
$\ell_1 \ge \ell_2 \ge \dots \ge \ell_N \ge 0$ on its diagonal. We write $\ell$, not $\lambda$, because $\lambda$
is a risk premium in this course.

### The best direction is $q_1$

Any direction $w$ can be written as a mix of the eigenvectors, $w = c_1 q_1 + \dots + c_N q_N$, with $c_k = q_k'w$.
Because the $q_k$ are at right angles and have length 1, the length of $w$ is $c_1^2 + \dots + c_N^2 = 1$. Put
this $w$ into the variance and use the decomposition:

$$
w'\Sigma w = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \ell_k (q_k'w)^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \ell_k c_k^2 \le \ell_1 \sum_{k=1}^{N} c_k^2 = \ell_1.
$$

The inequality holds because every $\ell_k$ is at most $\ell_1$. It is an equality when $c_1 = 1$ and all other
$c_k = 0$, that is, when $w = q_1$. So the direction with the most variance is $q_1$, and its variance is $\ell_1$.
Repeat the argument for directions at a right angle to $q_1$. Then $c_1 = 0$, and the best is $q_2$ with variance
$\ell_2$, and so on.

### Components, loadings and the share of variance

Component $k$ at month $t$ is the position along $q_k$:

$$
\Factor{f_{k,t}} = q_k'(\Data{z_t} - \bar z), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\Factor{f_{k,t}}) = \ell_k.
$$

The entries of $q_k$ are the **loadings**: they say how much each series moves when component $k$ moves by one.
Two components are uncorrelated, because $\operatorname{Cov}(\Factor{f_{j,t}}, \Factor{f_{k,t}}) = q_j'\Sigma q_k =
\ell_k\, q_j'q_k = 0$ for $j \ne k$.

The total variance is the sum of the $N$ variances on the diagonal of $\Sigma$. Entry $(i, i)$ of the decomposition
is $\Sigma_{ii} = \sum_k \ell_k q_{k,i}^2$. Add over $i$, and use $\sum_i q_{k,i}^2 = 1$:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N} \Sigma_{ii} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \ell_k \sum_{i=1}^{N} q_{k,i}^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \ell_k,
\qquad \text{share of component } k = \frac{\ell_k}{\sum_{j} \ell_j}.
$$

On the 25 portfolios from 1926 to 2026, the shares of the first three components are 83.8%, 4.4% and 3.1%.
Together they carry 91.2%.

### The first $K$ components lose the least

Keep the first $K$ components and rebuild each month from them:
$\hat z_t = \bar z + q_1 \Factor{f_{1,t}} + \dots + q_K \Factor{f_{K,t}}$. All $N$ components rebuild
$\Data{z_t} - \bar z$ exactly, so the part you lose is $\Err{z_t - \hat z_t} = \sum_{k > K} q_k \Factor{f_{k,t}}$.
Its squared length is $\sum_{k > K} \Factor{f_{k,t}^2}$, because the $q_k$ are at right angles. Add over the $T$
months. Each $\sum_t \Factor{f_{k,t}^2}$ is $(T - 1)\ell_k$, because $\Factor{f_{k,t}}$ has mean zero and variance
$\ell_k$:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T} \lVert \Err{z_t - \hat z_t} \rVert^2 = (T - 1) \sum_{k > K} \ell_k.
$$

No other choice of $K$ directions does better. With three components, the 25 portfolios lose 8.8% of their total
variance, and the 30 yield changes lose 1.1%.

<details>
<summary>Why no other $K$ directions do better</summary>

1. Take any $K$ directions $w_1, \dots, w_K$ of length 1 at right angles to each other. Write $e_t = \Data{z_t} - \bar z$.
   The closest point to $e_t$ in their span is $\sum_k w_k (w_k'e_t)$.
2. By Pythagoras, the squared distance is $\lVert e_t \rVert^2 - \sum_k (w_k'e_t)^2$.
3. Add over $t$. Since $\sum_t (w_k'e_t)^2 = (T - 1)\,w_k'\Sigma w_k$, the total error is
   $(T - 1)\big(\sum_j \ell_j - \sum_k w_k'\Sigma w_k\big)$. So the best directions make $\sum_k w_k'\Sigma w_k$ as large
   as possible.
4. By the decomposition, $\sum_k w_k'\Sigma w_k = \sum_j \ell_j h_j$ with $h_j = \sum_k (q_j'w_k)^2$.
5. $h_j$ is the squared length of the shadow of $q_j$ on the span of the $w_k$, so $0 \le h_j \le 1$.
   And $\sum_j h_j = \sum_k \sum_j (q_j'w_k)^2 = \sum_k w_k'w_k = K$.
6. With weights between 0 and 1 that add up to $K$, the sum $\sum_j \ell_j h_j$ is largest when the weight 1 sits on
   the $K$ largest $\ell_j$. That gives $\ell_1 + \dots + \ell_K$, which the first $K$ components reach.

</details>

### Sign and rotation: what PCA does not pin down

If $q_k$ is an eigenvector, so is $-q_k$. Flip it, and the component flips too: $\Factor{f_{k,t}}$ becomes
$-\Factor{f_{k,t}}$. The product $q_k \Factor{f_{k,t}}$, the variance $\ell_k$ and the rebuilt $\hat z_t$ all stay
the same. So the sign of a component means nothing by itself. Our library picks the sign that makes the largest
loading positive, but another program may pick the other one.

Rotation goes further. Mix the first two directions by an angle $\omega$:

$$
w_1 = \cos\omega\, q_1 + \sin\omega\, q_2, \qquad w_2 = -\sin\omega\, q_1 + \cos\omega\, q_2.
$$

Multiply out, and the cross terms cancel because $\cos\omega \sin\omega - \sin\omega \cos\omega = 0$, while
$\cos^2\omega + \sin^2\omega = 1$:

$$
w_1 w_1' + w_2 w_2' = q_1 q_1' + q_2 q_2'.
$$

The rebuilt $\hat z_t$ with two components uses only this sum, so the two rotated directions fit the data exactly
as well. Only how the variance splits between them changes: $w_1'\Sigma w_1 = \cos^2\omega\,\ell_1 +
\sin^2\omega\,\ell_2$. At $\omega = 45°$, each carries half of $\ell_1 + \ell_2$, which is why the widget showed
50%. PCA picks the one rotation that puts as much variance as possible first. It is a convention, not a law of
nature.

<Callout type="warning">A name like "size factor" or "slope" belongs to one rotation of the components, not to the
data. The fit of the first $K$ components together is fixed. How you split it among them is a choice.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Playground</span> Scree plots and loadings

Switch the widget to "scree". The bars are the shares $\ell_k / \sum_j \ell_j$, and the dashed line adds them up. A
**scree plot** is named after the loose stones at the foot of a cliff: a few tall bars, then a long flat tail.
Then switch to "loadings", which draws $q_1$, $q_2$ and $q_3$ across the 25 portfolios.

For the portfolios, every loading of component 1 is positive, from 0.13 to 0.28. All 25 portfolios move the same
way, so component 1 behaves like the whole market. Component 2 is highest for the smallest growth portfolio and
lowest for the largest value portfolio. It separates small stocks from big ones, with a growth tilt in the small
ones. Component 3 is hard to read. Its two largest loadings sit on the two corner portfolios, the smallest growth
and the largest value ones.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> choose the sample that starts in July 1963 and look again. Component 1
still carries 83.2%. But component 2 now carries 6.3% and component 3 carries 3.5%, and component 3 has a clean
shape: in every size group, its loading falls from the growth end to the value end. The same 25 portfolios give
different minor components in a different sample.</p>

Now choose "Yield changes, 1985–2026". The data are monthly changes of the 1- to 30-year zero-coupon yields. The three
shares are 89.0%, 7.7% and 2.2%, and the loadings have three shapes you can name. Component 1 loads between 0.12
and 0.20 on every maturity: all yields move up or down together, a change in the **level** of the curve.
Component 2 goes from 0.38 at one year to −0.15 at thirty years and changes sign once: short and long yields move
in opposite directions, a change in the **slope**. Component 3 is positive at both ends and negative in the middle,
so it changes sign twice: a change in the **curvature**.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> on the 25 portfolios from 1926, show the scree plot and rotate by 30°.
Component 1 drops from 83.8% to 63.9%, and component 2 grows from 4.4% to 24.2%. Their sum stays at 88.1%. Then press
"Flip the signs" in the loadings view: every line turns upside down, and no share changes.</p>

### What PCA can and cannot show

PCA describes the sample. It is not a model, and it reports no standard errors in this chapter. Each share and
loading is an estimate from one sample, and you have just seen the minor components change when the sample starts
in 1963 instead of 1926. A component whose eigenvalue is close to the next one is pinned down badly. For the
portfolios from 1926, components 3 and 4 carry 3.1% and 2.6%, so small changes in the data can mix them.

PCA also depends on units. It works on the covariance matrix, so a series with a large variance pulls the first
component towards itself. If you scale each series to variance 1 first, you get different components.

Most importantly, a component that explains much of the **variance** of returns need not explain their
**average**. PCA looks only at how returns move, never at what they pay on average. Whether a component earns a
risk premium is a separate question, and Chapter 7 starts to answer it with the CAPM.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- PCA writes the covariance matrix as $\Sigma = Q L Q'$. Component $k$ is $\Factor{f_{k,t}} = q_k'(\Data{z_t} - \bar z)$, with
  variance $\ell_k$ and share $\ell_k / \sum_j \ell_j$.
- The first $K$ components lose the least when you rebuild the data from $K$ directions: the error is
  $(T - 1)\sum_{k > K} \ell_k$.
- One component carries 83.8% of the variance of the 25 size–value portfolios from 1926 to 2026. Three carry 98.9%
  of monthly yield changes from 1985 to 2026: level, slope and curvature.
- A component is fixed only up to its sign, and a group of components only up to a rotation. Names like "slope"
  belong to one rotation.
- PCA describes how returns move. It does not say which movements earn a premium, and its minor components change
  with the sample.
