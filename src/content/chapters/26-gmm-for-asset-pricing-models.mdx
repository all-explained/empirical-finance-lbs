## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> The model that passes is the model that misses

Take the 25 portfolios sorted on size and book-to-market, in quarterly data from 1963-Q3 to 2026-Q2: 252
quarters. Write three models as stochastic discount factors: the CAPM, with the market as its factor; the
consumption CAPM (CCAPM), with consumption growth; and Fama–French 3 (FF3). Estimate each one by GMM and run the
$J$ test of its overidentifying restrictions.

The consumption CAPM passes with ease: $J = 13.0$ on 24 degrees of freedom, a p-value of 0.97. FF3 fails, with
$J = 67.7$ on 22 degrees of freedom and $p = 1.5 \times 10^{-6}$. The CAPM fails worse: $J = 91.2$ and
$p = 8.9 \times 10^{-10}$.

Now look at how far each model misses. At the first-stage estimates, the average absolute pricing error of the
consumption CAPM is 8.35% a year. For FF3 it is 0.99%, and for the CAPM 2.09%. The model that passes its test is
the model that prices worst.

This chapter explains what a GMM estimate of a factor model measures, why its first stage is an old friend, and why
each model's own $J$ test is the wrong scale for a comparison. Then it builds a scale that is the same for every
model: the Hansen–Jagannathan distance.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> A line through the origin

The stochastic discount factor $\SDF{M}$ of
[Chapter 8](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/08-euler-equation-and-sdf/) prices every excess return:
$\mathbb{E}[\SDF{M}\Ret{R^e_i}] = 0$. A linear factor model says that $\SDF{M}$ is a straight line in the factors.
With the market as the only factor, $\SDF{M} = 1 - b\,\Factor{\tilde f}$, where $\Factor{\tilde f}$ is the market
excess return minus its mean. Put this into the pricing equation and move one term across:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_i}] = b\;\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_i}\Factor{\tilde f}].
$$

The right side holds a covariance, because $\Factor{\tilde f}$ has mean zero. So the model makes one picture: plot
each portfolio's mean excess return against its covariance with the market. The points should lie on a line
through the origin, and its slope is $b$. An asset that does not move with the market earns no premium.

The widget draws that picture for the 25 portfolios. The line is the least-squares fit through the origin. The
next section shows that this line *is* the first-stage GMM estimate.

The slope is positive and more than three standard errors from zero. That looks like good news for the CAPM, but
look at the cloud. Covariances run from 0.023 to 0.047 a year, and mean excess returns from 5.3% to 14.5% a year,
with no upward pattern. If you let the line have a constant, its slope turns negative, −0.36. The line through the
origin explains why the average portfolio earns a premium. It does not explain why one portfolio earns more than
another. That is the CAPM's failure of
[Chapter 17](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/17-cross-sectional-regressions-and-fama-macbeth/), seen from a new
side.

First-stage GMM fits that proportion by least squares across the assets.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Moments, two stages and one distance

The general machinery comes from Chapter 24, *The generalized method of moments*, and Chapter 25, *Efficient and
feasible GMM*. Here are the parts this chapter needs. You pick $\theta$ to minimise $g_T(\theta)'W g_T(\theta)$ for
a weighting matrix $W$. The first-order condition is $D'W g_T(\hat\theta) = 0$, with the Jacobian
$D = \partial g_T/\partial\theta'$. Efficient GMM uses $W = \hat S^{-1}$, where $\hat S$ estimates the long-run
covariance of the moments.

### The linear SDF and its moments

With $K$ factors, the linear SDF is

$$
\SDF{M_{t+1}} = 1 - b'\Factor{\tilde f_{t+1}}, \qquad \Factor{\tilde f_{t+1}} = \Factor{f_{t+1}} - \bar f.
$$

The factors are demeaned in the sample, so $\mathbb{E}_T[M] = 1$. Why fix the mean at 1? With excess returns, if
$M$ prices the assets, so does $2M$, or any other multiple of $M$: the condition $\mathbb{E}[MR^e] = 0$ cannot see
the scale. So you must fix it. Nagel (2013) notes that any multiple gives the same pricing when the model is right, but tests
of a wrong model can depend on the choice (NBER WP, p. 13, fn. 5).

Each of the $N$ assets gives one moment, $\Ret{R^e_{i,t+1}}(1 - \Factor{\tilde f_{t+1}}'b)$. The sample mean of the
$N$ moments is

$$
g_T(b) = \mathbb{E}_T[\Ret{R^e}] - \mathbb{E}_T[\Ret{R^e}\Factor{\tilde f}']\,b.
$$

It is linear in $b$, so its Jacobian is a constant $N \times K$ matrix:

$$
D = \frac{\partial g_T}{\partial b'} = -\mathbb{E}_T[\Ret{R^e}\Factor{\tilde f}'].
$$

Because $\tilde f$ has a sample mean of zero, $\mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f'] = \tfrac1T\sum_t R^e_t(f_t - \bar f)'$ is the
sample covariance of returns and factors, with the divisor $T$. At an estimate $\hat b$, the vector
$g_T(\hat b)$ holds the $N$ pricing errors $\Err{\hat\alpha_i}$: actual mean minus fitted mean.

### First-stage GMM is a cross-sectional regression

The first stage uses $W = I$, so it minimises $g_T(b)'g_T(b)$, the sum of squared pricing errors. Write the
first-order condition $D'g_T(\hat b) = 0$ and cancel the minus sign in $D$:

$$
\mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f']'\,\big(\mathbb{E}_T[R^e] - \mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f']\,\hat b\big) = 0.
$$

Multiply out and solve for $\hat b$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat b_1 = {}&\big(\mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f']'\,\mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f']\big)^{-1} \\
&\times \mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f']'\,\Data{\mathbb{E}_T[R^e]}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the OLS formula. The "observations" are the $N$ assets, the dependent variable is the mean excess return,
the regressors are the covariances with the factors, and there is no constant. First-stage GMM is a cross-sectional
regression of mean returns on covariances.

It is also the regression of Chapter 17, on betas. The full-sample betas are $\hat\beta =
\mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f']\,\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}$, an $N \times K$ matrix with $\hat\Sigma_f$ the factors' covariance
(divisor $T$). So $\mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f'] = \hat\beta\hat\Sigma_f$. Put this in, and use that $\hat\Sigma_f$ is
symmetric and invertible:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat b_1 &= \big(\hat\Sigma_f\hat\beta'\hat\beta\hat\Sigma_f\big)^{-1}\hat\Sigma_f\hat\beta'\,\mathbb{E}_T[R^e] \\
&= \hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\big(\hat\beta'\hat\beta\big)^{-1}\hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\hat\Sigma_f\hat\beta'\,\mathbb{E}_T[R^e] \\
&= \hat\Sigma_f^{-1}\big(\hat\beta'\hat\beta\big)^{-1}\hat\beta'\,\mathbb{E}_T[R^e].
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiply both sides by $\hat\Sigma_f$:
$\hat\Sigma_f\hat b_1 = (\hat\beta'\hat\beta)^{-1}\hat\beta'\,\mathbb{E}_T[R^e]$. The right side is the
second-pass OLS estimate $\hat\lambda$ of Chapter 17, without the constant. For the CAPM on our data,
$\hat b_1 = 2.91$, and $\hat\lambda = \hat\Sigma_f\hat b_1 = 8.4\%$ a year with a standard error of 2.5%. The
standard errors here come from the GMM sandwich formula. They treat $\bar f$ and $\hat\Sigma_f$ as known, much as
the uncorrected two-pass errors treat the betas as known ([Chapter 18](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/18-errors-in-variables-and-shanken/) corrects those). The market's own mean excess
return is 7.5% a year. A traded factor's premium should equal its mean, but the first stage does not impose it,
because the market is not one of the test assets.

### Two forms, two questions: $\lambda = \Sigma_f b$

The step above holds in population too. Start from $\mathbb{E}[MR^e] = 0$ and follow three steps:

1. $\mathbb{E}[R^e] = \mathbb{E}[R^e\tilde f']\,b = \operatorname{Cov}(R^e, f')\,b$.
2. Insert $\Sigma_f^{-1}\Sigma_f$ in the middle: $\mathbb{E}[R^e] = \operatorname{Cov}(R^e, f')\Sigma_f^{-1}\,\Sigma_f b$.
3. The first part is the beta, $\beta = \operatorname{Cov}(R^e, f')\Sigma_f^{-1}$. So $\mathbb{E}[R^e] = \beta\lambda$,
   with $\lambda = \Sigma_f b$.

The SDF form and the beta form are one model. But $b$ and $\lambda$ answer different questions. $\lambda_k$ is the
premium for one unit of beta on factor $k$: is exposure to this factor paid? $b_k$ is factor $k$'s weight in the
SDF: does this factor help to price the assets, given the other factors? A factor can carry a premium and still
be redundant in the SDF. That happens when it is correlated with a factor that the SDF does need.

FF3 shows this. At the first stage, SMB has $\hat b = -0.38$ (standard error 1.35), but its premium is
$\hat\lambda = 1.93\%$ a year (standard error 1.34%). Neither number is significant at 5%, but their signs differ.
The row of $\lambda = \Sigma_f b$ for SMB explains why. It splits the premium into three parts, one per factor:
2.74% through the covariance of SMB with the market, −0.43% through its own variance, and −0.38% through HML. SMB
carries a premium because it moves with the market, which the SDF prices with $\hat b = 3.38$
(standard error 1.10).

### The second stage and the $J$ test

The second stage uses the efficient weighting. Build the moments at the first-stage estimate,
$g(x_t, \hat b_1) = R^e_t(1 - \tilde f_t'\hat b_1)$, where $x_t$ holds the date-$t$ returns and factors, and their
covariance $\hat S$, here with no Newey–West lags. Then set $W = \hat S^{-1}$ and solve again:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat b_2 = {}&\big(\mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f']'\,\hat S^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f']\big)^{-1} \\
&\times \mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f']'\,\hat S^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}_T[R^e].
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a GLS regression across assets, with weights from the moments' covariance. With $N$ moments and $K$
parameters, the test of the overidentifying restrictions is

$$
J = T\,g_T(\hat b_2)'\,\hat S^{-1}g_T(\hat b_2) \;\sim\; \chi^2_{N-K}
$$

when the model is right. The weighting decides which moments the estimate takes seriously. Nagel (2013) notes that
with an identity weighting, the estimate can put almost no weight on the moment for a traded factor's own return,
while efficient weighting prices that factor exactly in large samples (NBER WP, p. 21).

The $J$ statistic divides the pricing errors by $\hat S$, the noise of the model's *own* moments. That makes it a
poor scale for comparing models. The consumption CAPM has a first-stage $\hat b$ of 331 (standard error 181).
Quarterly consumption growth has a standard deviation of only 0.93%, so $M = 1 - 331\,\Delta\tilde c$ has a
standard deviation of about 3.1 a quarter. Its moments are mostly noise, $\hat S$ is large, and large errors
look small. The second-stage $\hat b$ is 177 (standard error 66). $M$ falls below zero whenever consumption growth
beats its mean by more than $1/177$, about 0.57 percentage points in a quarter.

### The Hansen–Jagannathan distance

The Hansen–Jagannathan distance uses one weighting for every model: the inverse second moment of the test assets.
That weighting has a clear meaning. Take a model's SDF $M$ and ask: how far is it from the nearest $M^*$ that
prices all $N$ assets exactly?

Write the gap as $M - M^* = w'R^e + \varepsilon$: a portfolio of the excess returns, with weights $w$, plus a
residual $\varepsilon$ with $\mathbb{E}_T[R^e\varepsilon] = 0$. Then:

1. Pricing needs $\mathbb{E}_T[M^*R^e] = 0$. Since $M^* = M - w'R^e - \varepsilon$ and $\mathbb{E}_T[MR^e] = g_T(b)$,
   this is $g_T(b) - \mathbb{E}_T[R^eR^{e\prime}]\,w = 0$, so $w = \mathbb{E}_T[R^eR^{e\prime}]^{-1}g_T(b)$. The
   residual does not enter.
2. The squared distance is $\mathbb{E}_T[(M - M^*)^2] = w'\,\mathbb{E}_T[R^eR^{e\prime}]\,w + \mathbb{E}_T[\varepsilon^2]$.
   The cross term vanishes because $\mathbb{E}_T[R^e\varepsilon] = 0$.
3. The nearest $M^*$ has $\varepsilon = 0$. Insert $w$:
   $\mathbb{E}_T[(M - M^*)^2] = g_T(b)'\,\mathbb{E}_T[R^eR^{e\prime}]^{-1}g_T(b)$.

The model may choose its best $b$. So

$$
\delta_{\text{HJ}} = \min_b \sqrt{g_T(b)'\,\mathbb{E}_T[\Ret{R^e}\Ret{R^{e\prime}}]^{-1}\,g_T(b)}.
$$

The payoffs here are excess returns, so the second moment is $\mathbb{E}_T[R^eR^{e\prime}]$. This is GMM with
$W = \mathbb{E}_T[R^eR^{e\prime}]^{-1}$, and that $W$ is the same whatever the model. That is the point: every model
is measured with one ruler. The distance also has a second reading. It is the largest pricing error of any
portfolio of the 25 assets, per unit of that portfolio's root-mean-square return.

<details>
<summary>The algebra: the largest pricing error, and the model with no factors</summary>

Call the weights of step 1 $w^* = \mathbb{E}_T[R^eR^{e\prime}]^{-1}g_T(b)$, so that
$g_T(b) = \mathbb{E}_T[R^eR^{e\prime}]\,w^*$. Any portfolio $w$ of the excess returns has the pricing error
$w'g_T(b) = \mathbb{E}_T[(w'R^e)(w^{*\prime}R^e)]$. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for averages gives

$$
|w'g_T(b)| \le \sqrt{\mathbb{E}_T[(w'R^e)^2]}\;\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_T[(w^{*\prime}R^e)^2]},
$$

with equality for $w = w^*$. The last root is $\sqrt{g_T(b)'\,\mathbb{E}_T[R^eR^{e\prime}]^{-1}g_T(b)}$. So the
largest pricing error per unit of root-mean-square return is the distance.

With no factors, $M = 1$ and $g_T = \mu = \mathbb{E}_T[R^e]$. The second moment is $\mathbb{E}_T[R^eR^{e\prime}] =
\Sigma + \mu\mu'$, with $\Sigma$ the covariance (divisor $T$). The squared highest Sharpe ratio of a portfolio of
the test assets is $SR^2 = \mu'\Sigma^{-1}\mu$, as in
[Chapter 16](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/16-time-series-tests-and-grs/). The Sherman–Morrison formula inverts
the sum: $(\Sigma + \mu\mu')^{-1} = \Sigma^{-1} - \Sigma^{-1}\mu\mu'\Sigma^{-1}/(1 + SR^2)$. So

$$
\delta_{\text{HJ}}^2 = \mu'(\Sigma + \mu\mu')^{-1}\mu = SR^2 - \frac{SR^4}{1 + SR^2} = \frac{SR^2}{1 + SR^2}.
$$

</details>

The model with no factors gives the benchmark. The highest quarterly Sharpe ratio of a portfolio of the 25 is 0.64
(1.28 a year), so $M = 1$ has $\delta_{\text{HJ}} = 0.540$. The same Sharpe ratio sets the tightest
Hansen–Jagannathan bound of Chapter 8. Against it: the CAPM reaches 0.513, FF3 0.488 and the consumption CAPM
0.470.

### What the two rankings can and cannot show

**The two rankings use different rulers.** In this sample they even put the three models in the same order. But
they tell different stories. The $J$ test calls the consumption CAPM fine and rejects FF3 decisively. The HJ
distance puts all three models within 0.05 of each other, and the best is only 0.07 below the model with no
factors at all.
Because each $J$ uses its own $\hat S$, a model with a noisy SDF gets a lenient test. Gospodinov, Kan & Robotti
(2019) report the same pattern on their data. Models with factors that barely move with returns look almost
perfect and pass the specification tests of continuously updated GMM and maximum likelihood. HJ-distance tests
point to severe misspecification of every model (manuscript, pp. 5–6).

**The consumption CAPM's sample is short and its factor is weak.** Our consumption data are quarterly, so 63
years give only 252 observations. Its covariances with the portfolios are tiny, so the estimate needs a huge $b$,
and small changes in the data move it a lot. A low HJ distance from such a model is not a win. Our consumption
growth comes from the quarterly consumption data behind $cay$ in
[Chapter 14](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/14-consumption-wealth-ratio/).

**The distances have no standard errors here.** The gaps of 0.02 and 0.03 between neighbouring models may be
noise. Testing them needs the sampling distribution of $\delta_{\text{HJ}}$, which this chapter does not derive.

**The normalisation matters for a wrong model.** The $J$ tests reject the CAPM and FF3, and the consumption CAPM
has the doubts above. For a wrong model, tests depend on how you normalise $M$, so another choice could move the
distances (Nagel, 2013, NBER WP, p. 13, fn. 5).

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- A linear SDF $M = 1 - b'\tilde f$ prices excess returns when $\mathbb{E}_T[R^e] = \mathbb{E}_T[R^e\tilde f']\,b$:
  mean returns are proportional to covariances with the factors.
- First-stage GMM is an OLS regression of mean returns on covariances, with no constant. Through
  $\lambda = \Sigma_f b$, it equals the two-pass estimate of Chapter 17 without the constant.
- $b$ asks whether a factor helps the SDF, given the others; $\lambda$ asks whether its exposure is paid. SMB has a
  positive premium but a loading near zero in FF3.
- Each $J$ test uses the model's own $\hat S$, so it cannot rank models. The HJ distance uses one weighting for
  all: on the 25 portfolios it gives 0.513 for the CAPM, 0.488 for FF3 and 0.470 for the consumption CAPM, against
  0.540 with no factors.
