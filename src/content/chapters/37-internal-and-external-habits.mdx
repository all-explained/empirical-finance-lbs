## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Fear that comes and goes

Chapter 35 left two puzzles. With annual consumption since 1930, power utility reaches the market's Sharpe ratio
of 0.45 only at $\gamma = 20.0$. At that $\gamma$ the model's real risk-free rate is 28.2% a year, while Treasury
bills paid 0.24%. Chapter 9 added a third fact that power utility with i.i.d. consumption growth cannot produce at all: expected
returns move.
Over ten years, a low price compared with dividends came before high returns, with an $R^2$ of 11.8%.

Each puzzle asks for something the same model cannot give. The premium asks for a lot of fear. The low rate asks
for little fear, or at least for a saver who is not desperate to borrow. Predictable returns ask for a fear that
changes over time.

So here is the question. What if people do not fear a fall in consumption as such, but a fall compared with what
they are used to? Then a small fall after a boom hurts little, and the same fall in a slump, when consumption is
already close to its usual level, hurts a lot. Can one modest curvature then look like a huge and changing fear of
risk?

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Consumption measured against a habit

Call the level of consumption people are used to the **habit**, $X_t$. Utility depends on $C_t - X_t$, the part
of consumption above the habit. Now suppose the habit is 94.3% of consumption, so the surplus $C_t - X_t$ is only
5.7% of $C_t$. A 1% fall in consumption then takes away 1% of $C_t$, which is a 17.5% fall in the surplus.
Measured against the habit, smooth consumption is not smooth at all.

How does the habit form? There are two answers.

- **Internal habit.** Your habit comes from *your own* past consumption. Then a meal out tonight raises the
  standard you will judge tomorrow by, and you know it. That extra cost lowers the value of consuming today.
- **External habit.** Your habit comes from *everyone else's* consumption. You take it as given, because your own
  dinner does not move the average. Campbell & Cochrane (1999, pdf p. 4) use this kind and link it to
  Abel's (1990) "catching up with the Joneses".

Does the choice matter for prices? Campbell & Cochrane (pdf p. 37) answer with a simple point. Prices depend only
on *ratios* of marginal utilities, such as $\SDF{M_{t+1}}$. If an internal habit lowers marginal utility by the
same proportion at every date, prices do not change at all.

Test this on a seeded century of monthly consumption with the simple habit $X_t = hC_{t-1}$. With $h = 0.5$, the
internal-habit marginal utility is between 0.49 and 0.52 times the external one, so prices would hardly move.
With $h = 0.8$, the ratio swings from 0.11 to 0.29, and prices would differ. In their own model, the authors find
the two marginal utilities nearly proportional near the steady state, and further apart far from it (pdf p. 38).
Their internal-habit version gives a risk-free rate that is higher and moves with the state, so returns are less
predictable (pdf p. 39). They name Constantinides (1990) among models where people adjust consumption slowly
after a wealth shock (pdf p. 37). External habits are the simpler choice, and the rest of this chapter uses them.

reverts slowly. Watch the middle panel. The **surplus ratio** $S_t = (C_t - X_t)/C_t$ wanders around 0.057, the
steady state, and falls in slumps. The price of a claim to all future consumption follows it.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> The Campbell–Cochrane model

### Preferences and local curvature

People maximise

$$
\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\delta^t\,\frac{(C_t - X_t)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}.
$$

The paper subtracts 1 in the numerator (pdf p. 4), which changes nothing that matters for prices. Define the
surplus ratio $S_t = (C_t - X_t)/C_t$ and its log $s_t = \log S_t$. $S_t$ lies between 0 and 1: near 0, consumption
barely covers the habit.

How averse to risk is a person at date $t$? Take $u(C) = (C - X)^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ with $X$ fixed. Then
$u'(C) = (C - X)^{-\gamma}$ and $u''(C) = -\gamma(C - X)^{-\gamma-1}$, so the local curvature is

$$
-\frac{C\,u''(C)}{u'(C)} = \frac{\gamma\,C}{C - X} = \frac{\gamma}{S_t}.
$$

With power utility, $S_t = 1$ and the curvature is $\gamma$. At Table 1's steady state, $\bar S = 0.057$ and
$\gamma = 2$, so $\gamma/\bar S = 35.1$. The paper writes this curvature $\eta_t$; we write $\gamma/S_t$, because
$\eta_t$ is the conditional SDF intercept of Chapter 22.

### How the habit moves

Consumption growth is i.i.d. normal, $\Delta c_{t+1} = \mu_c + \sigma_c\varepsilon_{t+1}$ with $\varepsilon_{t+1}
\sim N(0, 1)$, and the log surplus ratio follows (pdf p. 5)

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_{t+1} = {} & (1 - \phi)\bar s + \phi s_t \\
& + \Lambda(s_t)(\Delta c_{t+1} - \mu_c).
\end{aligned}
$$

Without the last term, $s_t$ is the AR(1) of [Chapter 5](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/05-persistence-ar1-and-var/)
with persistence $\phi$ and mean $\bar s$. Table 1 sets $\phi^{12} = 0.87$ a year, a half-life of 5.0 years, so
the habit catches up with consumption only slowly. The **sensitivity function** $\Lambda(s_t)$ says how much a
consumption shock moves the surplus. The curriculum and the paper write $\lambda(s_t)$, $g$ and $\phi$; here they
are $\Lambda(s_t)$, because $\lambda$ is a risk premium, and $\mu_c$, because $g$ is the GMM moment function. The
persistence keeps its letter $\phi$.

### The stochastic discount factor

The habit is external, so marginal utility is $(C_t - X_t)^{-\gamma} = S_t^{-\gamma}C_t^{-\gamma}$, and the SDF of
Chapter 8 becomes

$$
\SDF{M_{t+1}} = \delta\left(\frac{S_{t+1}C_{t+1}}{S_tC_t}\right)^{-\gamma}.
$$

In logs, $\log\SDF{M_{t+1}} = \log\delta - \gamma(\Delta c_{t+1} + s_{t+1} - s_t)$. Use the law of motion for
$s_{t+1} - s_t = -(1 - \phi)(s_t - \bar s) + \Lambda(s_t)\sigma_c\varepsilon_{t+1}$ and for $\Delta c_{t+1}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\SDF{M_{t+1}} = {} & \log\delta - \gamma\mu_c \\
& + \gamma(1 - \phi)(s_t - \bar s) \\
& - \gamma\sigma_c\big(1 + \Lambda(s_t)\big)\varepsilon_{t+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Given $s_t$, this is normal, with standard deviation $\gamma\sigma_c(1 + \Lambda(s_t))$.

### The price of risk

The lognormal fact of Chapter 35 gives $\sigma_t(\SDF{M})/\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M}] = \sqrt{e^{v} - 1}$ with $v$ the
variance of $\log\SDF{M}$. So the highest conditional Sharpe ratio, by the bound of Chapter 8, is (pdf p. 7)

$$
\frac{\sigma_t(\SDF{M_{t+1}})}{\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M_{t+1}}]} \approx \gamma\sigma_c\big(1 + \Lambda(s_t)\big).
$$

If $\Lambda$ falls as $s_t$ rises, the price of risk is high in slumps. At the steady state it is about 0.53 a year
(the monthly value times $\sqrt{12}$). Power utility with the same $\gamma = 2$ gives $\gamma\sigma_c = 0.03$.

### Choosing $\Lambda$: a constant risk-free rate

The risk-free rate is $\Rf{r^f_t} = -\log\mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M_{t+1}}]$. Apply the lognormal fact to the SDF above
(pdf p. 7):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Rf{r^f_t} = {} & -\log\delta + \gamma\mu_c - \gamma(1 - \phi)(s_t - \bar s) \\
& - \tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma_c^2\big(1 + \Lambda(s_t)\big)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The term in $s_t - \bar s$ is intertemporal substitution. In a slump, marginal utility is high and expected to
fall as $s_t$ reverts, so people want to borrow, which pushes the rate up. The last term is precautionary saving:
more risk makes people save, which pushes the rate down. If $\Lambda(s_t)$ is large in slumps, the two can cancel.

Campbell & Cochrane ask for three things (pdf p. 8): a constant risk-free rate, a habit that does not move with
consumption at the steady state, and one that does not move near it. Here is how they pin down $\Lambda$.

1. A constant rate needs the last two terms to sum to a constant, so
   $(1 + \Lambda(s_t))^2 = A - 2(1 - \phi)(s_t - \bar s)/(\gamma\sigma_c^2)$ for some number $A$.
2. The habit does not move at $\bar s$ when $\Lambda(\bar s) = 1/\bar S - 1$ (shown below), so $A = 1/\bar S^2$.
3. It does not move near $\bar s$ when $\Lambda'(\bar s) = -1/\bar S$. Differentiate step 1 at $\bar s$:
   $2(1/\bar S)\Lambda'(\bar s) = -2(1 - \phi)/(\gamma\sigma_c^2)$, so $1/\bar S^2 = (1 - \phi)/(\gamma\sigma_c^2)$.

That gives the steady state and the sensitivity function (pdf p. 8):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar S &= \sigma_c\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{1 - \phi}}, \\
\Lambda(s_t) &= \frac{\sqrt{1 - 2(s_t - \bar s)}}{\bar S} - 1.
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>The algebra: why the habit does not move at and near the steady state</summary>

Log habit is $x_t = c_t + \log(1 - e^{s_t})$, since $X_t = C_t(1 - S_t)$. Given $s_t$, the law of motion gives
$\partial s_{t+1}/\partial c_{t+1} = \Lambda(s_t)$, so

$$
\frac{\partial x_{t+1}}{\partial c_{t+1}} = 1 - \frac{e^{s_{t+1}}}{1 - e^{s_{t+1}}}\Lambda(s_t).
$$

At $s_t = s_{t+1} = \bar s$ this is $1 - \Lambda(\bar s)\bar S/(1 - \bar S)$. It is zero when
$\Lambda(\bar s) = (1 - \bar S)/\bar S = 1/\bar S - 1$, which is step 2.

For step 3, write $f(s) = 1 - \Lambda(s)e^s/(1 - e^s)$, the same derivative with $s_t = s_{t+1} = s$. Its slope is
$f'(s) = -\Lambda'(s)e^s/(1 - e^s) - \Lambda(s)e^s/(1 - e^s)^2$. At $\bar s$, put in $\Lambda(\bar s) = (1 -
\bar S)/\bar S$: $f'(\bar s) = -\Lambda'(\bar s)\bar S/(1 - \bar S) - 1/(1 - \bar S)$. It is zero when
$\Lambda'(\bar s) = -1/\bar S$ (pdf p. 9).
</details>

Now put $\Lambda$ back into the rate. Since $(1 + \Lambda(s_t))^2 = (1 - 2(s_t - \bar s))/\bar S^2$ and
$\gamma^2\sigma_c^2/\bar S^2 = \gamma(1 - \phi)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \tfrac12\gamma^2\sigma_c^2\big(1 + \Lambda(s_t)\big)^2 \\
& \quad = \tfrac12\gamma(1 - \phi) - \gamma(1 - \phi)(s_t - \bar s).
\end{aligned}
$$

The terms in $s_t - \bar s$ cancel, and the rate is the same at every date (pdf p. 8):

$$
\Rf{r^f} = -\log\delta + \gamma\mu_c - \tfrac12\gamma(1 - \phi).
$$

The square root in $\Lambda$ reaches zero at $s_{\max} = \bar s + (1 - \bar S^2)/2$. Above it, $\Lambda$ is set to
0. At Table 1's values, $\bar S = 0.057$ and $s_{\max} = -2.37$, so $S_{\max} = 0.094$. Because $\Lambda(s_t)$
grows without limit as $s_t$ falls, the price of risk is highest in the deepest slumps.

### Prices and expected returns

A claim to consumption pays $C_{t+1}$ next month and is then worth $P_{t+1}$. Its price–consumption ratio solves
(pdf p. 11)

$$
\frac{P_t}{C_t} = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\SDF{M_{t+1}}\,\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\left(1 + \frac{P_{t+1}}{C_{t+1}}\right)\right].
$$

$s_t$ is the only state, so $P_t/C_t$ is a function of $s_t$, found numerically on a grid. Our library solves it as
the sum of the prices of all future months of consumption. At Table 1's values the ratio is 41.6 years at
$S_{\max}$, 34.1 at $\bar S$ and 24.0 at $S_t = 0.01$. A low surplus means a high price of risk, a low price and
therefore a high expected return. Because both the price and the expected return depend only on $s_t$, the price
forecasts returns.

<Callout type="warning" title="What the evidence can and cannot show">
On 5,000 simulated years, our solution gives a typical price–consumption ratio, $e^{\mathbb{E}[\Signal{pc_t}]}$, of
35.1 years. Campbell & Cochrane
report 18.3 (Table 2, pdf p. 20). The price of a long claim depends on rare paths on which $s_t$ falls far below
anything a century visits, where $S_t^{-\gamma}$ is huge. So the price level depends on how deep slumps are priced,
and grids that stop at different depths give different levels. The shape, the Sharpe ratio and the predictability
do not depend much on this.
</Callout>

### The answer to Chapter 35

The table puts the model at Table 1's parameters next to Chapter 35. The model's moments come from 5,000 seeded
years, simulated monthly and summed to years, as the paper computes its statistics from time-averaged annual data
(pdf p. 20). [Chapter 36](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/36-measuring-consumption/) shows why that matters:
annual consumption in the national accounts is an average over the year, which smooths it.

| | Power utility (Chapter 35) | Habit model | Data (Chapter 35) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\gamma$ | 20.0 | 2 | |
| Local curvature | 20.0 | 35.1 at $\bar S$ | |
| $\delta$, a year | 0.99 | 0.896 | |
| $\Rf{r^f}$ | 28.2% | 0.94% | 0.24% |
| Sharpe ratio | 0.45 | 0.47 | 0.45 |

Read the table with care. Campbell & Cochrane *choose* $\delta$ to match the T-bill rate and $\gamma$ to match the
Sharpe ratio of postwar stocks (pdf p. 12). So the model does not predict these two numbers. What it shows is
that it can match both with $\gamma = 2$ and $\delta$ below 1, where power utility needed $\delta = 1.245$ in
Chapter 35. In the rate, impatience $-\log\delta$ adds 11.0% a year and growth $\gamma\mu_c$ adds 3.78%. The
term $\tfrac12\gamma(1 - \phi)$, the part of precautionary saving left after the cancellation, takes away 13.85%.
The model's Sharpe ratio is that of an unlevered claim to consumption: its excess return has a mean of 3.85% and
a volatility of 8.2% a year, about half of the paper's 6.64% and 15.2%.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- With utility over $C_t - X_t$, the local curvature is $\gamma/S_t$: 35.1 at Table 1's steady state with
  $\gamma = 2$, and higher in slumps.
- The SDF's volatility is $\gamma\sigma_c(1 + \Lambda(s_t))$, so the price of risk rises when the surplus falls.
- $\Lambda(s_t)$ is chosen so that precautionary saving offsets intertemporal substitution: the risk-free rate is
  constant, 0.94% a year at Table 1's values, with $\delta = 0.896$.
- A low surplus means a low price–consumption ratio and a high expected return, so the price forecasts returns,
  more strongly at long horizons.
- An internal habit changes prices only as much as it changes the ratio of marginal utilities over time.
