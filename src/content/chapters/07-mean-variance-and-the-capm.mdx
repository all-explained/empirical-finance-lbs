<Callout type="note" title="What you need from Chapters 2 and 3">
Chapter 2 is not written yet, so the first three points give the short version of what this chapter needs from
it. The last point recaps [Chapter 3](../03-regression-and-standard-errors/).

- **Expected return and volatility.** The expected return $\mu$ is the average return you plan for. The
  volatility $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of the return: how far it usually lands from $\mu$.
- **Covariance.** $\operatorname{Cov}(R_i, R_j)$ measures whether two returns move together. It is positive when
  they tend to rise and fall at the same time. For $N$ assets, the covariances form the $N \times N$ matrix $\Sigma$.
- **Sharpe ratio.** The reward per unit of risk: the expected excess return over its volatility,
  $SR = \mathbb{E}[R^e]/\sigma(R^e)$.
- **Regression.** Ordinary least squares (OLS) fits a line $y = a + b\,x$. With one regressor, the slope is
  $\operatorname{Cov}(x, y)/\operatorname{Var}(x)$, and every estimate comes with a standard error.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Which risk does the market pay for?

Take two groups of small US companies. The first group is **growth** stocks: their price is high compared with
the accounting value of the firm (the book value). The second group is **value** stocks: their price is low
compared with book value. From July 1963 to August 2026, small growth stocks earned 4.0% a year more than
Treasury bills, on average. Small value stocks earned 13.4% a year more.

Now look at risk. Small growth stocks were the riskier group by the usual measure: their volatility was 27.6% a
year, against 21.7% for small value. So the riskier group paid *less*. The gap of 9.4 percentage points a year has
a standard error of 1.9, so it is about five standard errors away from zero.

So here is the question you probably have. If risk earns a reward, why did the riskier stocks pay less? This
chapter gives the classic answer: the Capital Asset Pricing Model, or **CAPM**. It says that volatility is the
wrong measure of risk. Only one part of the risk earns a reward. Then it puts the CAPM to the data, and the data
do not agree.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Mixing assets, and the one portfolio everyone wants

Start with a simple question. You can hold a few risky portfolios. How should you mix them?

Each mix has an expected return and a volatility, so each mix is a point on a chart of expected return against
volatility. Mixing helps because the assets do not move in perfect step. When one falls, another often holds up,
so the mix moves less than the average of its parts. Push this idea as far as it goes, and for each target
expected return you find the mix with the least volatility. Those best mixes form a curve: the **minimum-variance
frontier**.

Now add a safe asset, Treasury bills, that pays the risk-free rate $\Rf{R^f}$ with no volatility. Any straight
line from $\Rf{R^f}$ to a risky mix shows what you get by splitting your money between the two. The steepest such
line touches the frontier at one point: the **tangency portfolio**. That line is the **capital market line**. Its
slope is the Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio, the highest reward per unit of risk you can get.

The widget below builds this picture from four of the 25 size and book-to-market portfolios of Fama and French
(1993). "Small" and "big" are the smallest and largest fifth of firms by market value. "Growth" and "value" are
the lowest and highest fifth by book-to-market ratio.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> look at the tangency weights first. The best mix is 149% small

Two lessons come out of this widget. First, the answer to "how should I mix them?" depends on every asset's
expected return and every covariance at once. The weights are not a ranking of the assets. Second, the weights
are very sensitive. A shift of 2 percentage points is smaller than the standard error of small value's average
return, which is 2.7. You cannot tell those two expected returns apart with 63 years of data, yet they give
quite different portfolios.

Here is the step that leads to the CAPM. Suppose every investor sees the same picture and wants the highest
Sharpe ratio. Then every investor holds the *same* tangency portfolio, plus more or less of the safe asset. If
everyone holds the same risky portfolio, that portfolio must be the whole market. In equilibrium, the market
portfolio is the tangency portfolio.

<Callout type="key">With a safe asset, every mean–variance investor holds the same risky portfolio: the tangency
portfolio. If all investors agree on the inputs, that portfolio is the market.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> The frontier, the tangency portfolio and beta

Write $\mu$ for the $N$-vector of expected returns, $\Sigma$ for their covariance matrix and $w$ for the
portfolio weights. A portfolio with weights $w$ has

$$
\mu_p = w'\mu, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = w'\Sigma w.
$$

For two assets, $\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\operatorname{Cov}(R_1, R_2)$. The
last term is where diversification lives. When the covariance is small or negative, the mix has a lower
variance than its parts.

### The minimum-variance frontier

For a target mean $\mu_p$, find the fully invested portfolio with the least variance:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\min_w\ \tfrac12\, w'\Sigma w \\
&\text{subject to}\quad w'\mu = \mu_p,\quad w'\mathbf 1 = 1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The $\tfrac12$ only tidies the algebra. Attach a Lagrange multiplier to each constraint. We call them $\eta_1$
and $\eta_2$, because $\lambda$ is kept for risk premia. Setting the derivative with respect to $w$ to zero gives

$$
\Sigma w = \eta_1\,\mu + \eta_2\,\mathbf 1.
$$

Multiply both sides by $\Sigma^{-1}$:

$$
w = \eta_1\,\Sigma^{-1}\mu + \eta_2\,\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1.
$$

So every frontier portfolio is a mix of the same two portfolios, $\Sigma^{-1}\mu$ and $\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$.
The constraints fix $\eta_1$ and $\eta_2$.

<details>
<summary>The algebra: solving for the multipliers and the frontier</summary>

Define three numbers, $A = \mathbf 1'\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$, $B = \mathbf 1'\Sigma^{-1}\mu$ and
$C = \mu'\Sigma^{-1}\mu$, and let $D = AC - B^2$. Put $w = \eta_1\Sigma^{-1}\mu + \eta_2\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$
into the two constraints:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w'\mu &= \eta_1 C + \eta_2 B = \mu_p, \\
w'\mathbf 1 &= \eta_1 B + \eta_2 A = 1.
\end{aligned}
$$

These are two linear equations in two unknowns. Their solution is

$$
\eta_1 = \frac{A\mu_p - B}{D}, \quad \eta_2 = \frac{C - B\mu_p}{D}.
$$

The variance follows without more work. Since $\Sigma w = \eta_1\mu + \eta_2\mathbf 1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_p^2 = w'\Sigma w &= \eta_1\, w'\mu + \eta_2\, w'\mathbf 1 \\
&= \eta_1\mu_p + \eta_2 \\
&= \frac{A\mu_p^2 - 2B\mu_p + C}{D}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a parabola in $(\sigma_p^2, \mu_p)$, so the frontier is a hyperbola in $(\sigma_p, \mu_p)$. It needs
$D > 0$, which holds when the assets do not all have the same mean.

</details>

### The tangency portfolio and the highest Sharpe ratio

Now add the safe asset. Put weights $w$ in the risky assets and $1 - w'\mathbf 1$ in Treasury bills. The weights on
the risky assets no longer need to sum to one. Write $\mu^e = \mu - \Rf{R^f}\mathbf 1$ for the vector of expected
excess returns. The portfolio's excess return has mean $w'\mu^e$ and variance $w'\Sigma w$, since the safe asset
adds no variance.

Find the least variance for a target excess mean. There is one constraint now, so one multiplier, $\eta$:

$$
\min_w\ \tfrac12\, w'\Sigma w \quad \text{subject to}\quad w'\mu^e = \mu^e_p.
$$

Setting the derivative to zero, and then multiplying by $\Sigma^{-1}$, gives

$$
\Sigma w = \eta\,\mu^e
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
w = \eta\,\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e.
$$

Every efficient portfolio holds the risky assets in the same proportions, $\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e$. Only the scale
$\eta$ changes. Scale the weights to sum to one, and you get the tangency portfolio:

$$
w^* = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e}{\mathbf 1'\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e}.
$$

What Sharpe ratio does it reach? For any $w = \eta\,\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e$ with $\eta > 0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
SR &= \frac{w'\mu^e}{\sqrt{w'\Sigma w}}
= \frac{\eta\,\mu^{e\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e}{\sqrt{\eta^2\,\mu^{e\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma\,\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e}} \\
&= \frac{\eta\,\mu^{e\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e}{\eta\sqrt{\mu^{e\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e}}
= \sqrt{\mu^{e\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e}.
\end{aligned}
$$

So $SR^2_{\max} = \mu^{e\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e$. The capital market line is
$\mu_p = \Rf{R^f} + SR_{\max}\,\sigma_p$. For uncorrelated assets, $\Sigma$ is diagonal and $SR^2_{\max}$ is the
sum of the assets' squared Sharpe ratios. Each new independent bet adds to the best reward.

### From the tangency portfolio to the CAPM

The CAPM of Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965) adds assumptions. Investors care only about the mean and variance of
next period's return. They agree on $\mu$ and $\Sigma$. They can borrow and lend at $\Rf{R^f}$, and there are no
taxes or trading costs. Then, as the intuition section said, everyone holds the tangency portfolio, so the market
weights $w_m$ satisfy $\Sigma w_m = \eta\,\mu^e$ for some $\eta > 0$.

Read that equation one row at a time. The $i$-th entry of $\Sigma w_m$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textstyle\sum_j w_{m,j}\operatorname{Cov}(R_i, R_j) \\
&\quad= \operatorname{Cov}\big(R_i, \textstyle\sum_j w_{m,j} R_j\big) \\
&\quad= \operatorname{Cov}(R_i, R_m),
\end{aligned}
$$

because the market return is $R_m = \sum_j w_{m,j}R_j$. So
$\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_i}] = \operatorname{Cov}(R_i, R_m)/\eta$ for every asset. Apply this to the market itself:
$\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_m}] = \operatorname{Var}(R_m)/\eta$, so $1/\eta = \mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_m}]/\operatorname{Var}(R_m)$.
Put that back in:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_i}] = \beta_i\,\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_m}], \\
\beta_i = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\operatorname{Var}(R_m)}.
\end{gathered}
$$

This is why only beta earns a premium. An asset's volatility has two parts: the part that moves with the market,
and the part that does not. The second part cancels out across the many assets in the market portfolio. What an
asset adds to the risk of the portfolio that everyone holds is its covariance with the market. Beta is that
covariance, measured in units of market variance.

### Estimating betas and the security market line

Beta is a regression slope. For each asset, run the **time-series regression** over the months
$t = 1, \dots, T$:

$$
\Ret{R^e_{i,t}} = \Err{\alpha_i} + \beta_i\,\Ret{R^e_{m,t}} + \Err{\varepsilon_{i,t}}.
$$

The OLS slope $\hat\beta_i$ is the sample covariance over the sample variance, as in
[Chapter 3](../03-regression-and-standard-errors/). The OLS line
passes through the sample means, so $\Data{\bar R^e_i} = \Err{\hat\alpha_i} + \hat\beta_i\,\bar R^e_m$. In words,
the **alpha** is the average excess return minus the CAPM prediction. The CAPM says every $\Err{\alpha_i}$ is
zero.

Plot each asset's average excess return against its beta. The CAPM says the dots lie on the **security market
line**: a line through the origin with slope $\mathbb{E}[\Ret{R^e_m}]$. On this plot, the alphas are the vertical
gaps between the dots and the line.

<Callout type="warning">Every input on this page is an estimate. The frontier, the tangency weights and the betas
all use sample means and covariances. Sample means are noisy, so the in-sample best Sharpe ratio tends to be higher than
what you could have earned by choosing weights in advance.</Callout>

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this too:</strong> switch the sample to 1926–2026. The dots move closer to the

The CAPM line fits the 25 portfolios badly. From July 1963 to August 2026, the market's average excess return
is 7.2% a year, with a standard error of 1.9. That is the slope the CAPM predicts. The fitted line has a slope of
−4.3%: across these portfolios, a higher beta goes with a slightly *lower* average return. The betas only range
from 0.86 to 1.42, while the averages range from 4.0% to 13.4%. The alphas run from −6.3% to +5.7% a year.
The pattern is not random. In every size group, the value portfolio has a higher alpha than the growth portfolio.

The full sample from July 1926 tells a milder story. There, the market premium is 8.3% a year, and the fitted
line has a slope of 4.8% and an intercept of 4.9%. The line still rises less than the CAPM says, and it starts
above zero.

Be careful about what this picture can show. Each alpha has a $t$-statistic, but 25 $t$-statistics are not a
test of the CAPM: they are correlated, and some will look large by luck. The betas are estimates too, and the
dashed line ignores their error. The CAPM's market is all wealth, while the data use the US stock market. A
proper test asks whether all 25 alphas are zero *jointly*. Chapter 16 builds that test, and Chapters 17 and 18
handle the cross-sectional line and its errors. Chapter 19 asks what the size and value patterns mean.

<Callout type="key">On the 25 size and book-to-market portfolios, average returns do not line up with beta. Value
stocks earn more than their betas predict, small growth stocks earn much less, and since 1963 the fitted line
falls as beta rises.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- The minimum-variance frontier holds the least-risky portfolio for each target mean. Every frontier portfolio
  mixes $\Sigma^{-1}\mu$ and $\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$.
- With a safe asset, the best risky portfolio is the tangency portfolio $w^* \propto \Sigma^{-1}\mu^e$, and
  $SR^2_{\max} = \mu^{e\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mu^e$. Its weights swing a lot when the expected returns move by less
  than their standard errors.
- If everyone holds the tangency portfolio, it is the market, and $\mathbb{E}[R^e_i] = \beta_i\,\mathbb{E}[R^e_m]$
  with $\beta_i = \operatorname{Cov}(R_i, R_m)/\operatorname{Var}(R_m)$. Only beta earns a premium.
- On ff25-size-bm-monthly data from July 1963 to August 2026, the fitted line of average excess return on beta has
  a slope of −4.3%, against 7.2% in the CAPM. The alphas run from −6.3% to +5.7% a year. The formal tests come in
  Part IV.
