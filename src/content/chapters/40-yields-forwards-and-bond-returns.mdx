## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> A safe bond that lost 17% in a year

At the end of 2021, the US Treasury's 10-year zero-coupon yield was 1.59%. Suppose you bought that bond and sold it a
year later. The Treasury was always going to pay, yet your bond lost 17.16% of its value. A 30-year bond bought the
same day lost 42.67%. Nothing defaulted. Interest rates rose, and the price of a promise to pay later fell.

That year was unusual, but the pattern behind it is not. Lay the yields of all maturities side by side and you get
the **yield curve**. We have it at the end of every month from November 1985 to September 2026, 479 months, in the
`gsw-zero-coupon` data. In 417 of those months (87.1%) the 10-year yield was above the 1-year yield, and over all months
the gap averaged 1.30 percentage points. In the other 62 months the curve was **inverted**: short rates were higher than long ones.

So here are the questions this chapter answers. What does a yield tell you that a price does not? Why does a long
yield usually sit above a short one? And if you buy a bond and sell it a year later, where does your return come
from?

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Four views of one curve

A zero-coupon bond pays 1 dollar at one future date and nothing before. Its price today and the date of that
payment fix everything else. The **yield** is the constant rate at which the price grows to 1 by that date. The
**forward rate** for year $n$ is the rate you can lock in today for lending from year $n - 1$ to year $n$. The
**par yield** is the coupon a bond must pay each year to be worth exactly its face value. These are four views of
the same prices.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Prices, yields, forwards and returns

We follow the notation of Cochrane & Piazzesi (2005, pp. 140–141). Time runs in years, and $n$ is a maturity in
years, always written as a superscript in parentheses.

### Prices and yields

Let $P^{(n)}_t$ be the price at $t$ of a zero-coupon bond that pays 1 at $t + n$, and $p^{(n)}_t = \log P^{(n)}_t$
its log price. The log yield is the rate that turns the price into 1 over $n$ years:

$$
P^{(n)}_t = e^{-n\,y^{(n)}_t}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
y^{(n)}_t = -\frac{p^{(n)}_t}{n}.
$$

At the end of September 2026, $y^{(10)}_t = 5.28\%$, so $p^{(10)}_t = -10 \times 0.0528 = -0.528$ and the
bond costs $P^{(10)}_t = 0.590$. The 30-year bond costs only 0.183. The GSW yields are continuously compounded,
so they are log yields already.

The one-year bond is the safe asset for a one-year investor. In
[Chapter 8](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/08-euler-equation-and-sdf/), a price is the expected discounted payoff,
$P_t = \mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M_{t+1}}X_{t+1}]$. With the payoff $X_{t+1} = 1$, the one-year bond's price is
$P^{(1)}_t = \mathbb{E}_t[\SDF{M_{t+1}}] = e^{-y^{(1)}_t}$, so its yield is the risk-free rate,
$\Rf{y^{(1)}_t} = \Rf{r^f_t}$, the rate that
[Chapter 35](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/35-equity-premium-and-risk-free-rate-puzzles/) asks a model to match.

### Forward rates

Can you fix today the rate for a loan that starts in $n - 1$ years and ends in $n$? Yes, with two trades that cost
nothing today:

1. Buy one $n$-year bond, for $P^{(n)}_t$.
2. Pay for it by selling $P^{(n)}_t / P^{(n-1)}_t$ units of the $(n-1)$-year bond.

At $t + n - 1$ you pay $P^{(n)}_t / P^{(n-1)}_t$. At $t + n$ you receive 1. So you have lent from $n - 1$ to $n$ at
the gross rate $P^{(n-1)}_t / P^{(n)}_t$, fixed today. Its log is the **forward rate**:

$$
f^{(n)}_t = p^{(n-1)}_t - p^{(n)}_t, \qquad f^{(1)}_t = y^{(1)}_t,
$$

with $p^{(0)}_t = 0$, since 1 dollar now is worth 1. At the end of September 2026, $y^{(1)}_t = 4.56\%$ and
$y^{(2)}_t = 4.84\%$, so $f^{(2)}_t = 2 \times 4.84\% - 4.56\% = 5.12\%$.

Now add up the first $n$ forward rates. Each log price appears once with a plus and once with a minus, except the
first and the last:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{(1)}_t + \dots + f^{(n)}_t
&= (p^{(0)}_t - p^{(1)}_t) + \dots \\
&\quad + (p^{(n-1)}_t - p^{(n)}_t) \\
&= -p^{(n)}_t = n\,y^{(n)}_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

So the yield is the **average of the forward rates** up to its maturity. Written the other way,
$f^{(n)}_t = n\,y^{(n)}_t - (n-1)\,y^{(n-1)}_t$, which is

$$
f^{(n)}_t = y^{(n)}_t + (n-1)\big(y^{(n)}_t - y^{(n-1)}_t\big).
$$

Where the yield curve rises, the forward curve lies above it, and where it falls, below. In September 2026,
$f^{(10)}_t = 5.84\%$ against $y^{(10)}_t = 5.28\%$, while $f^{(30)}_t = 4.90\%$ against $y^{(30)}_t = 5.66\%$,
because the curve dips between 29 and 30 years.

### Par yields

Real Treasury notes pay coupons. A coupon bond is a portfolio of zero-coupon bonds, one for each payment, and its
price is the sum of their prices (Campbell & Shiller, 1991, working paper, pdf p. 15). Let $d(m) = e^{-m\,y(m)}$
be the price of 1 paid in $m$ years. A bond with maturity $n$ that pays $\mathit{par}^{(n)}_t/k$ at each of $k$
dates a year, and 1 at $n$, is worth 1 when

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{kn} \frac{\mathit{par}^{(n)}_t}{k}\,d(j/k) + d(n) = 1.
$$

Solve for the coupon rate:

$$
\mathit{par}^{(n)}_t = \frac{k\,\big(1 - d(n)\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{kn} d(j/k)}.
$$

That is the **par yield**. With coupons once a year ($k = 1$) it needs only the GSW grid of whole years. The
Treasury pays twice a year ($k = 2$), so the half-year discount factors need $y(m)$ between whole years. We
interpolate it linearly and hold it flat below one year, an approximation the GSW file forces on us.

In September 2026 the 10-year par yield is 5.31% with semi-annual coupons and 5.38% with annual ones, next to the
zero yield of 5.28%. Over all 479 months, the 10-year zero and par yields differ by 0.06 points on average and by
at most 0.29. The par view of the widget adds FRED's constant-maturity yields, which are also par yields with
semi-annual coupons. In the 402 months where both exist, FRED's 10-year yield is on average 0.15 points from ours,
and at most 0.91. The two differ by design: FRED averages the month's days, while GSW takes its last day.

### The holding-period return and its three parts

Buy the $n$-year bond at $t$. A year later it is an $(n-1)$-year bond, and you sell it. The log
**holding-period return** is the log price you sell at minus the log price you paid:

$$
\Ret{r^{(n)}_{t+1}} = p^{(n-1)}_{t+1} - p^{(n)}_t.
$$

The **excess return** subtracts the one-year yield, the return you could have locked in with no risk:
$\Ret{rx^{(n)}_{t+1}} = \Ret{r^{(n)}_{t+1}} - \Rf{y^{(1)}_t}$. The simple return of Chapter 1 is
$e^{\Ret{r^{(n)}_{t+1}}} - 1$.

Where does the return come from? Write both prices as yields and split the change in the yield into two steps:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Ret{r^{(n)}_{t+1}}
&= -(n-1)\,y^{(n-1)}_{t+1} + n\,y^{(n)}_t \\
&= y^{(n)}_t - (n-1)\big(y^{(n-1)}_{t+1} - y^{(n)}_t\big) \\
&= \underbrace{y^{(n)}_t}_{\text{yield}}
\;\underbrace{-\,(n-1)\big(y^{(n-1)}_t - y^{(n)}_t\big)}_{\text{roll-down}} \\
&\quad\;\underbrace{-\,(n-1)\big(y^{(n-1)}_{t+1} - y^{(n-1)}_t\big)}_{\text{shift}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line adds and subtracts $(n-1)\,y^{(n)}_t$. The third adds and subtracts $(n-1)\,y^{(n-1)}_t$. No term
is dropped, so the split is exact.

- The **yield** is what you earn if the bond's yield stays where it is.
- The **roll-down** is what you earn because the bond slides one year down today's curve. On a rising curve,
  $y^{(n-1)}_t < y^{(n)}_t$, so its price rises and the roll-down is positive.
- The **shift** is the gain or loss from the move of the whole curve, at the maturity you sell. It is multiplied
  by $n - 1$, the bond's remaining life, so long bonds feel it most.

Back to the Hook. The 10-year bond bought at the end of 2021 earned its yield, 1.59%, plus a roll-down of 0.41%,
because the 9-year yield was 1.54%. Then the 9-year yield rose to 3.86%, about 2.3 points, nine times over: a
shift of −20.82%. The log return was $1.59 + 0.41 - 20.82 = -18.82\%$, the excess return over the one-year yield
of 0.42% was −19.24%, and the simple return was −17.16%.

### What bonds paid since 1985

Take every month from 1985-11 to 2025-09 that has a curve twelve months later: 455 purchase months. The mean
one-year excess return rises with maturity, and so does its risk:

| Maturity | Mean excess return | Standard error | t-statistic | Standard deviation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 years | 0.46% | 0.24% | 1.93 | 1.31% |
| 5 years | 1.74% | 0.71% | 2.44 | 4.37% |
| 10 years | 3.04% | 1.21% | 2.50 | 8.24% |
| 20 years | 3.87% | 2.01% | 1.92 | 14.86% |
| 30 years | 4.30% | 2.71% | 1.59 | 21.30% |

The returns overlap: two purchases one month apart share eleven months of the same year. So the standard errors
are Hansen–Hodrick with eleven lags, the flat-weight version of the overlap correction in
[Chapter 4](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/04-autocorrelation-and-newey-west/). Even so, 455 overlapping
months hold only about 40 separate years. Only the 5- and 10-year means are more than 1.96 standard errors from
zero.

The sample also had luck in it. Yields fell for most of it, and a falling curve shows up as a positive shift. Of
the 10-year bond's mean log return of 6.54%, the yield gave 4.81%, the roll-down 0.84% and the shift 0.89%. A
sample in which yields end where they start would have no help from the shift.

### The expectations hypothesis

Why does the curve usually slope up? The oldest answer is the **expectations hypothesis**: a long yield is the
average of the short rates expected over the bond's life, plus a constant,

$$
y^{(n)}_t = \frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_t\big[y^{(1)}_{t+j}\big] + c_n .
$$

Campbell & Shiller (1991, working paper, pdf p. 6, eq. 1) state it this way. The constant is a term premium. It
may differ across maturities, but it does not change over time. With $c_n = 0$, a rising curve would mean that
markets expect short rates to rise.

The hypothesis has two other faces. Take its equation for $n$ and for $n - 1$, multiply by $n$ and $n - 1$, and
subtract: the forward rate is the expected short rate in its year, plus a constant. And the expected excess return
of every bond is a constant:

$$
\mathbb{E}_t\big[\Ret{rx^{(n)}_{t+1}}\big] = n\,c_n - (n-1)\,c_{n-1}.
$$

<details>
<summary>The steps from the yield equation to the excess return</summary>

Write the excess return with yields: $\Ret{rx^{(n)}_{t+1}} = n\,y^{(n)}_t - (n-1)\,y^{(n-1)}_{t+1} - y^{(1)}_t$.

The hypothesis for maturity $n$ at $t$, times $n$:

$$
n\,y^{(n)}_t = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_t\big[y^{(1)}_{t+j}\big] + n\,c_n.
$$

The hypothesis for maturity $n - 1$ at $t + 1$, times $n - 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(n-1)\,y^{(n-1)}_{t+1} = {} & \sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{t+1}\big[y^{(1)}_{t+j}\big] \\
& + (n-1)\,c_{n-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Take $\mathbb{E}_t$ of the second line. The expectation at $t$ of an expectation at $t + 1$ is the expectation at
$t$, so the sum becomes $\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_t[y^{(1)}_{t+j}]$. Subtract it from the first line. Every short
rate from $j = 1$ to $n - 1$ cancels, and only $y^{(1)}_t$ is left:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathbb{E}_t\big[n\,y^{(n)}_t - (n-1)\,y^{(n-1)}_{t+1}\big] \\
& \quad = y^{(1)}_t + n\,c_n - (n-1)\,c_{n-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtract $y^{(1)}_t$ from both sides to get the expected excess return.

</details>

So the rising mean excess returns in the table do not contradict the hypothesis: they fit expected excess returns that
are constant over time and grow with maturity. The hypothesis fails only if the expected excess return *moves* over time. That is a claim
about forecasts, and a sample mean cannot test it.

### Hindsight is not a test

Still, it is tempting to compare a long yield with the short rates that actually followed. Replace the
expectations by the realised one-year yields and you get the **perfect-foresight yield**,
$\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} y^{(1)}_{t+j}$. The gap is the ex post term premium,

$$
\mathit{tp}^{(n)}_t = y^{(n)}_t - \frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} y^{(1)}_{t+j}.
$$

For the 10-year yield we can do this for the 306 months from 1985-11 to 2017-09, the last month with ten years of
short rates after it. In November 1985 the 10-year yield was 9.79%, and the 1-year yields of the next ten years
averaged 6.36%. In June 2007 it was 5.07% against 0.97%. On average the gap was 2.26 points, and it was positive in
290 of the 306 months. For 5 years it was 0.92 points, positive in 308 of 385 months.

Read this with care. The years after 1985 saw short rates fall much further than anyone could know at the time. A
long yield above the rates that followed may only mean that the market was surprised, again and again, in the
same direction. One path of history cannot separate a constant term premium from a run of surprises. This is
hindsight, not a test. Testing the hypothesis needs forecasts made at $t$ with what was known at $t$.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- A zero-coupon price, its yield $y^{(n)}_t = -p^{(n)}_t/n$, the forward rates $f^{(n)}_t = p^{(n-1)}_t - p^{(n)}_t$
  and the par yield all come from the same discount factors; the yield is the average of the forwards.
- The one-year log return splits exactly into the yield, the roll-down and the shift; the shift is multiplied by
  the remaining life, which is why the 10-year bond lost 17.16% from the end of 2021.
- Since 1985 the curve sloped up in 87.1% of months, and the mean excess return rose from 0.46% for 2 years to
  4.30% for 30, with wide standard errors and help from falling yields.
- The expectations hypothesis says a long yield is the average expected short rate plus a constant, so expected
  excess returns never change; comparing yields with the short rates that followed is hindsight, not a test.

returns the way the hypothesis says.
