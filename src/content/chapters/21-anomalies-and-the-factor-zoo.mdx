## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> 165 discoveries, and then what?

Size, value and momentum, the subjects of Chapter 19, *Size and value*, and Chapter 20, *Momentum*, are three
variables that predict stock returns. They are not alone. Academic journals have published hundreds of such variables. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016,
p. 8) catalogue 316 factors. Hou, Xue & Zhang (2020, p. 2020) rebuild 452 anomalies. Cochrane (2011, p. 1047) calls
the result a "zoo".

Our data come from the Open Source Asset Pricing project (Chen and Zimmermann 2022). It rebuilds the long–short
portfolio of each published predictor from the paper's own recipe. We use the 165 predictors that it rates as
clearly significant in the original paper. They were published from 1973 to 2016. In each paper's own sample, 158
of the 165 have a $t$-statistic above 1.96. In the months after that sample, only 66 do.

Take one famous case: the past year's return, the momentum predictor of Jegadeesh & Titman (1993). In the
paper's sample, from 1964 to 1989, the long–short portfolio earned 16.09% a year, with a $t$-statistic of 4.48 over 312 months. In the
420 months after 1989, it earned 9.21% a year, with a $t$-statistic of 1.82. So which of the 165 discoveries are
real? And how many factors do you actually need? This chapter gives two tools for those questions: an honest count,
and a spanning test.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Count the points above the line

Start with a definition. A **predictor** is a variable you know today, like book-to-market or past return, that
sorts stocks by their future average return. Buy the stocks at the top of the sort and sell those at the bottom.
The result is a **long–short portfolio**. It costs nothing to set up, so its average return is a reward for
something. A predictor is an **anomaly** when that reward is not explained by a benchmark model, such as the
CAPM of [Chapter 7](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/07-mean-variance-and-the-capm/). An anomaly is always an
anomaly *relative to a model*.

A simplification is in play here. The widget tests the raw mean return of each long–short portfolio, not its
alpha against a model. The two differ by the part of the mean that the model's factors explain.

Each point below is one predictor. It sits at the year its paper was published, at the height of its
$t$-statistic. The solid line is a significance line that you can drag. A point above it is filled. A point below it
is hollow. The dashed line marks $t = 3$.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> drag the line from 1.96 up to 3.00. In the original samples, the count
above it falls from 158 to 107. Now switch the window to "After the sample". At 3.00, 31 of the 165 remain. Move the
line back to 1.96: 66 remain.</p>

Three things can shrink a discovery.

1. **Luck.** Many researchers try many variables. A few will pass any line by chance, and those are the ones that
   get published. The project behind our data adds a second filter: it keeps only predictors that worked in the
   paper. So the in-sample panel is a set of survivors by design.
2. **The portfolio.** Many published portfolios weigh every stock equally, so tiny stocks count as much as giants.
   Hou, Xue & Zhang (2020, p. 2020) call stocks below the 20th size percentile of NYSE stocks *microcaps*. In their
   data, microcaps are 60.7% of the stocks but only 3.2% of the market value. When they weigh stocks by value and
   set the sort's cutoffs with NYSE stocks only, 65% of their 452 anomalies fall below 1.96. With all stocks in
   the cutoffs and equal weights, 43.1% fail.
3. **Time.** After a paper appears, traders can act on it. And a pattern that was luck does not repeat. Either way,
   the reward is smaller after the sample.

Our data cannot show the second point directly. The value-weighted and no-microcap versions of these portfolios
exist, but this site does not include them (see `docs/DATA.md`). So the widget's "Portfolio construction" control
has one option, the original papers' recipe. The readout rows that compare constructions therefore compare that
recipe with itself.

<Callout type="key">A predictor is an anomaly only relative to a model. Published $t$-statistics overstate the
evidence, because the predictors that we see are the ones that passed.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Many $t$-statistics, and one regression

### The $t$-statistic of one predictor

Write $\Ret{R_{t}}$ for the return of a long–short portfolio in month $t$, for $t = 1, \dots, T$. Its sample mean is
$\bar R = \tfrac1T \sum_t R_t$. Assume that the months are independent, with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$.
Then four steps give the test.

1. The variance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of their variances: $\operatorname{Var}(\sum_t R_t) = T\sigma^2$.
2. Divide by $T^2$: $\operatorname{Var}(\bar R) = \sigma^2/T$, so the standard error of $\bar R$ is $\sigma/\sqrt T$.
3. Replace $\sigma$ by the sample standard deviation $s$, as in
   [Chapter 3](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/03-regression-and-standard-errors/).
4. Under the null hypothesis $\mu = 0$, the ratio

$$
t = \frac{\bar R}{s/\sqrt T}
$$

is close to a standard normal when $T$ is large. You reject at the 5% level when $|t| > 1.96$.

### Many tests at once

A single test at 1.96 is wrong 5% of the time when $\mu = 0$. Now suppose that none of the 316 factors of Harvey,
Liu & Zhu had a true reward. You would still expect $0.05 \times 316 = 15.8$ of them to pass by luck. And if only
the passes get published, every published $t$-statistic is above 1.96, true reward or not. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016,
p. 23) report that most of the published $t$-statistics they collect are above 1.96.

A higher line makes luck rarer. Under the null, $|t| > 3$ has a probability of 0.27%, so you would expect only 0.85
of the 316 to pass. This is why Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016, p. 5) argue that a new factor needs $t > 3$.

But a higher line is a blunt fix. It also throws away real predictors that have short samples. A predictor whose
true annual Sharpe ratio is 0.5 has an expected $t$-statistic of $0.5 \times \sqrt{T/12}$. With 240 months, that is
2.24. It passes 1.96 with a probability of 61%, but it passes 3 with a probability of only 22%. A higher line trades
false discoveries for missed ones. It does not tell you which predictors are real.

### The spanning regression

The second question is different. Suppose that a predictor is real. Do you need a new factor for it, or do the
factors you already have explain it? Cochrane (2011, p. 1060) asks this of the zoo: which variables carry
independent information about average returns, and which are subsumed by others?

Take a candidate factor $\Factor{f_{\text{new},t}}$ that is an excess return, and the $K$ factors $\Factor{f_t}$ of a
model. Regress one on the others over time:

$$
\Factor{f_{\text{new},t}} = \Err{\alpha} + \beta'\Factor{f_t} + \Err{\varepsilon_t}.
$$

This is the time-series regression of
[Chapter 16](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/16-time-series-tests-and-grs/), with the new factor as the one test
asset. The new factor adds to the model only if $\alpha \ne 0$. Here is why, in three steps.

1. Hedge the new factor: buy it and sell $\beta$ of each old factor. The hedged return is
   $f_{\text{new},t} - \beta'f_t = \alpha + \varepsilon_t$. Its mean is $\alpha$, and OLS makes $\varepsilon_t$
   uncorrelated with $f_t$ in sample.
2. Chapter 16 showed that adding test assets to the factors raises the highest squared Sharpe ratio by
   $\hat\alpha'\hat\Sigma_\varepsilon^{-1}\hat\alpha$. With one new asset, this is a single number:

$$
SR^2(f, f_{\text{new}}) = SR^2(f) + \left(\frac{\Err{\hat\alpha}}{\hat\sigma_\varepsilon}\right)^2.
$$

3. So the best Sharpe ratio rises if and only if $\hat\alpha \ne 0$. When $\alpha = 0$, the new factor is a mix of
   the old ones plus noise that earns nothing. The old factors already reach the tangency portfolio, and the new one
   is **spanned**.

The fit of the regression does not decide this. A factor with a high $R^2$ and a nonzero $\alpha$ adds to the
model. A factor with a low $R^2$ and $\alpha = 0$ does not: it is noise that the model does not need. You test
$\alpha = 0$ with its $t$-statistic. With several new factors at once, you use the GRS test of Chapter 16.

### What the spanning test can and cannot say

HML shows the main limit. A factor can be spanned in one sample and not in another. Over its first 366 months, HML
earned more than the other five could explain. Over the next 392, it did not. The full-sample verdict mixes the two.
Before you drop a factor from a model, ask whether the verdict holds in each half of the data.

Mom shows the opposite case. Little of its monthly return comes from the other five, and its reward is far from
zero. You cannot build momentum from the other five.

SMB is the borderline case. Its $t$-statistic of 1.80 is below 1.96, so at 5% it is spanned. But "not rejected"
is not "proved to be zero". The 95% confidence interval for its alpha runs from −0.21% to 4.76% a year.

Two more limits. The answer depends on the base: a factor spanned by these five may not be spanned by a different
set. And the $t$-statistics here assume independent months. The readout also shows White's version, which allows
the variance to change over time. For these factors the two are close.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- A predictor sorts stocks into a long–short portfolio. It is an anomaly when a benchmark model cannot explain the
  portfolio's average return.
- Of 165 predictors with clear results in their papers, 158 pass $|t| > 1.96$ in the paper's sample and 66 after it.
  Selection, small stocks and publication all shrink the published evidence.
- A higher line such as $t > 3$ cuts false discoveries, but it also cuts real predictors with short samples.
- The spanning regression $f_{\text{new},t} = \alpha + \beta'f_t + \varepsilon_t$ asks whether a factor raises the
  highest Sharpe ratio. Only $\alpha \ne 0$ adds to the model; $R^2$ does not decide it.
- A verdict can change with the sample: HML is not spanned in 1963–1993, but it is spanned after 1993.
