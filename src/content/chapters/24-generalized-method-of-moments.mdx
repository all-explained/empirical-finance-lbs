## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span> Four facts, two unknowns

Here is a sample of 600 draws $x_1, \dots, x_{600}$ from a seeded simulation. It is not a return. It is a toy
series whose truth we know: the draws are independent and normal, with mean $\mu = 0.5$ and variance
$\sigma^2 = 1$. Pretend you do not know those two numbers, and estimate them.

The obvious answer uses two facts about a normal variable. Its mean is $\mu$, and its variance is $\sigma^2$.
Match them to the sample: the sample mean is 0.496 and the sample variance is 1.025. But a normal variable obeys
more facts than these two. Its third central moment, $\mathbb{E}[(x_t - \mu)^3]$, is zero. Its fourth,
$\mathbb{E}[(x_t - \mu)^4]$, is $3\sigma^4$. In this sample the fourth central moment is 3.547. The variance that
makes the fourth-moment fact hold exactly is $\sqrt{3.547/3} = 1.087$, not 1.025.

So you have four equations and two unknowns, and the equations disagree. Which two do you use? Can you use all
four at once? And once you have an answer, how precise is it? Asset pricing has the same problem at a larger
scale: one pricing equation for each test asset, and only a few parameters. This chapter
builds the method that turns any such set of equations into an estimate with a standard error: the
**generalized method of moments**, or GMM.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span> Get every equation as close to zero as you can

Write each fact as an average that should be zero at the true parameters. For the mean, the average of
$x_t - \mu$ should be zero. For the variance, the average of $(x_t - \mu)^2 - \sigma^2$. For the third and fourth
moments, the averages of $(x_t - \mu)^3$ and $(x_t - \mu)^4 - 3\sigma^4$. Each of these is a **moment
condition**. In the sample, try any pair $(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and compute the four averages. At a good guess they
are all small. At a bad guess some are large.

GMM turns the four averages into one number, a weighted sum of their squares, and picks the pair that makes it
smallest. The widget draws that number over a grid of guesses. Stronger shading means a smaller value. The lines
are contours: each joins the guesses with the same value, like the height lines on a map. The dot labelled
*minimum* is the lowest point, the estimate $\hat\theta$. The dot labelled $\theta_0$ is the truth.

<GMMObjective client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> start with 2 moments. The lowest point sits at the sample mean
and variance, 0.496 and 1.025, and the readout shows that the objective there is 0: two equations in two
unknowns can both hold exactly. Now switch to 4 moments, keeping one-step weighting. The dot moves to 0.501 and
1.086, and the objective at the minimum is no longer zero. Then switch to two-step weighting. The dot moves to
0.488 and 1.005.</p>

Three things happen. First, with 2 moments the weighting does not matter: switch it, and the dot stays where it
is. Second, with 4 moments no guess makes every equation hold, so the estimate is a compromise, and the weights
decide who gives way. Third, with equal weights the compromise is poor. The fourth-moment equation is measured in
much larger units than the others. A small change in $\sigma^2$ moves $3\sigma^4$ about six times as much as it
moves $\sigma^2$. So equal weights let that one noisy equation pull $\hat\sigma^2$ almost to the 1.087 it wants.
The standard errors in the readout say the same thing. With 2 moments they are 0.041 for $\hat\mu$ and 0.064
for $\hat\sigma^2$. With 4 moments and equal weights they grow to 0.060 and 0.087. Extra equations made the
estimate *worse*. With two-step weights they are 0.041 and 0.061: no worse for $\hat\mu$ than with 2 moments,
and a little better for $\hat\sigma^2$.

Nagel (2013, p. 21) makes the same point about asset pricing tests. A GMM estimator with an identity weighting
matrix may give almost no weight to one moment condition, while the efficient weighting matrix makes the model
fit it.

<Callout type="key">GMM picks the parameters that bring a set of sample averages closest to zero. When there are
as many equations as parameters, they all hold exactly and the weights do not matter. When there are more, the
weighting matrix decides the compromise.</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span> Moment conditions, the estimator and its distribution

### Moment conditions and their sample analogue

Let $x_t$ be the data at date $t$: here one draw, in an asset pricing test a vector of returns and factors. In
this Part $x_t$ means the data inside $g(x_t, \theta)$, not a predictor as in Part II. Let $\theta$ be the
vector of $\dim\theta$ unknown parameters, with true value $\theta_0$. A model gives $\dim g$ **moment
conditions**, written as a vector function $g(x_t, \theta)$ with

$$
\mathbb{E}[g(x_t, \theta_0)] = 0.
$$

The sample replaces the expectation by an average over $t = 1, \dots, T$:

$$
\Data{g_T(\theta)} = \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^{T} g(x_t, \theta).
$$

For the toy model, $\theta = (\mu, \sigma^2)'$, and the four moment conditions of the widget stack into

$$
g(x_t, \theta) = \begin{pmatrix} x_t - \mu \\ (x_t - \mu)^2 - \sigma^2 \\ (x_t - \mu)^3 \\ (x_t - \mu)^4 - 3\sigma^4 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

With 2 moments, keep the first two rows. The model is **just identified** when $\dim g = \dim\theta$, and
**over-identified** when $\dim g > \dim\theta$.

### The estimator

Choose a positive definite $\dim g \times \dim g$ **weighting matrix** $W$. The GMM estimate minimises the
weighted sum of squares of the sample moments:

$$
\Model{\hat\theta} = \arg\min_\theta \; \Data{g_T(\theta)}'\,W\,\Data{g_T(\theta)}.
$$

With $W = I$ this is the plain sum of squares of the averages. Write $D_T(\theta) = \partial g_T/\partial\theta'$
for the $\dim g \times \dim\theta$ matrix of derivatives. The gradient of $g_T'Wg_T$ is $2D_T'Wg_T$, so the
minimum solves the **first-order condition**

$$
D_T(\hat\theta)'\,W\,g_T(\hat\theta) = 0.
$$

This is $\dim\theta$ equations. GMM sets $\dim\theta$ linear combinations of the $\dim g$ sample moments to zero,
and $D_T'W$ chooses the combinations.

### Why the estimator is consistent

Consistent means that $\hat\theta$ gets as close to $\theta_0$ as you like when $T$ is large enough. Two
assumptions give it.

1. **The averages settle down.** For each $\theta$, the law of large numbers says that $g_T(\theta)$ goes to
   $g_\infty(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[g(x_t, \theta)]$. We assume that $g_\infty$ is continuous and that the
   convergence is uniform in $\theta$, over a closed and bounded set of parameters that contains $\theta_0$.
   Then the sample objective goes to $Q(\theta) = g_\infty(\theta)'Wg_\infty(\theta)$, also uniformly.
2. **The model is identified.** $g_\infty(\theta) = 0$ only at $\theta = \theta_0$.

Now follow the objective. $Q(\theta) \ge 0$ for every $\theta$, because $W$ is positive definite. $Q(\theta_0) =
0$, because the moment conditions hold. By assumption 2, $Q(\theta) > 0$ at every other $\theta$. So the limit
of the objective has a unique lowest point, at $\theta_0$. The sample objective is uniformly close to that limit,
so its lowest point cannot stay far from $\theta_0$: away from $\theta_0$, the limit is above some positive
number, and for large $T$ the sample objective is too. Hence $\hat\theta$ goes to $\theta_0$. The weighting
matrix plays no role in this argument. Every positive definite $W$ gives a consistent estimate. The choice of $W$
changes only the precision.

### The sandwich covariance

Four steps give the distribution of $\hat\theta$ in large samples.

**Step 1: expand the moments.** By the mean value theorem, applied row by row,
$g_T(\hat\theta) = g_T(\theta_0) + D_T(\bar\theta)(\hat\theta - \theta_0)$, where each row of $D_T(\bar\theta)$
is evaluated at a point between $\hat\theta$ and $\theta_0$.

**Step 2: put the expansion into the first-order condition.**
$D_T(\hat\theta)'W g_T(\theta_0) + D_T(\hat\theta)'W D_T(\bar\theta)(\hat\theta - \theta_0) = 0$. Solve for
the estimation error and multiply by $\sqrt T$:

$$
\sqrt T(\hat\theta - \theta_0) = -\big[D_T(\hat\theta)'W D_T(\bar\theta)\big]^{-1} D_T(\hat\theta)'W\,\sqrt T g_T(\theta_0).
$$

**Step 3: take the limits of the pieces.** $\hat\theta$ and $\bar\theta$ go to $\theta_0$, so both derivative
matrices go to $D = \mathbb{E}[\partial g(x_t, \theta_0)/\partial\theta']$. The vector $\sqrt T g_T(\theta_0)$ is
$1/\sqrt T$ times a sum of $T$ terms with mean zero. Its variance is $\tfrac1T\operatorname{Var}(\sum_t g_t)$,
with $g_t = g(x_t, \theta_0)$. [Chapter 4](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/04-autocorrelation-and-newey-west/)
showed that the variance of such a sum is $T S$, with

$$
S = \sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}[g_t\,g_{t-j}'] = \Gamma_0 + \sum_{j \ge 1}\big(\Gamma_j + \Gamma_j'\big).
$$

$S$ is the **long-run covariance** of the moments. A central limit theorem for dependent series then gives
$\sqrt T g_T(\theta_0) \to N(0, S)$.

**Step 4: pass the normal through the linear map.** To first order, $\sqrt T(\hat\theta - \theta_0)$ is the
fixed matrix $A = -(D'WD)^{-1}D'W$ times an asymptotically normal vector. This is the delta method: a smooth
function of an asymptotically normal vector is asymptotically normal, with covariance from its first
derivative. A linear map $A$ turns covariance $S$ into $ASA'$. Since $W$ and $D'WD$ are symmetric,

$$
\sqrt T(\Model{\hat\theta} - \theta_0) \to N\Big(0,\; (D'WD)^{-1}D'WSWD\,(D'WD)^{-1}\Big).
$$

This is a sandwich, the same shape as the OLS formula of
[Chapter 3](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/03-regression-and-standard-errors/): the bread $(D'WD)^{-1}D'W$ on
the outside, the noise $S$ in the middle. To use it, replace $D$ by $D_T(\hat\theta)$ and $S$ by its Newey–West
estimate $\hat S$ with $L$ lags, built from the moments $g(x_t, \hat\theta)$ exactly as in Chapter 4. Divide by
$T$ to get the covariance of $\hat\theta$ itself. In the toy model the draws are independent, so $L = 0$.

The formula holds for any $W$. One choice simplifies it: with $W = S^{-1}$, the middle $WSW$ becomes $S^{-1}$,
and the sandwich collapses to $(D'S^{-1}D)^{-1}$. The widget's two-step method uses that choice. It first
estimates with $W = I$, then computes $\hat S$ at that estimate, and estimates again with $W = \hat S^{-1}$.
This chapter uses that recipe without asking whether it is the best one.

### Just identified: the weights drop out

When $\dim g = \dim\theta$, $D$ is square. Assume it is invertible. Then $D'W$ is invertible too, and the
first-order condition $D_T'W g_T(\hat\theta) = 0$ can only hold if

$$
g_T(\hat\theta) = 0.
$$

Every moment condition holds exactly in the sample, whatever $W$ is. The covariance loses $W$ as well. With
square invertible matrices, $(D'WD)^{-1} = D^{-1}W^{-1}(D')^{-1}$, so the bread is

$$
(D'WD)^{-1}D'W = D^{-1}W^{-1}(D')^{-1}D'W = D^{-1},
$$

and the sandwich becomes $D^{-1}S(D^{-1})'$. Neither the estimate nor its covariance depends on $W$. That is why
the dot did not move when you switched the weighting with 2 moments.

In the toy model with 2 moments, $g_T(\hat\theta) = 0$ gives $\hat\mu = \bar x$ and
$\hat\sigma^2 = \tfrac1T\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2$, the sample mean and the variance with divisor $T$. The
derivative matrix at $\hat\theta$ is $D = -I$: the first row of $\partial g/\partial\theta'$ is $(-1, 0)$, and
the second is $(-2(x_t - \mu), -1)$, whose first entry averages to zero at $\hat\mu = \bar x$. So the covariance
of $\hat\theta$ is $\hat S/T$, and the standard errors are

$$
\operatorname{se}(\hat\mu) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat\sigma^2}{T}}, \qquad
\operatorname{se}(\hat\sigma^2) = \sqrt{\frac{\tfrac1T\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^4 - \hat\sigma^4}{T}}.
$$

The first is the familiar $0.041$. The second is 0.064, from the sample fourth moment. The textbook formula for
normal data, $\sqrt{2\hat\sigma^4/T}$, gives 0.059. GMM did not use normality to get its standard error, only the
two moment conditions.

### OLS, IV and maximum likelihood are GMM

**OLS.** In the regression $y_t = x_t'\beta + \Err{\varepsilon_t}$ of Chapter 3, the data at $t$ are $y_t$ and
the regressors $x_t$. The assumption that the error is uncorrelated with the regressors is a moment condition:

$$
g(x_t, \beta) = x_t\,(y_t - x_t'\beta), \qquad g_T(\beta) = \frac1T\big(X'y - X'X\beta\big).
$$

It is just identified, so $g_T(\hat\beta) = 0$. That is $X'X\hat\beta = X'y$, the OLS normal equations, so
$\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$. The derivative is $D = -X'X/T$. The just-identified covariance $D^{-1}S(D^{-1})'/T$
is

$$
\frac1T\Big(\frac{X'X}{T}\Big)^{-1} S \Big(\frac{X'X}{T}\Big)^{-1} = (X'X)^{-1}\,T S\,(X'X)^{-1},
$$

which is Chapter 4's formula. Here $g_t = x_t\varepsilon_t$ is the $z_t$ of that chapter. With $\hat S$ from
$L = 0$ lags you get White's standard errors, and with $L > 0$ the Newey–West ones.

**IV.** Suppose the error may be correlated with $x_t$, but a vector $z_t$ of **instruments** is not. Part V used
$z_t$ for a state variable. Here it names the instruments, which work in a similar way: multiplying the error by
$z_t$ creates more moment conditions, as a
[managed portfolio](/empirical-finance-lbs/chapters/22-conditional-factor-models/) $z_tR^e_{i,t+1}$ creates more test
assets. With $\dim z \ge \dim\beta$,

$$
g(x_t, \beta) = z_t\,(y_t - x_t'\beta), \qquad g_T(\beta) = \frac1T\big(Z'y - Z'X\beta\big).
$$

Now $D = -Z'X/T$, and the first-order condition $D'Wg_T(\hat\beta) = 0$ reads
$X'ZWZ'y = X'ZWZ'X\hat\beta$. So

$$
\hat\beta = (X'ZWZ'X)^{-1}X'ZWZ'y.
$$

With as many instruments as regressors, $Z'X$ is square, and the formula reduces to $\hat\beta = (Z'X)^{-1}Z'y$,
for any $W$. With more instruments, take $W = (Z'Z/T)^{-1}$. Write $P_Z = Z(Z'Z)^{-1}Z'$, which is symmetric and
satisfies $P_ZP_Z = P_Z$. The scalars $T$ cancel, and

$$
\hat\beta = (X'P_ZX)^{-1}X'P_Zy = (\hat X'\hat X)^{-1}\hat X'y, \qquad \hat X = P_ZX.
$$

$\hat X$ holds the fitted values of a regression of each regressor on the instruments. So this is two-stage least
squares: regress $X$ on $Z$, then regress $y$ on the fitted values.

**Maximum likelihood.** If $x_t$ has density $p(x_t; \theta)$, maximum likelihood sets the average **score**, the
derivative of $\log p(x_t; \theta)$, to zero. That is GMM with the score as the moment function, just identified.
For the normal toy model, $\log p = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - (x_t - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$, and the score is

$$
\begin{pmatrix} (x_t - \mu)/\sigma^2 \\ \big((x_t - \mu)^2 - \sigma^2\big)/(2\sigma^4) \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Each row is one of our two moments divided by a positive number, so its average is zero at the same point. The
maximum likelihood estimate is the 2-moment GMM estimate: 0.496 and 1.025 again. For a correctly specified
likelihood, the GMM covariance $D^{-1}S(D^{-1})'$ of the score is the inverse of the **Fisher information**
$-D$, the familiar ML covariance. That needs one identity, derived below.

<details>
<summary>Why $S = -D$ for the score of a correct likelihood</summary>

1. Write $s(x; \theta) = \partial \log p(x; \theta)/\partial\theta$, so $\partial p/\partial\theta = p\,s$.
2. A density integrates to one for every $\theta$: $\int p(x; \theta)\,dx = 1$.
3. Differentiate in $\theta$: $\int p\,s\,dx = 0$, which says $\mathbb{E}[s] = 0$. The score is a valid moment
   condition.
4. Differentiate $\int p\,s'\,dx = 0$ once more: $\int p\,s\,s'\,dx + \int p\,\partial s/\partial\theta'\,dx = 0$.
5. So $\mathbb{E}[ss'] = -\mathbb{E}[\partial s/\partial\theta']$. With independent draws, $S = \mathbb{E}[ss']$
   and $D = \mathbb{E}[\partial s/\partial\theta']$, so $S = -D$.
6. Then $D^{-1}S(D^{-1})' = D^{-1}(-D)(D^{-1})'$. That is $-(D^{-1})'$, which equals $(-D)^{-1}$ because $D$ is
   symmetric here.

</details>

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> keep 2 moments and press <em>New sample</em> a few times. For
every sample, switch the weighting: the dot never moves, and the objective at the minimum is always 0. With 2
moments the contours change shape when you switch, but their centre does not.</p>

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this too:</strong> switch to 4 moments and compare the two weightings on several
samples. With one-step weights the valley is long and flat along $\mu$: the fourth-moment equation, in its large
units, makes the objective rise steeply when $\sigma^2$ moves. With two-step weights the valley is rounder. Both
estimates scatter around the true $\theta_0$, because both are consistent.</p>

Why does the shape change? Near the minimum, put $g_T(\theta) \approx g_T(\hat\theta) + D(\theta - \hat\theta)$
into the objective. The cross term is zero by the first-order condition, so the objective lies above its lowest
value by about $(\theta - \hat\theta)'D'WD(\theta - \hat\theta)$. With $W = \hat S^{-1}$, the matrix
$D'WD$ is the inverse of $T$ times the covariance of $\hat\theta$. So the two-step contours have the shape of a
confidence region: they stretch most in the direction where the estimate is least precise. The one-step contours
have no such reading.

Two limits. The standard errors rest on large-sample theory, not on the exact distribution of $\hat\theta$ in
600 draws. And in the toy model all four moment conditions are true. If one were false, GMM would still return an
estimate; only the objective at its minimum would show the problem.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap & next up</span> What to remember

- GMM starts from moment conditions $\mathbb{E}[g(x_t, \theta_0)] = 0$ and minimises $g_T(\theta)'Wg_T(\theta)$
  over the parameters.
- Every positive definite $W$ gives a consistent estimate, as long as the moment conditions pin down $\theta_0$.
- $\sqrt T(\hat\theta - \theta_0)$ is asymptotically normal, with the sandwich covariance
  $(D'WD)^{-1}D'WSWD\,(D'WD)^{-1}$. $S$ is the long-run covariance of the moments, estimated by Newey–West as in
  Chapter 4.
- When the model is just identified, $g_T(\hat\theta) = 0$ and $W$ drops out of the estimate and its covariance.
- OLS, IV, two-stage least squares and maximum likelihood are all GMM with particular moments and weights.
